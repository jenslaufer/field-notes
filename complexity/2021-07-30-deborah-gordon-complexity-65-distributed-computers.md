# Deborah Gordon on COMPLEXITY 65

> **The one sentence that carries the episode:** *"when a system has to spend more than
> it's getting in, it's expensive to run the system. And it seems that systems that work
> like that have the default set not to do anything unless it's really worthwhile."*
> [26:25–26:40]

The host reads two of her papers to her and asks for analogies to markets, careers and
society. Gordon answers narrowly and refuses most of the analogies. This is the
earliest of the three Gordon notes here (2021) and the source of the framework the
[2023](2023-11-02-deborah-gordon-ant-colonies.md) and
[2026](2026-08-05-deborah-gordon-autm-collective-behavior.md) interviews only touch.

## 1. Against "division of labor"

- The view when she started in the 80s: each ant has a genetically fixed task, so a colony with more foragers
  forages more. That *"locates all the causes of the ants' behavior inside the ant."*
  [07:29]
- Two objections. Ants switch tasks. And even knowing an ant is a forager today *"still
  doesn't tell you how much foraging that ant is going to do, or when"* [08:29–08:35].
- Task allocation shifts with conditions: with extra food, ants from other tasks become
  foragers. *"They're triggered by the availability of food"* [12:25], not by something
  inside them.

**Why specialization fits people and not ants.** *"The value of division of labor in
people is dependent on learning."* [16:06] Janitors get good at cleaning, chefs at
cooking — Adam Smith's boots and candles. *"That doesn't apply to ants or cells or most
other collective systems."* [16:52] And: *"we don't really have any evidence that an ant
gets better at anything by doing it."* [17:15] Where nobody improves by repetition,
rotating the work costs nothing.

## 2. No identity

Ants react to the rate of encounters, not to who they meet: *"an ant doesn't care if it
meets ant number 20 or ant number 566"* — glass beads coated with the right smell get the
same response [14:35–14:48]. For humans, *"identity is a really crucial component of
sanity"* [15:05]. In the evolutionary sense the colony is the individual: *"ants don't
make more ants colonies make more colonies."* [19:19]

## 3. The framework: three constraints, three answers

From *The Ecology of Collective Behavior*. Same environment, same algorithm — in ants,
cells, or anything else that runs without central control.

| Constraint | Environment | Algorithm |
|---|---|---|
| **Patchiness in space** | Scattered (desert seeds, spread by wind and flood) | No recruitment — *"there's not likely to be any more seeds in the same place"* [25:00] |
| | Patchy (your picnic, *"a windfall of great food"* [25:14]) | Fast recruitment, *"so that they can be the ones to get the picnic instead of the ants next door"* [25:26] |
| **Patchiness in time** | Rare, all-at-once events (fire) | *"patchy resources or patchy events require a rapid mobilization, whereas scattered ones don't."* [26:07] |
| **Operating costs** | Expensive (harvester ants spend water to get water) | Positive feedback, default stop: *"the system is set not to go unless it's really great out there"* [27:23]; *"The default is to stop."* [27:30] |
| | Cheap (turtle ants in the humid canopy) | Negative feedback: *"just keep going unless something really bad happens to stop it."* [27:48] |
| **Risk of rupture** | Frequent-small vs rare-catastrophic | Separate processes for each; modularity so a hit cluster does not take down the rest [33:00–33:48] |

On risk: *"how bad is the bad thing that might happen? And the other is how likely is it
to happen?"* [29:56–30:02] Her example is her own region — a big earthquake is coming,
*"but it's really easy to not do anything about it because it seems so rare"* [30:37],
while traffic lights handle the small, frequent risk. Modularity is the answer to
rupture, not specialization: the cell-tower network, where one tower going down *"doesn't
bring down the whole system"* [33:23].

Side line when the host calls stability a luxury: *"flexibility takes work, change takes
work."* [29:40]

## 4. Dopamine and colony temperament

- Give ants dopamine *"and they are more likely to go out even when it's dry."* [35:07]
- Colonies differ. Some cut foraging when it is dry; *"Other colonies are more, you could
  say, entrepreneurial"* — *"they just keep going out no matter what."* [35:39–35:49]
- The cautious colonies have a reason: their workers *"actually lose water faster"*
  [35:54] — and they respond more to dopamine.
- The reward is collective, not individual: ants carry food home and share it, so *"the
  individual reward response has been shaped into a kind of collective reward
  response."* [37:08]

## 5. Zero transaction costs remove the brake

The host brings J. Doyne Farmer's work on herding in markets and Robinhood's zero fees.
Gordon's reading: forces that *"would dampen the positive feedback that's leading people
to follow each other"* are being removed [38:51], so the system can *"go to the brink of
chaos or to keep going maybe past when it should."* [39:00] Good for some, *"not a good
thing for the individual investors"* [39:06]. She also corrects the host: low operating
costs go with high competition in the tropics, but *"I don't think that the low operating
costs cause the competition."* [40:19]

## 6. Cancer as collective behavior

Cancer is *"a very diverse set of collective behaviors"* [41:32]; cancer cells evolve from
healthy ones under local conditions. The patchiness rule carries over: cancers that
metastasize only to particular tissues are hunting patchy resources and should show
recruitment-like processes [43:00–43:29]. She took part in a National Cancer Institute
workshop on emergent cell–cell interactions; *"it doesn't work just to try to kill as
many cancer cells as you possibly can."* [44:55]

## 7. No sacrifice for the collective

- *"there never was an ant by itself."* [50:14] Ants evolved from wasps, which already
  had many forms of working together.
- She rejects the widespread premise that a solitary individual gave something up to join
  the group — the wild dog that could have eaten a whole gazelle alone. *"We also could
  never have made it if we were each living alone."* [51:24]
- Identity itself is relational: *"if you were really alone, you couldn't have an
  identity because there wouldn't be anything to have an identity relative to."* [52:58]
- On evolutionary transitions toward more connection: *"I really think is a kind of a
  fantasy. I don't think anything was ever working independently."* [49:42–49:45]

## What the episode does not give

No numbers at all — no colony counts, no effect sizes for the dopamine work (*"there's a
lot to learn about how that works"* [36:46]). About a third of the airtime is the host's
analogies (gold rush, Neo downloading kung fu, Cordyceps, Dogecoin); Gordon endorses
none of them beyond the transaction-cost point.

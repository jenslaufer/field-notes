# Geoffrey West on COMPLEXITY

> **The one sentence that carries all four episodes:** *"the very mechanisms of
> social networks that give rise to our huge success — in material terms, anyway —
> is the very source of our weakness."* Everything else is that claim, made
> quantitative. Cities are superlinear engines: double the population and you get
> ~15 % **more** wealth, ideas and innovation per capita while needing ~15 % **less**
> infrastructure per capita. The same positive feedback delivers more disease, more
> crime, more corruption, on the same exponent. Feed that superlinearity into the
> growth equations and you get **open-ended growth** — and, buried inside it, a
> finite-time singularity that collapses unless you reinvent yourself, faster on every
> cycle. And the sting in the tail, which West only published later: **that per-capita
> dividend accrues to the top decile. The bottom decile scales close to linearly —
> they get nothing out of the city.**

Four appearances, eighteen months apart at the tightest and four years at the widest.
E35/E36 are one conversation split in two and are the core. E74 is West and David
Krakauer marking what complexity science got right and wrong about COVID — and it
contains West's own correction to the cheerful version of urban scaling. S2:E1 is a
short, personal, beautifully produced piece recorded on a deck in South Africa with
lions audible in the background.

## S1:E35 — Scaling laws & social networks (17 June 2020, 49:30)

### How a particle physicist ended up measuring elephants

Two seeds. He was good at mathematics as a kid, and he noticed early that scientific
answers carried "a wealth of facts and knowledge and prediction and understanding"
that the folklore and Bible explanations of his religious upbringing did not. The
operational seed came at Stanford, teaching physics to non-physics majors — mostly
pre-meds. To make the course worth their time he invented examples from physiology
and ecosystems, and stumbled on "the original scaling law of all scaling laws," the
scaling of metabolic rate. Then he spent 25–30 years at the opposite end of the
spectrum: elementary particles, dark matter, string theory. The seed propagated
anyway.

### Metabolism first, genome second

The framing move that makes everything else work:

- Biology displaced metabolism with the genome — molecular reductionism plus
  information exchange. West thinks that is a mistake of ordering, not of content:
  **"nothing happens without energy and energy is fundamental. You can't have
  information exchange, can't have genes doing what they're doing unless you have
  energy first."** The evidence, he says, points to metabolism *constraining* what
  genomic networks can do.
- The same claim, transposed: **the metabolism of a city — how energy and resources
  flow through it — is a huge constraint on what its social networks can accomplish.**
  No information exchange, wealth creation or business without energy and resources.
- Two ways to see metabolism. Reductionist: chemistry inside cells, ATP as the
  currency, up to ~a million little engines in one active cell, ~100 trillion cells in
  a body. Holistic: you are not conscious of any of that, yet you use energy
  constantly — so something at the system level translates it into action.
- That something is **networks**. To supply a hundred trillion cells "in an efficient,
  roughly democratic fashion" you evolve distribution networks. And **the scaling laws
  are the scaling of those networks.**

The payoff is the counterintuitive part. Evolution is contingent — frozen accidents,
niches, survival of the fittest — so you would expect nothing systematic to survive
it. Instead: *"in terms of anything that you could measure about us, we're actually
scaled versions of both an elephant and a mouse."* Because the same class of network
governs all of them.

### The brain is the anomaly — and the bridge to cities

- The brain does **not** scale linearly with body size the way other organs do. An
  elephant's brain is a much smaller fraction of its body mass than ours; ours is a
  smaller fraction than a mouse's. **You need proportionately less brain the bigger
  you are.** (Look at a baby: enormous head, and it is essentially finished growing by
  about five.)
- But white and gray matter often scale **superlinearly** — more per capita — which is
  rare in biology and is the same signature cities show.
- Hence the analogy that runs through all four episodes: a city is the intertwining of
  **physicality** (buildings, roads, gas and electric lines, transport) with
  **information exchange** (social networks). *"One can think of the city as the stage
  and the infrastructure that is there to facilitate social interaction."*
- Infrastructure scales **sublinearly** — the bigger the city, the less of everything
  per capita, exactly like biology. Social output scales **superlinearly** — more
  interaction, more wages, more innovation, more disease, more COVID-19 per capita,
  "because the city is doing what it should be doing."

### The interface nobody works on

The honest gap, and West says it is his current work: *"Although I must say I'm not
making huge progress."* The energy networks and the information networks are
"necessarily intertwined and integrated… carefully tuned to one another," and almost
nobody studies the interface. His example is sharp — an fMRI does not measure the
neural network at all. **It measures blood flow, and assumes that is a proxy.**

### Why there is no Godzilla, and what actually stops a city

- **Organisms:** the point of the network is to supply cells. As the system grows, the
  capillaries feeding those cells get further and further apart, until you cannot
  deliver enough energy. Whales come near the limit. Larger is "extremely unlikely" —
  with the caveat, which he insists on, *given the body designs and chemistry that
  happen to have evolved.*
- **Cities:** nothing in the equations stops them. Nothing in the *world* is another
  matter. Los Angeles already struggles to supply its cells; going to 50 million means
  25-lane freeways and ten-track rail lines, "and even with electric cars, I'm not
  sure we could do it." The condition that matters is that everyone be connected to
  **one** network.
- So what actually happens is not a wall but a **phase change**: the city
  *"alchemizes, it breaks off."* Physically contiguous, sociologically two or three or
  four semi-autonomous cities whose social networks are only loosely coupled. We
  already see it.
- A definition falls out of this: **you belong to a city if you interact
  socioeconomically with someone in it above some cutoff rate** — say once a week. You
  can legitimately be a citizen of two. (Cf. Melanie Moses on ant colonies as the
  adaptation to the size limit on insects, and page 406 of the *Scale* paperback: the
  **longest-lived companies are modest-sized and operate in highly specialized niche
  markets**, and look nothing like the Fortune 500.)

### The good, the bad and the ugly are the same mechanism

*"The same dynamic — indeed, the same mathematics — that governs the spread of ideas
and the growth of innovation and the growth of cities"* governs the spread of disease,
crime, antisocial behaviour and corruption. Which is why you cannot reduce social
connectivity to suppress the bad without suppressing the good in the same stroke.

On the pandemic substitute for physical presence, in June 2020: Zoom and Skype have
"done remarkably well, I must say, I'm amazed" — **and they are two-dimensional.
"They're soulless, they're not four-dimensional."** Meaningful interaction happens in
what he calls four-dimensional space: near enough to smell and touch you, and to see
what is happening around you. He expects cities to return to trajectory once a new
stable configuration settles, with genuine but quantitative changes. And: *"the future
of the planet is completely dependent upon the future of our cities."*

## S1:E36 — Open-ended growth, and the treadmill (25 June 2020, 58:18)

### "You gotta be somewhere"

Against the idea that the internet decouples social networks from geography: network
diagrams floating in the cloud are a metaphor. *"Every node has to be someplace. You
have to be in your kitchen, in your bathroom, in your office, on the train."* That is
"the hegemony of the infrastructure… the hegemony of Newton's law of gravitation."

And the two 15 %s are **not independent**: modelling the intertwined networks shows
that the ~15 % per-capita social gain and the ~15 % infrastructure saving drive each
other and cannot be separated. Which is the real reason he doubts the internet
dissolves place.

His June-2020 prediction on Facebook's permanent-remote announcement, offered
explicitly as intuition and not science: *"I sincerely doubt that that will happen. I think it might
for maybe six months, a year after things become sort of normalized."* Qualitatively
little changes; quantitatively, flexible hours and working from home will have a
lasting positive effect. The general mechanism he names is the useful one: **a
discontinuity in the social fabric speeds up things that were already happening in
some nascent way.**

### The Keynes warning label

After WWII, Keynes and Sir Charles Dickens (the novelist's grandson) both predicted
the coming crisis would be **too much leisure** — a 20-hour week, and what on earth
will people do with themselves. The week went 48 → 44 → 40 and stopped; Americans, he
argues, now work harder than ever, in an informal expansion of a nominally 40-hour
week. The only group they thought would work *more* than 40 hours: scientists and
technicians. West's own moral: *"this is also a lesson in why it's very dangerous to
speculate too much about the future… when you have people like Keynes, an
extraordinary man, getting it all wrong, you know that you're on dangerous ground."*

### The finite-time singularity

The core argument, in his own order:

1. **Sublinear scaling (biology) → bounded growth.** Fast growth in childhood, then a
   stable asymptotic size you live most of your life at. Death is in the equations
   too. That stable configuration is the source of biological sustainability.
2. **Superlinear scaling (cities, economies) → open-ended growth.** Same equations,
   different exponent. This is the paradigm modern society runs on and it fits the
   data, qualitatively and quantitatively.
3. **Life gets faster the bigger you are** — and you feel it. Where a human lifespan
   used to contain no major paradigm shift, it now contains one or two. Hence "a sense
   of anxiety, a sense of the floor shifting under your feet."
4. **Open-ended growth implies a finite-time singularity** — infinite size in finite
   time, which is absurd, and the equations tell you what happens instead: **before
   you reach it, the system stagnates and collapses.**
5. **Innovation is the escape, and this is not the Malthusian argument.** Malthus and
   Ehrlich were wrong precisely because we innovate: the Industrial Revolution, oil,
   the automobile, the telephone, IT, the computer — each a paradigm shift that
   **resets the clock to zero.** You can state it almost as a theorem: to sustain
   open-ended growth indefinitely you must reinvent yourself periodically.
6. **And the interval shrinks.** "It's like being on a treadmill that's accelerating,
   and at some stage you've got to jump off onto another treadmill that's accelerating
   even faster." A paradigm shift is a **stop-gap, not a solution**. Reductio: you
   would eventually need something like the invention of the internet every eight
   months, then every six.

The image is Sisyphus, and West insists Sisyphus had it easy: *"the rock and the
mountain remained the same every time… Ours, unfortunately, every time you get to the
top and it rolls down, the ball gets bigger and the mountain higher."* The endpoint he
names is a **socioeconomic heart attack**.

(Worth reading with his own honesty about the numbers: describing the compression of
computing paradigms he says *"I don't know; I'm making up these numbers, actually."*
The shape is the claim; the intervals are not.)

### The way out he actually believes in

He got despondent, then found he had been **confounding innovation with technology**.
*"When you think of the word 'innovation'… you always think of technology these days.
But of course innovation is fortunately much broader than that."* Cultural innovation
counts. And the question that follows is the one he says nobody has answered:

> **"Can we have the kind of vibrant high standards and quality of living that we have
> with innovation and new ideas without growth? No one knows the answer to that."**

His grounds for hope are deliberately unscientific and he flags them as such ("I'll
probably be fired by the Institute after this"): the 2016 US election showed that a
**phase transition in what is socially possible can happen in under a year**, when
everyone had assumed such a shift would take fifty or a hundred. If the transition can
run that fast in one direction — appealing to what he calls the dark side, which "is
there" in everyone — then a comparably fast transition in the opposite direction is
not impossible. That is the whole argument: not optimism about people, but evidence
about **the speed at which collective norms can flip.**

### The exponential rant (the most immediately usable thing he says)

- "Exponential" has degenerated into a colloquial synonym for *very fast*. The irony:
  **an exponential is slow at the beginning** and only becomes dangerous much later.
  People hear it and picture something linear moving quickly.
- During the pandemic everyone reported **doubling times**. He wanted to write in the
  *New York Times* that this is the wrong unit. Early on, the **factor-of-ten time was
  about a week**: a hundred now → a thousand in a week → ten thousand → a hundred
  thousand → a million in a month. *"Ten has much more punch than a shitty little
  doubling."*
- And there is **no English word for increasing by a factor of ten**. He looked it up.
  ("Deciling," he improvises, unhappily.)
- The real danger is not even exponential — exponential is the borderline case. **What
  we have been doing is super-exponential.**

### What the framework leaves out — stated by its author

*"It's a theory. It's a model."* It explains an enormous amount of data on a very
coarse-grained scale and it has made confirmed predictions. What it does **not**
contain: the long-run influence of the internet, an unexpected social movement, an
asteroid. He extrapolates by assuming the dominant past variables — the structure of
social networks — continue to dominate, and says so. The contrast he draws with his
own earlier career is the useful methodological line: particle physics makes **precise
predictions of precise phenomena**; this makes **coarse-grained predictions of
imprecise phenomena** — but it is analytic and quantitative, and that is what makes it
science rather than storytelling.

The thread the conversation runs out of time on, left hanging deliberately: a human
this size, eating ~2,000 food calories a day, is a **90-watt** animal — but plugged
into a city we each burn about **11,000 watts**. *"So what is a human being now?"*

## S1:E74 — Reflections on COVID, with David Krakauer (21 Dec 2021, 1:10:52)

Occasion: the SFI Press volume *The Complex Alternative* — 60+ contributors, each
original COVID essay followed by the author's own reflection on what they got right
and wrong. "The complex alternative" means the alternative **to simplicity**.

### Where coarse-graining has to live

Krakauer's framing is the sharpest thing in the episode. Two poles:

- The **Standard Model** of physics — the model of the non-living universe — has about
  **25 free parameters**. It is beholden to symmetry, minimality, elegance: all of
  which are "surrogates for understanding," for being able to grasp something in your
  mind.
- **GPT-3** — a model of *adaptive* reality that organizes a bit of text, not the
  universe — has **175 billion free parameters**. Predictive, incomprehensible: *"you
  sacrifice clarity for the opacity of the predictive algorithm."*

> **"What's coarse-grained theory look like? It's not going to look like the standard
> model, but it sure as hell should not look like GPT-3."**

West prefers "coarse-grained / low resolution" to "simplicity", and adds the working
recipe: a small number of dominant variables **as a baseline and point of departure**,
then add resolution deliberately. And the indictment: *"as far as I know, it actually
hasn't been done. That sort of strategy was not employed during the pandemic."* The
classic SIR model is elegant and a hundred years old and sits squarely in the physics
paradigm — deterministic, no statistics, "certainly pays no attention to the vagaries
of human interaction and social behaviour."

### The admitted failure: we cannot theorize agency

This is the episode's real payload, and Krakauer states it twice because the producers
lead with it:

> *"Where we're not good is how do we put in agency and reflexivity. We're not
> particles and fields. We have beliefs and desires and superstitions. And I think the
> great lesson of the last two years was that we don't know how to do it. And we got
> frustrated, and the way we manifested our own limitations, I think, as scientists is
> to say: look how irrational people are. Well, yes — but that's what it means to be
> human. What we were really saying is we don't know how to, in a reasonably
> parsimonious way, theorize human agency."*

West: in the end these are political problems, and "how do you put that into your
equations" is the challenge. The community is itself split — one camp says get
behaviour into the models or they are useless; the other says drop deterministic mass
action, put stochasticity in, and accept that **you get causality out, not
prediction**.

### Preparation beats reaction (the Clausewitz principle)

Krakauer's reversal on his own optimism: he had hoped the fact that individuals could
*act* — get vaccinated, isolate, explain to others — would make COVID different from a
cancer diagnosis where you must defer to expertise. It did not play out. His
conclusion: **the timescale was wrong.** *"Politics is war by other means"* — the
strategy has to be worked out before the battle. Expecting people to learn what an
mRNA vaccine is *during* an epidemic is "extremely optimistic." So preparedness must
be **preventative, like diet, sleep and exercise** — a systematic educational effort
running for decades. *"I just don't think you can act rationally in the heat of the
moment."*

West agrees and adds the institutional version: we were globally unprepared "in a very
disgraceful way," despite epidemiologists having said so for years. He wants a
standing-army analogue — permanently on standby — while noting war is the wrong
metaphor. And his personal shock: the assumption that turning it over to Anthony Fauci
was sufficient, held *"ironically throughout the academic community"*, not just among
politicians.

### What you are not allowed to black-box

Krakauer: most people cannot explain a television, a combustion engine or a vaccine
and use all three daily. The pandemic turned that into a moral question — **"what can
you not outsource?"** If you believe a vaccine can be transmitted over 5G and a USB
stick is a shield, something has broken. *"And that's really the whole AI debate."*

His corrective to the anti-vax surprise is the best historical note in the episode:
when Semmelweis proposed handwashing in maternity clinics he was resisted — he was
Austro-Hungarian, so the British rejected it for not being British, and the politics
and xenophobia of the early 19th century "denied a self-evident cheap remedy" for
decades. *"I'm a little shocked by our shock, I guess."*

### The correction: the urban dividend is a top-decile phenomenon

The single most important update to everything in E35/E36, from West's own paper with
Elisa Mora, Vicky Yang and Chris Kempes on urban income inequality in the US:

> **Income in the lowest decile scales close to *linearly* with city population.
> Income in the top decile scales significantly *superlinearly*.**

In West's words: *"if you're in the lowest deciles… you're getting nothing out of the
city. You're not getting that benefit that's come from the positive feedback in social
interaction. Whereas the rich, the top deciles, are getting more than their share."*
He calls it very disturbing and distressing. Krakauer, less gently: *"Geoffrey started
as a bit of a Pollyanna — with the cities, they were these general accelerators,
everyone benefits. And then you look carefully at the data and you realize, no,
they're much more like some of these online winner-takes-all platforms."* He puts
cities in the same class as Uber and online trading platforms: **nonlinear engines
that produce highly skewed equilibria**, and says understanding that mechanism is an
open SFI research project.

### Fragmentation as a feature

Does a community that is *not* fully connected help rare ideas survive against the
population-average idea? Krakauer thinks so, and offers SFI itself as the evidence:
it sits on a mountain in a very low-density state, *"and I think it's critical to the
production and persistence of novel ideas. I think if we were in a city, it couldn't
work — and people have tried and failed. So there is an argument for a kind of
isolation, but hopefully isolation doesn't have to equal intolerance."*

West's version: a city is a collective made of neighbourhoods that behave differently
and sometimes oppose each other. Against the image of the social reactor stands the
image of one person going up a mountain and coming back with the commandments. *"It
could be that when the collective breaks up into pieces and allows for more freedom of
interaction amongst smaller groups, even down to the individual, maybe that is even
more productive than just the willy-nilly gas of ten million people bumping into each
other. I don't know the answer."*

And the empirical anchor, from James Evans' work across tens of millions of papers:
**the larger the team, the more likely the paper is cited — and the less likely it is
to be disruptive.** An inverse relationship. Small groups (two, or one) are the most
disruptive, "but no one listens"; large groups have large networks and get cited.
Krakauer's read: *"it's almost as if we want to be periodic and move between those two
states."*

### Zoom, honestly, from a man who defended remote work's upside

West's anecdote against his own convenience: a collaboration with Chris Kempes,
Manfred Laubichler and a postdoc ran on Zoom for a year, met weekly, made progress,
wrote a paper. Then they got one in-person window. *"We did more in that hour and a
half — tons of ideas and excitement and writing things down — than we felt we had done
in the previous year meeting essentially every week."*

Immediately followed by the caveat that most commentary on the future of work omits:
*"we're talking about people whose jobs are amenable to being at home. Whereas there
is a huge number of people that have to be at a place… So it's a little elitist to
only talk in those terms about the future of work."*

### Blame the system, not the person

Krakauer: *"We like to blame people and we're not good at blaming systems. And that's
what makes socio-economic reform hard, because that's what has to be fixed. It's not a
person. So we'd like to fire people and elect new presidents under the illusion that
that's going to change everything. But of course it doesn't change much."*

He sees a real cultural shift toward the systemic view: Niall Ferguson, a historian,
embracing complexity science and rejecting great-man causality; Tolstoy already
arguing for "a kind of statistical mechanics of history" at the end of *War and Peace*;
the fall of Rome as a plague rather than as Trajan or Augustus; and Jonathan Rauch's
*The Constitution of Knowledge*, which is about the constitution — **an operating
system for society** — rather than about Madison and Jefferson.

West's counterweight: our *culture* is still individualistic, and the pandemic exposed
how deep the sanctity-of-the-body intuition runs — *"even if my body is causing damage
to lots of people, you still can't touch it."* Individual agency, he suspects, is
historically a **luxury of the elite** and a modern phenomenon.

### What actually went right

Asked to name successes rather than failures:

- **Vaccine development.** Discoveries deployed in a short period — a decade of
  underlying science, not one year — is "a scientific success story however you look
  at it, no doubt about it."
- **The failure attached to it:** imagining that was all we needed.
- **SFI's own call was conceptually right**: this was never only an epidemiological
  problem. Behaviour, complex contagion, network structure, energy, vulnerable
  populations, economic implications, isolation, depression. *"I think that was a
  success for SFI, but it was probably a failure for the rest of the world."*

### The two closing burning questions

- **Krakauer:** *"We do not understand how collective intelligence works, and we had a
  wonderful — we were all schooled in collective stupidity… We understand how stars
  work, how systems work, engines work, computers work, but we're not even in the
  foothills of understanding how complex reality works."*
- **West:** the effort is minuscule — "about 150 people" — against the money and
  intelligent time poured into medical science. And the field needs to get "in bed
  with" practitioners and politicians who are not scientifically minded.
- **Krakauer's immediate objection to West's own proposal**, which is why the episode
  ends unresolved: *"as soon as you move in that direction, the great magnets of
  ideological alignment start to operate on you… we are very nervous of moving down
  that path without then being written off as pundits for a particular political
  perspective. And I don't know how to solve that."* West adds the second horn: if it
  were embraced, "we would get swallowed up" — and being small has been the operational
  strength. *"Small is beautiful."*

## S2:E1 — Physics of Life, Ep 1 (31 Jan 2024, 34:55)

A different show: seasons, two hosts (Abha Eli Phoboo and Chris Kempes), two guests
(Vijay Balasubramanian and West), and a producer's ear. West's segment was recorded on
a deck overlooking a dry riverbed in South Africa during the Complexity Global School;
lions, baboons and mongooses interrupt him mid-sentence and stay in the edit.

### "It wasn't so much life, it was more death"

The origin story, more personal than in E35. In the early 1990s the **Superconducting
Super Collider** — the machine that was the lifeblood of his field — was cancelled by
the Clinton administration after costs ballooned and foreign investment never arrived.
Simultaneously he was in his early-to-mid 50s, *"and it turns out that I come from a
short-lived line of men, people who die young, and I had always expected to die
relatively young, meaning in my early 60s."* The machine was dying and he became
conscious of his own death.

Meanwhile the fashionable line in Washington was that physics was the science of the
19th and 20th centuries and biology would be the science of the 21st. His reaction —
*"without knowing any biology, by the way"* — was the arrogant one: **if biology were
a real science it would have to incorporate the paradigms, culture and techniques of
physics**, moving from qualitative to principled, mathematical, computational,
predictive. Then he decided to put his money where his mouth was, and asked: **what
determines that a human lifespan is of order 100 years and not 1,000, or a million, or
ten?** He looked at the literature and found aging and mortality was "quite a backwater
of biology."

### Galileo asked the first scaling question

Why can't we have infinitely tall trees or buildings? Double the size and you double
the weight — but **strength does not increase linearly**. Beams and limbs scale
nonlinearly, and eventually weight crushes strength.

And the line that compresses the whole research programme: *"an elephant is actually
not just a scaled-up mouse but a scaled-down whale and a scaled-up giraffe, which is a
scaled-up human being, biologically."*

### The sleep prediction — a real falsifiable moment

With Van Savage he derived how much an animal should sleep from how its metabolism
scales. They put in the numbers on the blackboard and got **3–4 hours for an
elephant**. West's reaction: *"That's great. But there's a real problem here… which is
obviously nuts!"* They went away assuming they had missed something. Then Savage called
back: elephants sleep two to three hours. Mice sleep 16–17. The same theory explains
why human babies sleep 16–17 hours and settle ontogenetically at eight.

### Why we die at 100, and why the brain is different

- Metabolism keeps you alive **and** produces wear and tear — like a car, a washing
  machine, the pipes in your house. Living things are not at equilibrium the way a
  rock is.
- We evolved repair mechanisms, but **repair is exceedingly costly**. Natural selection
  funds it well enough to get you to 35 or 40 and produce children — "and then natural
  selection doesn't care, and we die." Expected human lifespan was close to 40 or less
  until roughly the last century.
- **One organ has to be repaired faithfully: the brain**, because damage there "very
  quickly results in you not being you." Which is why repair mechanisms in the neural
  system are so strong, and why the brain takes proportionately much more metabolic
  energy than the rest of the body — *"it's because it's fighting the forces of
  entropy."*
- *"Sleep is like death — or maybe one can console oneself at my age that death is like
  sleep."*

### Balasubramanian's half: thinking *is* compression

Worth keeping even though he is not West:

- **Cognition is coarse-graining.** Borges' *Funes the Memorious*: a man kicked in the
  head wakes with perfect, total recall — he sees a tree and all its individual leaves,
  and therefore cannot see why one tree belongs in the same category as another. *"He
  slowly loses the ability to think, because cognition is really about this process of
  making effective theories… compressing the complexity of all the detail into these
  effective concepts."*
- The brain is the most expensive organ we own, and is constrained in **space, cells,
  energy and time**. The time constraint is the one people forget: *"if I have to make
  a decision and I take a day to do it, it'll probably be too late."* All four
  constrain the architecture of the circuits.
- *"If you are at equilibrium as a living thing, you're dead."* Life funnels energy
  through itself — which is why the physics of active/living matter had to be invented
  separately.

### The closing, with lions

## The solo-builder read

Not motivation. Five structural claims that change what you would actually do.

**1. Being *in* a superlinear network is not the same as capturing its returns — and
the difference is a decile, not an effort level.** This is the E74 correction and it is
the most useful thing in all four episodes. The famous urban dividend (+15 % per
capita per doubling) is a **top-decile** phenomenon; the bottom decile scales linearly
and "gets nothing out of the city." Krakauer generalises it explicitly to winner-take-
all platforms. Every distribution channel a solo builder uses — an app store, Google,
a freelance marketplace, a social feed — is that same nonlinear engine with the same
skewed equilibrium. **The median listing on a superlinear platform earns the linear
return, which is approximately nothing.** So "we published it, we're on the platform"
is not a distribution strategy; it is the linear decile. This is the same finding as
four months of calculators producing four clicks a month, arriving from the other
direction: the bottleneck was never inventory.

**2. The escape from the treadmill is not the next artifact.** West's theorem is that
open-ended growth survives only through periodic reinvention, and that **the required
interval keeps shrinking** — each paradigm shift is a stop-gap that buys less time than
the last. At personal scale that is what a high build cadence with flat revenue looks
like: every new artifact resets the clock briefly, and the next one has to come faster.
West's own way out of the despondency is the part to steal: he caught himself
**confounding innovation with technology**. The reset that actually re-bases the curve
may be cultural or distributional, not technical — a different channel, a different
buyer, a different way of being found. Building more, faster, is the treadmill, not the
jump.

**3. Watch the maintenance/production split — it is the growth equation, not a mood.**
Sublinear systems stop growing because supply costs eat the surplus; the E35 exchange
about Brian Arthur makes the point that a large enough economy has to shift resource
allocation from productivity to distribution and maintenance. A repo portfolio has the
same metabolism. A month where the repo receiving the most engineering work receives it
almost entirely as hardening, refactors, test gaps and docs is a portfolio spending its
budget on repair. Repair is not optional — West is emphatic that it is what keeps you
alive — but **selection only funds repair up to reproductive age**, and it never funds
it for its own sake. The question to ask each cycle is not "is this work good?" but
"which organ is this, and does it have to be repaired faithfully?"

**4. Report factor-of-ten times, not doublings — and never say "exponential" when you
mean "fast".** Directly operational. A metric that ten-xes in a week and a metric that
doubles in a week feel similar in a sentence and differ by orders of magnitude in a
month. When a number is moving, write down the **deciling time**; when it isn't,
saying "growing exponentially" is a claim about the *shape*, and an exponential is slow
at the start, which is exactly when people say it. And the real hazard is
**super-exponential** — the case where the growth rate itself is growing, which is what
both West and any burn-rate curve are describing.

**5. Small and disconnected is a real strategy, not a consolation.** The Evans finding
is empirical: **large teams get cited, small teams disrupt, and the relationship is
inverse.** Krakauer defends SFI's mountain — low density is *"critical to the
production and persistence of novel ideas… if we were in a city, it couldn't work, and
people have tried and failed."* For anyone building alone without a network, this
reframes the position accurately rather than flatteringly: the isolation genuinely
protects the rare idea from the population-average idea, and it genuinely costs you
citation — which is to say, distribution. Both halves are true, which is why Krakauer's
conclusion is **periodicity**: move between the two states deliberately. Isolation to
produce the idea; connection to get it heard. Choosing one permanently is the error in
either direction.

**And the caveat West puts on his own work, which is the standard to hold any model
to:** it makes *coarse-grained predictions of imprecise phenomena*; it leaves out the
internet, unexpected social movements and asteroids; it works because it fits a lot of
data and is analytic and quantitative. When he does not know, he says "I don't know the
answer to that" — four times across these episodes — and when he is speculating he says
he has taken off his scientific hat. That is the tell that separates a model from a
story.

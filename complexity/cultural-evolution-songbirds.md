# Cultural evolution in humans and songbirds

> The headline finding, and it is counterintuitive enough to be worth the whole
> episode: **partial connectivity beats full connectivity.** If everyone can see
> everyone else's solution, problems get solved faster in the short run and the
> population converges on one solution — and is worse off in the long run. Groups
> that innovate separately and *then* meet carry more unique solutions to
> recombine. Measured in real hunter-gatherer social networks: innovations spread
> **faster** when people exchanged information only along their actual (restricted)
> networks than when everybody exchanged with everybody.

Companion notes: [`thinking-in-systems.md`](thinking-in-systems.md) (structure
determines behaviour) and [`nature-of-intelligence.md`](nature-of-intelligence.md)
(learning vs. innate). This episode is the *evolutionary* case for structured
heterogeneity — and it converges, from a completely different direction, on
Krakauer's "engineer misalignments"
([`rigorous-uncertainty-covid.md`](rigorous-uncertainty-covid.md)).

## What makes cultural evolution different from genetic evolution

- **Genes are vertical and one-shot.** You get them from your parents, at
  inception, and that's the channel.
- **Culture has many input channels and never closes.** Parents, other adults,
  peers, teachers who are *dedicated* to transmitting information — plus your own
  innovation, plus the ability to change your mind. You have your whole life to
  acquire and modify traits. "So there's the potential for so much more
  complexity in learned behaviours."
- **Both inheritance and rejection are real.** Children demonstrably reject
  parental traits (the "haircuts and hemlines" effect — what you saw in your
  parents' generation reads as outdated and worthy of rejection), *and* political
  and religious beliefs correlate with parents more often than not. Any model has
  to carry both a probability of adoption and a probability of wholehearted
  rejection — which is where the dynamics get genuinely complex.
- **Culture feeds back into genes.** Assortative mating on cultural traits —
  geography, shared language, beliefs, commitments — narrows the field of possible
  partners, and those cultural traits then become correlated with genetic
  evolution.

## "Greater than the sum of its parts" — culture changing its own parameters

The puzzle: around 50,000 years ago, at the Middle-to-Upper Palaeolithic
transition, the record shows art, jewellery, musical instruments and an explosion
of tool types in a relatively narrow window.

- **The methodological caveat comes first**, and it is Darwin's own point about
  the fossil record: a sparse, imperfect record *manufactures* apparent
  suddenness. "If you only have a dinosaur fossil from every million years, you
  might think that there was rapid change when there was actually a very slow
  change." Before explaining a step change, check whether the sampling made it.
- The standard explanations all invoke an **external** cause: a dramatic
  environmental shift, a change in the human brain, the emergence of language.
- **Creanza, Kolodny and Feldman's move (Occam's razor):** can a *purely cultural*
  process produce the same pattern? Their hypothesis is that **human culture can
  change the parameters of its own evolution** — populations carrying different
  toolkits come into contact, combine tools, and the repertoire explodes, with no
  cognitive, genetic or environmental change required. The model is deliberately
  offered as a **null hypothesis**, not as the answer.

### Tools get lost even when they are useful

- Henrich's influential 2004 model links **population size to the rate of cultural
  loss**. The mechanism is a sampling argument: you identify the best needle-maker
  and try to copy them, and on average you land slightly *worse* than the master.
  With a thousand learners, a few will overshoot and the average proficiency
  ratchets upward. With a very small population, the sampling alone can drag the
  best available model downward generation over generation.
- **Contact is the repair mechanism.** Separate groups don't lose the *same* tools,
  so migration and contact reintroduce a lost tool or a substitute for it.
  Isolation loses; contact re-seeds.
- **Writing preserves what practitioners can't.** Writing systems, drawings and
  oral histories keep a trait alive even when the best living practitioner has
  degraded — "if your best model of making that tool isn't as good as it used to
  be, but someone wrote down some information before, then you might have a better
  chance of preserving that cultural process."
- **The simulation result that names the paper:** two populations in regular
  contact can maintain a repertoire **larger than either would hold alone — and
  larger than one population of their combined size.**

### Partial connectivity: the central finding

- If the world is "totally flat" and everyone knows how everyone else solves
  problems, the short term looks great: you can access everyone's innovations. But
  **in the long run the population converges on one solution and is worse off.**
  Independent solutions are the raw material for recombination against a harder
  problem later.
- The empirical case Creanza cites: anthropologists instrumented real
  hunter-gatherer groups with wearables to measure who was actually near whom.
  An innovation spread **more slowly when everyone exchanged information** than
  when it moved along their real social networks — and faster still when exchange
  was restricted to kin. **"The more that they restricted their social networks,
  the faster innovations were able to spread and solve problems."**
- Related work: Derex & Boyd on partial connectivity; Carja & Creanza,
  *The evolutionary advantage of cultural memory in heterogeneous contact
  networks* — network structure supports or suppresses innovation and variance.
- Globalisation is therefore genuinely two-sided: local loss (bookstores, regional
  dialects) against a global gain, with diversity of perspective as the thing
  actually doing the work.
- Same logic drives her argument for interdisciplinarity: the "Bridging cultural
  gaps" workshop existed because evolutionary biology, anthropology, archaeology,
  economics, psychology, cognitive science and behavioural ecology study the same
  underpinnings under different terminology. Putting them in a room and forcing
  "what do you mean by this?" is the recombination step.

## Niche construction: why the population-size debate had no answer

The field had a contradiction. Kline & Boyd found that larger Oceanian fishing
populations had larger, more complex tool repertoires. Others went elsewhere and
found nothing — so the conclusion was that the relationship "can't be universal."

**Creanza and Fogarty's resolution: split the studies by subsistence mode.**

- **Food-gathering (hunter-gatherer) societies: mostly no relationship** between
  population size and cultural complexity.
- **Food-producing societies (agricultural, modern): the correlation appears.**

The proposed mechanism is **niche construction** — humans altering their
environment and thereby altering their own evolution. A population that modifies
its environment to produce food is *buffered* against environmental change: the
same tool repertoire keeps working, so it accumulates and compounds. A
hunter-gatherer population hit by drought, foliage change or migration finds its
tools no longer suited to the environment, and **replaces rather than
accumulates** — so the repertoire tracks environmental fluctuation, not population
size.

Two environmental dimensions have to be kept separate: **harshness** and
**variability** (the rate of change). When the environment's effect on survival
and innovation is strong, the effect of population size becomes more complex, and
depends crucially on how fast the environment is changing. (Michael links this to
Jessica Flack's collective computation: a society coarse-grains its environment,
and that computation breaks down when the environment changes too fast.)

**Modern harshness is redefined, not removed.** We are buffered — 72°F indoors,
food always available — but harshness in the model's sense is *how many problems
you have to solve*, and by that measure we have plenty, along with the resources
to attack them.

### Necessity or opportunity as the mother of invention

Creanza's current thinking with Oren Kolodny, and the most personally useful idea
in the episode:

> If you are operating at your capacity for what you can handle, you don't have
> the time to solve the problem. **"Maybe opportunity is the mother of
> invention"** — a week at SFI let her thoughts drift to problems she "wouldn't
> solve if I was completely overbooked every single day."

She keeps the balance explicit: there is an interplay between *needing* to solve
problems and *having the time* to do it. "Where are humans right now on that
spectrum, and how do we give ourselves the space to solve them?"

On supply-chain disruption (asked in March 2020, prophetically): a **short**
disruption of cultural networks and we bounce back; a **long** one and we may not,
in the same way. "It would take a very short amount of time before you realise
that some of the knowledge you thought you had at your fingertips isn't there
anymore." The silver lining Michael offers and she accepts: a long disruption
forces unique local solutions and might kick the species off the local optimum
that global homogeneity has trapped it on.

## Birdsong: millions of years of cultural evolution you can actually measure

- **Oscine songbirds** are one clade sharing a common ancestor tens of millions of
  years ago; somewhere in that lineage song learning evolved, and — a genuine
  cultural ratchet — **no lineage appears to have lost the ability to learn to
  sing.** That makes birdsong a record of millions of years of cultural evolution.
- **Two learning strategies, and it isn't a choice.** *Closed-ended*: juveniles
  learn a song from an adult, the sensitive period closes at sexual maturity, and
  the song is fixed for life. *Open-ended*: mimics and mockingbirds keep modifying
  and mimicking for the rest of their lives. Open-ended learning arose through
  several **independent** evolutionary events.
- **The human parallel is domain-specific.** Language is learned best before
  puberty — afterwards it's "a textbook way of learning" rather than absorption —
  while something like toolmaking is preferentially learned later. Different
  learning windows for different domains, in the same organism.
- **The puzzle: why would lifelong learning ever be lost?** It looks purely
  adaptive — sing badly, fail to attract a mate, try a different song. So the
  field assumed it must carry a cost (cognitive or metabolic).
  **Creanza's answer: sexual selection for large repertoires can offset that
  cost.** If the female is listening for a short, species-specific sequence,
  paying for lifelong learning isn't worth it. If she is listening for maximum
  complexity, it is. The theory was then borne out empirically: species where song
  complexity correlates with reproductive success tend to be the species that
  already carry large repertoires. Peacock's-tail logic — an elaborate signal is
  credible precisely because it was maintained while evading predators and finding
  food.
- **The reframe she is working on now** (with postdoc Emily Hudson): maybe
  **birdsong is more like an arrowhead than like a language** — its function
  closer to a *tool* than to a communication behaviour. Note what changes with the
  category: songs get measured by complexity or by performance (how fast, how
  stereotyped), which is not how we measure linguistic capability, where the point
  is conveying information.
- **Stone tools need language.** Laland's group found these tools were
  *dramatically* easier to transmit **with** language than without — showing and
  miming was much harder to pick up even when the demonstrator knew exactly what
  they were doing. Stout's group found it took on the order of **a hundred hours**
  of training and practice to produce one. Conclusion: human communication and
  human culture co-evolved and facilitated each other.
- **Dialects, and a familiar-looking null result.** Birds in one region sing alike;
  a few miles on they sound different. Peter Marler reportedly could be dropped
  blindfolded into the Bay Area and locate himself from the white-crowned sparrow
  dialect. The natural hypothesis — dialect signals local adaptation, so mate
  choice on dialect should produce genetic clustering — was tested, and **some
  studies found it and some didn't, even across subspecies of the same sparrow.**
  Creanza notes this is "somewhat analogous to the human cultural complexity
  literature": the same shape of unresolved debate, in birds.
- **Her synthesis:** learn from those who share your environment (information
  immediately useful in your context) *and* from those who don't (information that
  buffers you, useful across more contexts). **"Both humans and birds hedge their
  bets as much as they can. And the more you can learn and the more flexible your
  learning is, the better you might be at hedging your bets."**
- **What keeps her up at night:** cultural evolution on the multi-million-year
  timescale — mining hundreds of thousands of citizen-science birdsong recordings,
  building the computational tools and interfaces to extract features, and mapping
  them back onto phylogenies. Layered pressures: body size, learning duration,
  dense foliage versus prairie, plus sexual selection and male–male and
  male–female interaction. And the open question underneath everything: why humans
  are so good at cumulative culture and other species are not — "or maybe they're
  better at hiding it from us."

## Solo-builder read (insights for Jens)

- **Partial connectivity reframes "I have no network" from deficit to structure.**
  Your standing constraint is that you have no audience and no warm circle. This
  research says the *failure mode of full connection* is convergence: everyone
  reading the same founder podcasts, running the same playbooks, arriving at the
  same solution and collectively getting worse at hard problems. Your isolation is
  producing genuinely unique solutions — an autonomous agent partner wired into
  your actual business is not a thing the flat world converges on. **But the model
  is explicit that the payoff is realised at contact, not in isolation.** Unique
  solutions are only worth anything when two populations meet. That is precisely
  the distribution problem, restated in evolutionary terms — and it says the
  answer is *contact*, not *more toolkit*.
- **You are a population of one, and Henrich's mechanism is running on you.**
  Small population → the best available model drifts *downward* through sampling
  alone → tools get lost even when useful. The paper's own antidote is the one you
  already built without knowing it was the antidote: **writing preserves what
  practitioners can't.** `field-notes/`, `MEMORY.md`, the skills library and the
  learnings archive are literally the "writing system" that stops a
  population-of-one from losing its repertoire. This is the strongest argument
  yet for keeping that discipline — and for the housekeeper routine, since a
  record nobody can read has stopped being a record.
- **Food-gathering versus food-producing is the sharpest business analogy in the
  episode, and it isn't flattering.** The correlation between size and repertoire
  only holds where niche construction buffers the population from environmental
  swings. Gathering mode: every environment change invalidates the toolkit, so you
  *replace* instead of *accumulate*. Look at the pattern — Trade Republic ships
  native CSV export and TradeGrab dies; Google policy-blocks the ad account and
  the channel dies; the SEO bet doesn't convert and FK/HC dies. Each shift
  discarded a repertoire rather than compounding one. **The question worth sitting
  with is which of your assets is actually a constructed niche** — something that
  keeps working across environment changes and therefore compounds. Launch Kit and
  the agent infrastructure are the only real candidates; the extension portfolio
  is gathering.
- **Opportunity, not necessity, is the mother of invention — and you are
  chronically overbooked.** Creanza's own good idea came from a week of slack at
  SFI. You run ~18 sessions a day across a house sale, freelance applications,
  bookkeeping and four repos. On this model that is not merely tiring, it is
  **causally suppressive of invention**: at capacity, you don't get to the problem
  at all. Slack is an input, not a reward. The honest counterweight she gives:
  you need a real problem *and* the time — pure leisure invents nothing either.
- **Sparse records manufacture false step-changes.** Darwin's caveat applies
  directly to your dashboards: with 2 clicks in 28 days or four leads in a week, an
  apparent jump may be sampling, not signal. This is the same rule the Krakauer
  episode reaches from the other side — bad data licenses only the crudest model.
  Two independent lines of complexity research converging on "don't over-read thin
  data" is about as strong as methodological advice gets.
- **Open-ended learning is costly and only pays where the market rewards the
  repertoire.** This is the check against scatter. Lifelong retooling — new
  framework, new niche, new extension — carries a real metabolic cost, and
  selection only pays for it where the buyer is "listening for maximum
  complexity." If your market is listening for one short, reliable,
  species-specific sequence, a growing repertoire is pure overhead. Ask per
  product which regime you are in *before* adding capability.
- **"Maybe a birdsong is more like an arrowhead than a language."** The move is
  Sutherland-shaped and cheap to run: question the category, and the metric
  changes with it. If FinGrab is a *tool*, complexity of features is the metric; if
  it is a *signal* in a store-search environment, the wording of the listing is,
  and that is exactly what the CWS ranking evidence showed. Getting the category
  wrong means optimising the wrong number with perfect discipline.
- **A hundred hours, and demonstration is not transmission.** Even someone who
  knows how to knap a stone transmits it dramatically better with explicit verbal
  explanation than by showing. That is the strongest available argument for how
  you write agent-task YAMLs and issue specs: a spec that *shows* (code pointers,
  examples) without *explaining* (why, what for, what counts as done) is the miming
  case — and the documented spec-drift failures look exactly like it.

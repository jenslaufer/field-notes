## 1. Ergodicity, in one story and one number

> *"And from him, I've learned a lesson that performance is subordinate to survival. That
> it's not the fastest skier who wins the race, but the fastest one amongst those who make
> it to the finish line."*

He is explicit that this is more than the obvious point:

> *"And here I'm just not, I'm not just making the battle point that survival matters to
> performance. We all know that, but I'm saying that it matters more than performance over
> the long-term."*

**The number that makes it bite.** A championship of 10 races; the skier has a 20 % chance
of winning each race and a 20 % chance of breaking his leg:

> *"The naive answer would be two races, because we think 10 races, 20% chance of winning
> each, that makes two. However, if you crunch the numbers, the real expected number of wins
> is only 0.64."*

> *"It's because irreversible losses absorb future gains."*

And the definition, which is the part worth memorising because it tells you when averages
are allowed:

> *"When losses are not irreversible, you are in an ergodic environment, and you can
> extrapolate averages. But in the real world, all losses are irreversible. They all absorb
> future gains, and so you cannot extrapolate averages."*

Provenance, in his own words: he first heard of ergodicity from Taleb — *"he mentioned this
briefly in his book, Antifragile"* — has **never actually met or spoken with him** (twice in
the same city, never managed it), read Ole Peters for the theory, *"and then I wrote the
practical part about it."*

## 2. Winning Long-Term Games: the day does not sum to the year

> *"The book is centered around the fighting, the false belief that if you get the most out
> of every day, you will also get the most out of the year. And that's false because if you
> want to get the most out of the year, you also need to do things which are not optimal for
> the day."*

**Worked example 1 — lying, which is really an example about repeated small risks:**

> *"You mentioned that you are a very good liar and you have a 99% chance of getting away
> with lying. That sounds like a perfect tactic. However, if you lie once a week, that 1%
> chance of getting caught means that you have a 99.5% chance of getting caught over the
> next 10 years. So what looks like a great short-term tactic becomes a terrible long-term
> strategy."*

**Worked example 2 — the newsletter, and the sentence that makes it a decision rather than
a virtue:**

> *"You can build trust or you can consume trust. And there are so many newsletters which,
> for example, they consume the trust of the readers by trying every email they're trying to
> sell something. And that, and there are other people like me who instead try to build
> trust. I don't write the email to sell something, but I try to write the email so that
> people are more likely to read my next email."*

> *"Now, the crucial thing is what's the best strategy? It depends by the time horizon.
> Because if you consider a six-month time horizon, the first strategy is much better. They
> will make more sales. But if you consider a 10-year time horizon, my strategy is better."*

The generalisation:

> *"The effect of skills, the effect of trust, and the effect of relationships do not allow
> you to extrapolate the return of short-term tactics to long-term. And if you want to win
> over the long-term, you need to accept that you will have to run strategies that are
> suboptimal in the short-term."*

## 3. Reproducible vs non-reproducible success — the test

Carlisle's framing, which Dellanna accepts: a reproducible strategy is one where *"if it's
adopted by 100 people, it leads to success for most of all of them, rather than requiring
luck or leaving me casualties behind."*

> *"Are they doing better in a way that's reproducible or not? Because if they're doing
> better in a way that's reproducible, which means if they do it again and again and again,
> they always get greater results, then you should learn from them. But if instead they're
> doing better than you in a way that's not reproducible, then you should absolutely not
> learn from them. You should ignore them."*

His roulette example (three people, three arbitrary betting rules, one wins) is deliberately
banal — and then he transplants it to stock picking with arbitrary selection rules, where
*"it's a bit more plausible"* that the winner's rule was good. Same structure, different
plausibility. That plausibility is the trap.

**Three questions he offers as the test:**

1. > *"If you ask yourself, like, in 20 parallel universes in how many will this strategy
   > succeed, it's a great question. And in general, you want to shift your thinking from
   > maximizing the expected average of to maximizing the distribution of our, of our edges.
   > Like, I don't want to be as rich as possible to have the highest expected wealth. But
   > for example, I just want to be wealthy in 20 out of 20 of the, of the universes."*
2. **Does the strategy have a failure mode that stops you playing?** *"There are some
   strategies which the worst that can happen is it's a little bit bad, but you can keep
   carrying it on. And there are some strategies where instead you might be constrained to
   stop playing."*
3. **Do you get closer to where you want even when you lose?** The failed-startup version:
   *"Like you never over promised you learn skills, you demonstrated good things, you learn
   things along the way, and your investors, even of course, they're not happy that they
   lost their money. They would still want to invest with you again."*

## 4. Kelly: why it is too aggressive, and it is not the math

Dellanna's objection is about inputs, not formulas:

> *"The Kelli Criterion is a formula, and like all formulas, they're only as good as the
> variables that you plug into them. And the thing is, when we plug variables into a formula,
> we often do not know their true value. And this is even true for variables that represent
> historical data."*

The S&P example is the sharp bit — **a historical number is one realised draw, not the
parameter**:

> *"You might check the data and maybe the data says that it was plus 25%. And you plug that
> in the formula and you think it's the real value of the variable, it's not. Why? Because
> what happened last year is that the conditions of the market produced the distribution of
> possible variables or possible outcomes from which random process peaked 25%. But that's
> not the real value, even if it's the historical value."*

Hence fractional Kelly: *"that's why serious and bad does, they use a fractional Kelly
criterion, which means you had the margin of safety."*

**Three further objections the hosts add, and they are independent of his:**

- **Jake — Kelly optimises the median, and the left tail is still there.** *"Not just for
  the potential being wrong about the inputs, but also Kelly gives you the median optimized
  outcome. And there's a whole bunch of outcomes below that that are very bad for you,
  potentially."*
- **Toby — Kelly assumes serial play at a blackjack table.** *"Kelly is conceived as
  something that you play in series, like sitting at a blackjack table where you have kind
  of, you have a better idea of the fixed mathematics of what you're doing. Rather than most
  investments and most bets and most people, we just, we live in a world where there is an
  abundance of opportunity to take bets on and properly understood. Kelly should include all
  of those other bets, which might mean that you have to, you sort of have to size down."*
  The consequence he names: *"you're not putting 40% of you book into a single position."*
- **Dellanna via Harry Crane — you are uncertain about the edge itself.** *"When you're using
  it, it implies that you have an edge... you have uncertainty on the edge. Like you think
  that you have an edge, but you don't know whether you really have it and how big it is.
  And so you need to start betting small so that if you discover that you do not have the
  edge or that the edge is smaller than you think, you discover before you lost too much
  money."*

What survives the criticism, in Toby's words: *"the objective of Kelly is to avoid ruin...
and then it says you want to size your bet to the frequency and magnitude of the opportunity
that you have"* — and bet size tracks **frequency** more closely than magnitude.

## 5. Jake's segment: the 63 % rule

The mechanic: if something has a 1-in-N chance in a single try and you try N times, the
chance it happens **at least once** is about 63 %. `(1 - 1/N)^N = e^-1 = 37 %` is the
complement. His three examples, as given:

| setup | per-try odds | tries | at least once |
|---|---|---|---|
| cold emails to clients | 1 in 10 | 10 | 65 % |
| car accident per trip | 1 in 10,000 | 10,000 (once a day, 27 years) | ~63 % |
| catastrophic trading loss | 1 in 100 per day | 100 trading days | 63 % (avoiding it: 37 %) |

> *"As humans, we round that down to zero."*

The symmetric reading, which is the useful one:

> *"You want to be on the right side of that 63%. You want to have it work for you. And then
> also you want to make sure that you don't give that 1% risk 100 chances of killing you."*

Dellanna's reply is the line to keep: *"You want to make your success inevitable and not
your demise."* And in his own vocabulary: *"your strategy should be inevitable... even when
you fail in an endeavor, you kept building skills, relationship and trust so that you're
almost guaranteed success."*

## 6. Near misses: how to estimate your own rate instead of the base rate

This is the most immediately usable idea in the episode, and it is a measurement method.
A population base rate does not describe you:

> *"Even if it is covered that the average probability is one in X for the average
> population, what happens really is that this driver has one has three times as much as
> that and the other driver may be three times less than that."*

> *"The tip for this to me for me is to look at near misses. So for example, in the case of
> driving, what you want to check is how many times are you checking the phone or how many
> times do you see yourself crossing the red line."*

> *"And this applies to everything in business. How many times are you doing near misses in
> business?... Things which you realize it was a terrible decision, but I got lucky or I
> missed this piece of data or like that's a very good indicator of how long it will happen
> to you before you crash if you don't change your habits."*

## 7. Hindsight gerrymandering

> *"Maybe this is investing this way is risky, but smart investors or people with smart
> strategies make profit with this and I am smart. And therefore, I will also make profits.
> We very often tend to think this way. ... The problem is that we don't know yet if we
> are smart or not."*

> *"It's not because I made money last month that I was [good] at investing. To know whether
> I was [good] at investing it takes 20 years, 30 years, whatever."*

The naming: you *gerrymander* the winning strategy around a trait you have assumed of
yourself — *"you're increasing your expected probability of survival based on something that
doesn't exist."*

Adjacent, from Jake: **non-stationarity.** *"Maybe you [find] a hedge, but the question is
will these hedge remain."* Once a strategy is public, copying neutralises it.

## 8. Alice and Bob, and why the leaderboard lies

Two skiers. Alice: 20 % chance of winning a race, 5 % of breaking a leg. Bob: 10 % and 1 %.
(Dellanna flags his own uncertainty: *"I don't remember the exact numbers."*)

> *"If we measure it in 5 races or less, Alice has the highest numbers of expected wins. And
> if we measure it after with more than 5 races, Bob has the more."*

Two psychological failures follow, and the second is the one worth carrying:

> *"Even if Bob has the best long term strategy, the risk is that after 3 or 4 races, he
> notices that Alice is ahead and his [switches] to Alice's strategy."*

> *"If there are 100 Alice's and 100 Bob's and after 10 races, we only look at the top 10
> [skiers], they will all be Alice's. The problem is that there are a few Alice's at the
> top, then all the Bob's and then all the Alice's at the bottom. But if we only look at the
> top, we think that Alice's strategy is better. However, if you're a Bob, if you're a
> single person, Bob's strategy will be much better for you."*

Carlisle: *"I think you just described 90% of the problem in the investment world."*

**And therefore: do not copy the winners.**

> *"It's not to copy what the people at the top did, because there is an incredible amount of
> luck. Most likely, even if they did something good, you probably don't know what amongst
> the 10 or 20 [things] that they did, which is the one that actually brought them success.
> You will probably not be able to know that. And you will copy the wrong thing. Instead,
> the much better question to ask is, how did the other people that tried fail?"*

## 9. Fat tails: good and great are different jobs

> *"The [more] power driven, the more is the impact of chance. Of course. And then the good
> question is then, how do you expose yourself to chance?"*

His own worked case as an author:

> *"Writing a great book is the prerequisite, but it's definitely not sufficient to get the
> kind of success that I don't know, Stephen King [has]."*

> *"The more the outcome is is [fat] tailed, the more there is a difference from the thing,
> the kind of things you need to go to do to become good, and the things that you need to do
> to become great."*

On winner-take-all races he adds a limit that applies to any "category leader" plan:

> *"Because it's a race with a limited number of winners, that means that you cannot have a
> certainty of winning, because even if you have the best strategy, all it takes is another
> person running the same best strategy, and then it becomes a flip of the coin."*

He prefers relationships and trust: *"those are all compounding assets that become more
valuable when the time passes."*

## 10. What he actually does

- **Newsletter builds trust, sells almost nothing.** *"There is extremely low amount of
  selling."* Book launches get mentioned, that is all.
- **He has published 11 books, on purpose.** *"There is extremely high variance, low control
  of that. If you want to become a successful author, you need to write to write more than one book."*
  A portfolio because the payoff is fat-tailed, not because any one book is weak.
- **He refuses viral tweets.** *"The quickest way to write a viral tweet is to exaggerate or
  misrepresent or playing on the outrage or telling a funny joke. And the problem is that it
  gets you a lot of likes, but it's not the right kind of likes. It's the kind of likes of I
  liked the joke or I trusted the lie, but it's not the kind of like that says I trust the
  author more."*

**The one habit he is asked to name**, lightly de-garbled for grammar only:

> *"[Ask] yourself did I last week [do] at least one thing that is unnecessary in the short
> term, but necessary for to go beyond a certain level in the long term."*

Plus: *"Always ask yourself how did other people with the same goal as mine fail."*

## 11. Luca's razor: a short horizon deletes your options

The mafia example: a man demands one million.

- **Now** — *"Maybe you sell your organs and you take the proceeds and you go to the casino
  and you put them all on the 36 and you hope that you win."*
- **Five years** — *"Maybe you open a startup, you work super hard for five years."*
- **Thirty years** — *"if you are decently smart and work decently hard, you can do it."*

> *"The thing is because of the very short time horizon, you only have bad options."*

> *"So the point is the longer your time horizon, the better options open up to you."*

> *"But if instead you give yourself an artificially short time horizon... I want to become a
> millionaire next year, then you only give yourself bad options."*

Carlisle names the counterweight honestly — the horizon is not always a free choice:
*"When you're younger and poorer, you're more focused on making rent or feeding yourself
when you have no sort of asset cushion."* Dellanna's answer is not "wait": it is to stop
optimising for a single horizon, because **your own horizon has high variance too** (*"maybe
I die next year. Or maybe I live until 100"*):

> *"Every month you do a few things which are only good for the short term. And you do a few
> things which are only good for the long term."*

And on FOMO, with his own adolescent example (no girlfriend at 16, *"I literally did this
for three or five years"*, fixed by lengthening the horizon):

> *"The people who are getting ahead, maybe they will crash. If they're getting ahead in
> ways that's not reproducible. And number two, even if it's true that you're falling behind,
> trying to get back as fast as possible will not help you."*

## 12. On AI, briefly

Asked whether models reason in ensemble averages, he says it depends entirely on the prompt:

> *"I have noticed a super big difference in how AI answers the same question depending on
> how you prompt it. Depending on how much details you give about your goals, about your
> background, your situation, what you're trying to optimize for. And then, of course, if you
> don't specify any of these, it will make some assumptions about you which might be wrong."*

Jake supplies the mechanism — the model is trained on humans, and humans have poor ergodicity
intuition, so the blind spot is inherited. Dellanna:

> *"The default behavior mirrors the above average but still relatively average human. But if
> you prompt them correctly, if you remind them... they can be relatively good, and of course,
> you should never trust them, you should always use a grain of salt."*

Read narrowly, that is a prompt-engineering remark. Read against the rest of the episode it
is the same claim as everywhere else: **the default is the ensemble average, and you have to
name your own situation to get off it.**

## What it says against our own situation

1. **A Kelly reading of our holdings has to be fractional and has to state the uncertainty
   on the edge.** Jens asked for a Kelly reading of the portfolio this morning. This episode gives
   three independent reasons the raw number is wrong — the inputs are single draws and not
   parameters, Kelly optimises the *median* with a live left tail, and it assumes serial play
   rather than a world full of simultaneous bets. Any Kelly output we produce should carry a
   fraction and the sentence about uncertainty on the edge, or it is the confident-formula
   failure he describes.
2. **The near-miss idea is the one to actually build with, and we already half-built it.**
   Every watcher in `~/repos/assistant/tools/` is a near-miss counter: `entwurf-offen`
   (finished, never sent), `inbox-offen` (asked, never answered), `zusage-check` (promised,
   never reported). Each exists because a "got lucky" was noticed once. Dellanna's addition
   is to **count them as a rate**, not just to fix each one — the number of near misses per
   week is the estimate of when the real failure lands. We have the events and have never
   summed them.
3. **A live near miss from today, to make the point concrete.** The 0-byte draft: a `--fix`
   redirect emptied a finished message, both send gates reported green on the empty file, and
   it was caught only because it happened to carry a link. That is exactly *"a terrible
   decision, but I got lucky."* It produced a fix (exit 2 on an empty draft). It has never
   been counted.
4. **Asking how others with the same goal failed beats studying winners — and we keep
   studying winners.** The extension work reads top listings and copies their shape. The question he
   proposes is the inverse and is cheap on the Chrome Web Store: the listings with rank and
   no revenue are visible, and we have three of our own (Jigsaw at rank 2 and 0.00 USD in 187
   days is the cleanest specimen we own).
5. **The portfolio-of-13 has the right shape and the wrong wiring.** His 11 books are a
   fat-tail portfolio: high variance, low control, so write more than one. Our 13 listings
   look identical — except **only 4 can take money**. A bet you cannot collect on is not a
   lottery ticket; it is a ticket you did not buy.
6. **The short-horizon warning lands directly on the stated goal, and it should be named
   rather than argued.** "5k/month within 4 weeks" is the artificially short horizon in his
   sense, and his claim is not that it is too ambitious but that it *removes the good
   options* — the moves available in a four-week window are the casino ones. His actual
   prescription is not to abandon it: run both horizons every month, a few things that only
   pay now and at least one that pays nothing now. Distribution moves on already-built assets
   are the short-horizon half; trust and relationships are the long half, and that half is
   currently empty (no list, no audience, no network).
7. **"Build trust or consume trust" is a channel decision we have not made.** There is no
   newsletter to consume trust with yet. If one is ever built, the 6-month vs 10-year split
   above is the whole design decision, made once, at the start.

# Steven Pinker on Scaling Theory

> **The one sentence that carries the episode:** *"It's the mental state that allows
> coordination to be possible and humans are unusual in the degree to which we
> coordinate our behavior with one another."* [06:35] — Pinker's point is that
> common knowledge is not a logician's toy: it is what lets two people meet for coffee,
> a country drive on one side of the road, and a crowd turn on an emperor, and it
> works because minds treat a signal as *public* rather than computing infinite
> nesting.

The host reads the book explicitly as a scaling theory: a single mind holds only a
few layers of who-knows-what, yet humans coordinate in the millions. Pinker accepts
the frame but keeps pulling the answer back to one mechanism — salience plus the
knowledge that the salience is shared.

## 1. Mutual knowledge vs common knowledge

- The host's framing: *"Nothing was added to the world except the knowledge that the
  knowledge is shared."* [00:28]
- Pinker's definition: common knowledge is *"the state when not only does everyone
  know something but everyone knows that everyone knows knows it"* [04:26], ad
  infinitum.
- Coffee example: it isn't enough to go to the other person's favourite cafe, because
  they might be second-guessing you and going to yours — the regress has no stable
  stopping point without a shared signal [05:13].
- The escape from the regress: common knowledge *"needn't involve actually thinking
  through all those steps. It could involve an awareness that something is public, it
  is out there, it is self-evident."* [05:42]
- Scale range he names: from two people meeting for coffee to driving on the right,
  paper currency (*"because you know other people will accept it because they know
  that other people will accept it"* [06:12]), the words and rules of a language, and
  large organizations.

## 2. Coordination is the neglected half of sociality

- Evolutionary biology spent decades on altruism — helping another at a cost to
  yourself — the puzzle of The Selfish Gene, whose 50th anniversary he notes [08:20].
- The other kind of interaction is mutualism: the oxpecker eating ticks off a zebra
  helps both [08:47]. No altruism puzzle there, but when the behaviour is flexible
  *"it does still involve a difficult conceptual problem and that is the problem of
  coordination"* [09:03].
- The resolution in each case: *"It requires some, in the case of humans, some verbal
  agreement, both humans and other animals, it could depend on some conspicuous public
  signal"* [09:25].

## 3. Global common knowledge in 2026: available, but refused

- Global coordination is still possible. Economists call universally perceived
  unpredictable events "sunspots" [10:43]; viral events can do it too, *"if people
  have reason to believe that it has gone viral"* [11:12].
- Filter bubbles, in his reading, are mostly motivated rather than informational:
  everyone could tune into the same sources, *"But they may choose not to because they
  have lost confidence that there is a single objective source of data."* [11:49]
- *"The ability to create objective knowledge is very precious, very fragile."*
  [11:57] It needs error-correcting mechanisms: falsification in science, editing and
  fact-checking in journalism, the adversarial system in courts, Wikipedia's sourcing
  and open editing [12:07].
- The default it fights: a tendency *"to look at the world as just competing factions,
  each with its own truth"* [13:05].

## 4. LLMs, shared priors, and agents that model deeper

- Schrepel's question: LLMs are trained on largely the same data, so do they share
  Aumann-style priors and converge? Pinker: *"it's too soon to tell"* [14:27], but LLMs
  have been more aligned with objective reality than social media.
- His explanation is the business model: social media optimizes time on platform,
  while AI companies sell to corporations and governments for decisions — *"And if
  those systems were to misdirect users, to do crazy things that are out of touch with
  reality, no one would use them."* [15:44]
- Errors remain, but local rather than systematic: hallucinated references and blended
  facts, not *"big conspiracy theories"* [16:14]. Example: "Grock" [Grok] often exposes
  Musk's own claims when used to fact-check him [16:36].
- Agents modelling far deeper than the human "about four layers" [17:16]: Pinker had not
  considered it. Strict common knowledge needs infinite depth anyway — *"It can't even
  be 10,000 layers."* [17:50] — so practice runs on "almost common knowledge" or common
  belief, e.g. *"0.95% sure instead of 100% sure"* [18:17] [as spoken; presumably 95%].
- A self-referential formula captures the infinity without spelling it out, so extra
  depth *"may not be decisive"* [18:47]. Host's summary: *"infinity is already factored
  into the system"* [19:35].

## 5. Anonymous giving and the CC/BCC experiment

- Maimonides' ladder of charity: the top rung is double-blind giving; the lowest is
  common knowledge, money handed directly to the recipient [20:59].
- Pinker's experiments found the same modern intuition: *"there's no amount of the size
  of the gift that can make up in righteousness for the fact that the giver made his
  identity known"* [22:34].
- The explanation: observers try to read inherent altruism. An anonymous giver *"has no
  possible advantage in calling in the favor"* [23:32]; a public giver may be buying
  esteem or creating indebtedness, so is *"less altruistic, less likable"* [24:08].
- CC vs BCC: correcting a teaching assistant's harsh grade by BCC gives *"Reciprocal
  knowledge, each one knows, but each one doesn't know that the other one knows."*
  [25:55] CC would make the correction common knowledge and cost the TA authority.
- *"common knowledge changes relationships"* [26:09] — like the emperor, whose dignity
  held *"As long as it was private knowledge"* [26:37].
- Evidence status: run once as a hypothetical by a student for a class project, never
  published (no human-subjects approval) [27:15].

## 6. Communal outrage

- What Pinker wants to make common knowledge: the "communal outrage" — an attack in a
  public setting where everyone knows that everyone knows, *"which often triggers an
  outsized reaction"* [30:30].
- The logic: if the affront is not avenged, *"then that lowers my group in the eyes of
  the world"* [30:44] — revenge to keep standing in the dominance hierarchy.
- Credited to "John Tubi" [John Tooby] and to Schelling's The Strategy of Conflict
  [31:14].
- Examples he lists: George Floyd, 9-11, Pearl Harbor, the Rodney King beating in
  1991, the Arab Spring fruit peddler, the "Charlie Curtin" [Charlie Kirk] shooting
  [31:30]–[32:27].
- His stake: understood better, *"we would perhaps not overreact, which is a deep
  tendency in human nature"* [32:39].

# Peter Howitt on Scaling Theory

> **The one sentence that carries the episode:** *"One way to deal with our competition
> is to try to stay ahead by innovating yourself. And another way is to try to prevent
> those ideas from developing."* [20:13] — Howitt's whole argument hangs on this fork.
> Competition makes frontier firms innovate to escape it; but incumbents can also
> escape it by buying, lobbying or blocking, and he thinks that second route is what
> has been draining dynamism in the US and Europe.

The host is an antitrust lawyer, so the creative-destruction half bends toward merger
control; the second half is about Howitt's agent-based simulation work, which he calls
the part of his career that did not land.

## 1. Origin of the model: Schumpeter, made operational

- Howitt (macro) and Aghion (micro) met at MIT in 1987. Aghion had heard of creative
  destruction in an IO course, but *"it was mentioned as just sort of a curiosity"*
  [08:21]; standard growth theory rested on the Solow model, where technical progress
  is exogenous and *"everybody benefits uniformly from technical progress"* [08:40].
- The building blocks came from IO patent-race models: *"When there's competition
  between incumbents and rivals, and the rival lens, incumbent suffers, there's
  creation, something new."* [09:21] ["lens" = wins]
- Timeline as told: *"the paper was pretty much finished in its final form in 1988 and
  didn't get published until 1992."* [13:05]
- Howitt only believed in it after seminars: *"everywhere I went, I got a really
  favorable reception"* [10:11] — from people who had not appreciated his earlier work.

## 2. The inverted U and the escape-competition effect

- Schumpeter was *"right in that sense, but he was incomplete"* [15:15]: too much
  competition kills the rents that justify R&D — *"If you can't earn it up on Opeli
  Profits to justify the R&D expenditure, R&D is going to slow down."* [15:06]
  [= enough monopoly profits]
- What Schumpeter missed: firms *"on the technological frontier themselves, and had to
  innovate in order to stay on that frontier"* [15:41]. Those firms innovate more under
  more competition.
- The empirical result (UK, sample including half of the publicly listed firms): most
  firms sat *"on the part of the curve where the competition, the escape competition
  effect was the dominant one."* [16:33] His verdict: Schumpeter *"captured an
  important effect, but it turns out it's not the most important."* [17:15]
- Hence the book's claim that competition policy and growth policy are the same policy
  on different timescales.

## 3. Preemptive mergers and the EU "innovation shield"

- Asked about the European Commission's 2026 merger guidelines easing acquisitions of
  small innovative firms, Howitt is skeptical: *"One of the ways in which established
  firms can try to escape competition other than by innovating is to engage in
  preemptive mergers."* [18:57]
- The mechanism: buy the threat, then bury it or fold it into the existing path —
  *"blend that innovation into the existing technological trajectories that they're on
  without threatening their core business model."* [19:28] ["blend" probably = bend]
- Why that matters: *"every once in a while you need to have a fundamental change in
  technological trajectories."* [19:47]
- Other escape routes he lists: patent thickets, lobbying, regulatory capture. He ties
  preemptive mergers to rising concentration in *"almost every industry in the United
  States"* [20:47] and Autor's superstar firms.
- He hedges: *"I honestly don't know enough about the details of how they're planning
  to implement the idea."* [21:50]

## 4. Market power becomes political power

- Schumpeter feared voters would resent successful innovators. Howitt's version is the
  reverse: *"when incumbents get strong enough, they end up getting a lot of political
  power as well as a lot of financial power that they can use to retain their dominant
  position"* [23:48]
- His example is fossil fuels and dark money, firms *"worried about becoming the
  victims of creative destruction in the energy sector"* [24:33].
- Elting Morison's three stages (ignore, explain why it cannot work, create fear)
  *"sounds plausible to me"* [26:33].

## 5. Agent-based modeling: regularities instead of theorems

- The goal was to do away with the Walrasian auctioneer and explain coordination
  without assuming equilibrium. Firms are the coordinators: *"you don't just go
  wandering around looking for some of the brochures, you go to a grocery store."*
  [30:03] [brochures = groceries]
- The method: *"we could maybe do enough simulations and repeat them that it would be
  almost like an empirical analysis."* [30:39] And: *"instead of proving theorems, we
  could establish some statistical regularities"* [30:59] about emergent properties.
- In the model, shops, a trading network and money emerge from agents with simple
  rules. He built it *"one summer at a summer cottage teaching myself C++"* [31:43].
- Reception: *"people just don't find it very persuasive."* [32:28] It is cited, *"But
  that's a very small group of people."* [32:39] His explanation is creative
  destruction in academia: people *"defend their own human capital"* [32:44]; ideas
  spread by convincing the young, not by converting the established.
- What he thinks it needs is an aha moment like Schelling's segregation model, which
  *"makes graduate students say, oh, yeah, I want to write a dissertation on this
  subject."* [34:56]
- His complaint about the field: *"A lot of macroeconomics really wastes the power of
  the computer."* [36:30]

## 6. AI agents, and incumbents financing their own disruptors

- On AI agents as economic actors: *"I expect that we will have institutional evolution
  just as we always have and it will definitely be different with agents."* [40:45] He
  adds: *"I'm not sure that we really understand what's going to happen with AI
  agents."* [40:54]
- Amazon already is an algorithmic market that *"has emerged from competition and does
  much what the little shops that Clara and I talked about."* [41:45] [Clara = Clower]
- On the inverted U inside AI: incumbents learned the Kodak lesson. *"they better do it
  to themselves before other firms do it, because they want to survive."* [44:47]
- Finance as the workaround for corporate culture: *"a lot of the finance behind
  anthropic and open AI is coming from Google and Microsoft and others who have a lot
  to lose from winning technology."* [45:21] Building a contradicting department
  in-house is *"Very difficult"* [45:55]; financing an outside firm keeps a stake while
  leaving it *"uninhibited by that and free to develop."* [46:23]
- Net effect, tentatively: *"it seems as if it's going to make the escape competition
  effect even stronger."* [46:57]

## 7. Personal: trial and error, and a complementary partner

- With Aghion: *"Billy was always very quick to say, well let's scrap that. I was
  always the one who said, well let's push it a little further."* [49:14] [Billy =
  Philippe] Neither alone would have got it right.
- On big decisions: *"I think I'm more at trial and error men."* [50:40] He dropped
  other projects for the growth model because it *"sounded interesting"* [51:05], and
  bought a house in Columbus from France after seeing it driving by.

# Xavier Gabaix on Scaling Theory

> **The one sentence that carries the episode:** *"So really the engine of growth is
> the firms that became large and often they can start small."* [27:12] — Gabaix
> treats the giant firm as the normal output of random proportional growth, not a
> defect to be corrected. His one reservation for antitrust is entry:
> *"So what you want antitrust to do is make sure there's enough free entry for small
> firms."* [27:25]

The host is an antitrust law professor, so every statistical result gets pushed
toward enforcement. Gabaix answers as a mathematician-economist: rough approximations,
orders of magnitude, and a refusal to read policy off a single distribution.

## 1. A physicist's view of economics: universality, orders of magnitude

- He came to economics from French math-physics training and was put off by the
  standard models: *"I was horrified by completely incredible the models were
  completely way to assuming way"* [02:59] — *"too much rationality"* [03:06].
- The physicist's habit he brings: *"We always do rough approximations, and it's okay"*
  [03:46], aiming at *"the quantitative essence of things"* [03:58].
- The hook to scaling is universality — near a phase transition matter *"rescaling,
  everything becomes the same"* [04:32], despite *"10 to 23 atoms around"*
  [04:39].
- On biology: metabolic scaling is *"amazing work"* [07:21]; for cities economists
  found similar laws, *"but it's less spectacular"* [07:39] — *"It's all closer to an
  exponent of one everywhere."* [07:48]

## 2. Zipf's law for cities: random growth plus a floor

- The empirical fact (host): *"half the size of the biggest"* [09:11]; Gabaix: *"I think it's very, very correct"* [10:01], with small
  discrepancies like mountains on a round Earth.
- The mechanism: fixed population, each city grows at random, e.g. *"2% or maybe plus 2%
  minus 2.1%"* [12:31]. Without friction the distribution
  degenerates.
- Add a floor: *"let's say less than 15 inhabitants, then it bounces back"* [12:59].
  The steady state is a Pareto distribution; if the friction is small, it has
  *"an exponent of one, which is exactly the"* [14:01] Zipf's law.
- Why it is everywhere: *"That happens under very weak conditions."* [14:26]
- Lower moving costs (the Netherlands vs France) change volatility, not the shape:
  *"It doesn't depend on the absolute scale of volatility."* [15:50]

## 3. CEO pay scales with firm size

- Model (with Landier, 2008; update with Sauvagnat): best CEO matched to largest
  firm, frictionless market. It covers all top performers — *"Top coders would be part
  of it"* [17:18].
- When firm size rose about sixfold (1980 to 2000, as he recalls it), pay followed:
  *"the ecosystem wage of all the CEOs is going to be multiplied by six"* [20:02].
- The Tim Cook arithmetic: 1% of a *"$1 trillion"* [20:23] market cap is *"$10
  billion"* [20:37]; his pay of *"$100 million"* [20:57] implies an improvement of
  *"only 0.01%, which is really small"* [21:02].
- Cross-section: pay is proportional to firm size *"to some power, which empirically
  is about 1.0%"* [21:38] [ASR garble, probably one third; Gabaix–Landier report about 1/3 — this figure is from the paper, not the episode].
- He says it still holds after the update, *"maybe slightly less well"* [16:49].

## 4. Concentration as the baseline: what it means for antitrust

- Firm sizes follow Zipf's law *"almost exactly"* [23:38]; the explanation is the same
  random growth. Big firms are firms with a run of large productivity gains — Walmart
  is large because *"they're really good at shipping efficiently"* [25:34]; failed bets
  such as Vision Pro count as negative productivity shocks.
- Large firms are where growth comes from; Japan and Korea took off partly through
  Samsung and Sony *"accumulating TFP growth"* [27:05].
- Policy he draws: low entry burden, *"for large firms, you know, light touch"*
  [27:42]; act on conduct (collusion, the Microsoft browser case, non-competes), not
  on structure.
- Zipf is probabilistic, not a target: a merger of firms two and three is no reason
  to block — *"that would be a very wrong use of zip-flow type"* [33:17]
  [Zipf's-law-type reasoning]. The worry is *"local monopolies that would stifle
  competition"* [32:28].
- A lone giant over a dust of small firms for decades is rare; his example is an
  economy where the army owns everything — a product of government, not of markets.

## 5. Random growth beats increasing returns and lock-in

- Against Brian Arthur's lock-in view as the main force: *"it would make a large number
  of predictions and I think almost all"* [36:18] *"of them are wrong."* [36:24]
- Gibrat's law is the test: big and small units grow at the same mean rate with the
  same dispersion; lock-in would make *"a much bigger growth rate than the smaller
  firms and you really don't see that empirically"* [36:56].
- Podcasts are his live example: *"for podcasts, you have something very much like a
  power low and a zip slow"* [38:30] [power law and a Zipf's law]. Even Joe Rogan
  *"doesn't really grow at"* [39:18] *"a much higher rate than everybody else."* [39:23]
  It is *"just about the random growth of productivity or maybe attractiveness"*
  [39:30].
- Churn is the other evidence: *"IBM was number one, now it's definitely not"* [40:08].

## 6. The granular hypothesis: a few firms move GDP

- Standard objection: with a million firms, idiosyncratic shocks average out at
  *"the square root of one of one million, which is extremely small."* [44:16]
- The claim: *"a sizable fraction of economic"* [44:23] *"fluctuations is due to
  idiosyncratic shocks to large firms."* [44:29] Examples: GM's new car or GM's strike
  in the 1960s.
- The check is order-of-magnitude: large firms with sales volatility of *"plus or minus
  10%"* [46:10] should create GDP volatility of *"one or two or three percent a year"*
  [46:37]. Tracing shocks to the top 20 firms, granular shocks explain *"maybe a firm"* [47:11]
  *"to have for GDP volatility"* [47:18] [a third to a half — ASR garble, reading
  uncertain].
- Network centrality (Thurner, Acemoglu et al.) is a sub-case: *"It's about the size of
  your total sales, which is exactly the sufficient statistics"* [48:52].

## 7. Complexity of decisions, and what AI does to it

- With Graeber he measures what makes a decision complex. The most harmful ordinary
  one: *"the choice of your professional life"* [52:13] — major and job — chosen
  without thinking through wages and employment, costing *"around 30% less"* [53:54].
- AI lowers complexity of old problems but not the aversion: *"so the version will still remain."* [57:22]
  [the aversion] *"It's just the complexity will fall."* [57:27]
- It also adds new uncertainty to career choice: *"there's an enormous amount of
  volatility"* [58:24].
- The host objects that AI can also multiply options. Gabaix: users must learn
  metacognition — *"first, give me the gist five lines"* [60:48], then the long
  version.
- Open questions need data, not only intelligence: AI is cracking math because
  *"you don't need empirical data"* [62:10]; in economics *"more and quicker data"*
  [62:07]; *"by accumulating tons of new data, that's"* [63:33] *"the most
  plausible path."* [63:41]

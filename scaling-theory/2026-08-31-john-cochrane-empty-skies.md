## 1. Growth swamps every other question

- The host's opening thesis: growth *"is what happens when a large number of attempts"*
  [01:06] *"are made and a few of them work."* [01:14]
- Cochrane's own number: *"2% versus 3.5%, the economy is twice as big in 60 years."*
  [06:42] Against that: *"The economic cost of climate change is five, maybe 10% of output in 100 years."* [06:51]
- The cause is old and uncomfortable: *"we've known the answer since Adam Smith, get the hell out of"* [07:18] the way.
  Growth *"is embodied usually in new companies that send"* [07:30] old companies out to pasture.
- *"And 99% of what policy does is to get the way of that project."* [07:43] Later: *"But 90% of it is the get out of the way."* [09:44]
- Politics is about distribution, not growth: *"What government does is take from A and gift B."* [04:33]
  His speculative reason: for millennia *"All wealth came from forcible redistribution."* [05:21]
- He rejects the word "policy makers": politicians *"deliver protections for people who demand protections."* [08:58]
- Examples of foregone growth: *"not allowing nuclear"* [07:49] *"power for 40 years has been a disaster."* [07:53]
  China *"going from about"* [09:14] *"$200 a person per year to $20,000 a person per year"* [09:20].
- On AI hype: *"We'll repeat the whole Industrial Revolution in three years and I go, we should be so lucky."* [04:13]

## 2. Function, not size — finance and antitrust

- Paper: *Finance: Function Matters, Not Size* (Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2013).
- Old antipathy to middlemen *"is from an economic point of view completely"* [14:11] wrong.
- Fees are paid voluntarily; his own money sits in *"zero fee passive investments."* [16:50] *"You don't want to pay fees?"* [16:52] *"You don't have to pay fees."* [16:53]
- Durable monopolies need the state: *"there really is no such thing as a durable monopoly other than that, which"* [15:11] is enforced by government.
  Illustration: *"Yahoo, AOL, Time Warner and Netscape have this thing all wrapped up, don't they?"* [15:21]
- Merger control as political lever: *"Come bow and bend the knee to me and thou shall get your permission."* [15:54]
- The standard test he wants applied: find the market failure first. *"90% of the time, the reason there's a market failure is the government forces the market"* [17:41] to fail.

## 3. Regulation as practised, and a retrospective sunset

- The Chicago critique (Stigler, Buchanan): look at actual regulations. What you find is
  *"businesses get together with regulators and say, we love regulation"* [20:06] *"because that stops the competition."* [20:11]
- Ex-ante cost-benefit analysis is guesswork: *"nobody has any idea what the cost is."* [22:17]
- His proposal: *"a sunset to every regulation where you retrospectively have to show cost benefit analysis."* [22:26]
  *"You have to show did it work as we intended?"* [22:32]
- He endorses the host's (unpublished) idea of forcing regulators to show by experiment that an intervention beats the counterfactual: *"Absolutely."* [18:59]

## 4. Permission vs right — fracking and the $10 veto

- The host notes *"the stock of rules only ever goes up."* [23:29] Cochrane's answer is the default rule: *"is the ground rule that you must ask permission for everything or that you"* [24:15] have the right to do things.
- Fracking: *"in the US subsurface, minerals are the right of the property owner."* [25:50] *"And the default is you can't stop me."* [25:55]
  *"And in Europe, you have to ask permission."* [26:05] *"So you can just say no."* [26:07]
- The veto asymmetry: a neighbour's project worth a million vs *"$10 of shade"* [26:31] on tomato plants. In a market *"he pays me $20, and we're both good with it."* [26:40]
  Without that right, *"in order to benefit me $10, I can stop a million"* [26:51] dollars of project.
- Why grown firms switch camps (host's framing): AI firms have spent on hardware and software *"going to be completely worthless in three years."* [29:54]
  Cochrane's cynical reading: *"you need the government regulating competition, certifying new models"* [30:10].

## 5. Empty skies — learning by crashing vs asking first

- Counterfactual: *"imagine if Orville and Wilbur Wright had to go to the FAA and ask permission"* [28:30]. Result: *"Never get anywhere."* [28:50]
- How flight became safe: *"Well, they flew."* [28:56] *"They crashed."* [28:57] *"People died."* [28:58] Each crash, a regulatory body *"learned from experience."* [29:05]
- He offers that as *"a good model for AI regulation."* [29:09] Foreseeing every danger first *"that's just never going"* [29:12] to happen.
- Light aviation today: the FAA *"has so regulated the safety of like planes as"* [31:10] [light planes] to almost kill the business. *"We're still flying around in engines from the 1950s"* [31:36].
- Innovation re-entered through exemptions: *"the crack in the system of home built airplanes"* [31:51], and engines on hang gliders that *"turned"* into ultralights. *"The weeds are growing but it's taken decades and decades."* [32:06]
- Priorities: pilot licensing could be private, but *"on the list of the"* [32:42] *"dumpster fires of our government maybe we can leave that one."* [32:47]

## 6. Universities: scale was good, capture was not

- Conformity did not come from scale: *"Harvard, MIT, Stanford, Yale, they never scaled."* [34:17] State universities did.
- Growth from matching: *"A good part of our growth has not been just great ideas, but getting education to smart"* [34:34] people.
- The dysfunction came with federal student loans that could not distinguish degrees; the larger problem is ideological capture, *"not really related to scale"* [37:25].
- Minimum viable university: unknown. Remove subsidies and nonprofit protection, *"let them compete, we'll find out what works."* [39:43]
  Predictions of obsolescence (online classes, textbooks, now AI) keep failing: *"the social force of showing up to class every week"* [39:08].

## 7. Peer review, AI referees, and writing for the training data

- *"It is not true that big names have an easier time publishing."* [42:07]
- Reviewing is shallow: *"equation one is wrong in this paper."* [42:48] *"You can get published anyway"* [42:50].
- AI as floor: *"The AI is pretty darn good at not missing obvious things."* [45:03] *"AI report is better than a bad report from my referee who wasn't thinking about it."* [45:11]
- AI as ceiling: after *"multiple rounds of Claude and Refine and Referee"* [45:18], a Chicago colleague *"just destroyed"* [45:24] *"three parts of my paper that Claude hadn't even noticed."* [45:29]
- On being read by AI: *"I want to make sure that I'm in the training data."* [48:17] His only known method: *"just make sure everything's up on the web in a publicly visible format."* [48:24]
- *"AI is pros is deadly boring."* [48:00] [AI prose] — so human writing and reputation still count: *"your reputation with humans is what counts."* [47:49]
- Substack vs journals as complements: *"a trade off also between depth and breadth and between durability and accessibility."* [49:10]
  *"I do think people read my academic work a little more than they would otherwise because I do"* [49:36] popular work.
- *"99% of normal press science is this process of digesting, simplifying and thinking that's"* [50:18] very valuable, but unpublishable in top journals.

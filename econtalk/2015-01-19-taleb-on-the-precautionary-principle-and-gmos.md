# Taleb on the Precautionary Principle and GMOs

> **"There's some water on the floor. Do you drink from it? Would you drink from it? No. Why do
> you not drink from water on the floor, if you are thirsty, you are very careful. But you have
> no evidence that it's poisonous."**
> That is the whole precautionary principle, and it is the reason this episode is worth reading
> closely rather than quoting from memory. Everything Taleb argues about GMOs follows from a
> decision rule most people already apply daily and then abandon the moment someone says the
> word *science*. This is the one hour in the EconTalk Taleb arc that is technical rather than
> aphoristic: it walks through a specific paper — with Rupert Read, Raphael Douady, Joseph
> Norman and Yaneer Bar-Yam — and the argument has moving parts that are easy to misquote. It
> is also the hour where the limits are stated out loud: he refuses to apply the principle to
> nuclear power, and refuses the "expert of experts" label. Getting this one wrong
> misrepresents him, so the quotations below are verbatim and the seams are marked.

## 1. 0:33 — the principle, in a puddle

Roberts opens with the paper and its four co-authors, and Taleb's first move is to warn about
the conversation itself: **"Perhaps we think too much alike, and it may be a problem from a
scientific standpoint but it's always a pleasure."** Roberts's reply is both the best joke in
the hour and a methodological point: **"Well, it looks like two data points; it may only be
one."** It is the fairest description of what this episode is.

Then the puddle. You do not drink water off the floor, you have no evidence it is poisonous,
and you decline anyway. **"So you are making a decision without evidence. This is the exercise
of the precautionary principle in your daily life."** The formal version follows:

> **"the definition of the precautionary principle is on decision making, what should be
> accepted or rejected in situations for which you do not have enough evidence or you do not
> have evidence yet."**

Roberts supplies the paper's term — *non-evidentiary problems* — and Taleb adds why it is not a
marginal case: **"most of the risks we are taking are of non-evidentiary nature, in the sense
that the evidence comes always too late."** The obstacle is what Hayek called scientism, "this
idea of using mechanistic tools from science to make claims and techniques", which has "blinded
people to this sort of reasoning." And the sentence that justifies the method — the asymmetry,
not the caution:

> **"That is, effectively more rigorous than science because you have an asymmetry: you may die
> if you are wrong; and if you are right,"** [?] very [?]
> — the tail of that sentence is lost to the transcription

## 2. 0:33 continued — three layers of knowledge, and why the expert is the wrong expert

Roberts notes that the paper says experts matter but you must pick the right kind. The answer
is a hierarchy, and getting it right is most of the argument:

| Layer | Competence | What it cannot tell you |
|---|---|---|
| **The domain scientist** (biologist, neurobiologist) | the process, the mechanism | whether the *evidence* supports the claim |
| **The statistician** | whether the claim clears the evidentiary bar | what happens when the claim is wrong |
| **The risk analyst** | the left tail, ruin | nothing about the body of the distribution |

His words for the layer break: **"knowing biology doesn't mean you understand the evidence."**
And one level up: **"understanding statistical evidence, doesn't mean you understand
statistical risk."**

The worked example is a real statistical error, stated precisely: testing whether a is better
than b is often done as "a test of significance of a and the significance of b, and without
testing the significance of the difference between a and b" — and **"more than half of papers
in top journals in neurobiology make that mistake."** (The transcript's first attempt at the
number garbles into "more than 53% of the time"; the clean sentence follows.) Which is why
"there's a cop called a statistician on top of them."

Then the epistemological core, stated as a rule about what a statistician is *allowed* to
say:

> **"no statistician would ever let you say 'I have evidence that'--this is again the Black
> Swan problem. A statistician would only let you say, 'I failed to reject the null at x%
> confidence."**

That is the technical form of *absence of evidence is not evidence of absence*. Note what it
does: it does not claim GMOs are harmful. It claims that *we have evidence they are safe* is
not a sentence anyone is entitled to utter. Roberts teases — so Nassim is the expert of
experts — and gets a refusal that bounds the claim: **"No, no, I'm not an expert on experts.
Our job is the left tail, which is a sub-specialty."** On benefits and on the body of the
distribution, "we have no specialty; or we may understand some things but we don't rank
higher."

## 3. 5:46 — the Carpenter Fallacy

The generalisation of the layer argument gets a name. If you want to know the risk of ruin over
a sequence of bets, would you ask the carpenter who built the roulette table, or a probability
person? The carpenter may protest that he knows exactly how it is built — **"But his knowledge
of the carpentry involved in building the roulette table doesn't allow him to make claims as to
the probability distribution of what is going to happen."**

That is the entire structure of his objection to pro-GMO biologists, and it is a claim about
*jurisdiction*, not competence. Science, he says, is for verifiable and generalisable claims
about a process: "it doesn't make claims about risk." Hence:

> **"Science never really talk about left tails. Only journalists think science talks about
> that--or bad scientists. And then you need a cop for that."**

## 4. 12:12 — harm is not ruin

Roberts states the distinction he took from the paper, and it is the cleanest formulation in
the episode: a neighbourhood poker game where some nights you lose a dollar is *harm*; being
wiped out is *ruin*; and in the case of the globe, ruin means extinction. Taleb accepts it and
adds the technical consequence: **"Our job is ruin; completely different dynamics. And for many
probability distributions, there is a complete decoupling between variation and ruin."**

The historical proof of that decoupling is the Great Moderation. **"if I received a Mexican
peso for every time someone mentioned the Great Moderation to me, that the world is becoming a
lot safer because it's less volatile, I would probably own a big strip of land in northern
Mexico."** Then the crisis came, and it was not a regime change — it was people "making claims
concerning tail events from observations about the distribution", which for fat-tailed classes
cannot be done at all. **"So the risk can increase while at the same time variation can get
smaller."** He names Bernanke as the case: not trained enough in fat tails to see the risk.

Then something rare on this show: **Taleb credits Roberts for one of his own images and admits
he stopped attributing it.** "let me steal a method[?] you gave me; actually I've used it before
and I gave you credit the first couple of times and I stopped giving you credit." The image:

> **"if a plane crashes, it's a tragedy because it will kill the people on the plane and it's a
> great loss--very bad news. But a plane crash will not kill every single person who ever took
> a plane before. Whereas in some domains, such as finance, for example, banks can lose in a
> single quarter every single penny they ever made before."**

That is the systemic/local distinction in one sentence, and it is the hinge of the GMO
argument. Aviation risk is local and additive; banking risk is absorbing. His extension:
finance is "a lot worse, but that's only money", and ecology is "vastly worse, because this is
not renewable."

## 5. 15:45 — why fat tails, specifically

Roberts asks the amateur's honest question: if I have thin tails, ruin is merely very unlikely —
still possible. So why do fat tails matter so much? Three answers. **You cannot see the risk**:
variation is compressed, "so you don't notice that the risk is present." **Evidence accumulates
too slowly**: "If you go to Las Vegas and are there for 3 days, you pretty much understand
everything" — thin tails are tractable and the law of large numbers operates quickly, whereas
"For fat tails, you need a lot more data to know what's going on." And **the event, when it
comes, is unbounded**: "the consequences of the event can be monstrous."

And then the observation the paper turns on: ecology *is* fat-tailed, but its crises have not
been systemic, "because we have isolations." Roberts supplies the image and Taleb confirms it —
a forest fire is extremely destructive, but oceans, rivers, mountains and natural firebreaks
keep it from becoming global. What Taleb worries about is anything that crosses those
barriers.

> **"nature seems to have survived thanks to a mechanism by which capacities stay relatively
> local. So things don't spread."**

The counter-case is us: economic life had the same isolation "of course until globalization; so
what happened, a crisis now took place on the planet in 2008 and there is no place to hide."
And the biological equivalent is already eroding — island separation produced diversity
("diversity is much higher in [?] square meter on an island than it is on a continent"), and
"we're losing it."

## 6. 15:45 continued — a risk of ruin has to be zero, not small

This is the technical heart of the episode and the part most summaries flatten. The claim is
not that small probabilities are underestimated. It is that a *repeated* exposure to ruin has
no small version:

> **"Many people understand that there is a risk of ruin, and it could be very small, and
> sometimes we've got to take it. Many people understand that. But few understand that risk
> needs to be zero. Not small."**

The mechanism is the sequence, not the single bet. **"If you take a risk, say with Russian
roulette, a risk of ruin, and survive, what would you do next? You may take it again. So many
risks that are very, very small, because you've survived them, lead to 100% risk of ruin."**
With real people in it: **"mountain climbers have a very small probability of dying in any
given episode... So eventually their life expectancies are going to be much shorter."** The
name for the property: **"ruin is not a renewable resource."**

*(This is the argument later formalised as non-ergodicity. The word does not appear anywhere in
this transcript — the reasoning is entirely here, the vocabulary is not.)*

Roberts contributes the two illustrations that make it stick. The **Turkey Problem**, credited
to Bertrand Russell: every day the farmer feeds the turkey, and every day the turkey gets
"additional and new evidence that it's safe" — until Thanksgiving. And **Value at Risk**: a
friend objects that everyone knows VaR is dangerous, to which Roberts answers that knowing it
in theory does not survive years of the tool working — "you are very likely to start thinking
'I've got this licked.'"

The exception that proves the rule is insurance. An insurer runs a *reservoir*: premiums flow
in, it is drawn down but not to zero, and the risk taken is calibrated to the inflow — the
tradition Taleb traces to Cramer [probably Harald Cramér — the transcript's own editorial
note]. **"In insurance you can do that. In ecology, and many domains, you cannot do that,
because the reservoir is not being filled. We are just wasting risk."**

And the boundary of his own objection — the answer to everyone who reads him as anti-risk:
**"So I have no problem of people taking risk so long as everyone stays local"** [?] "doesn't
[?] the whole human race."

## 7. 23:19 — bottom-up is thin-tailed, top-down is fat-tailed

Roberts asks about the paper's contrast: local, bottom-up events produce thin tails; global,
connected, top-down events produce fat tails. Taleb answers through Hayek, and his reason for
Hayek's position is not the usual one:

> **"Why did Hayek want distributed knowledge in society, nothing, no monopoly of knowledge by
> anyone? Because he wants the errors to be distributed."**

Not because distributed knowledge is more accurate — because distributed *error* is survivable.
Plus the second-order point he calls another profundity: **"he thinks we cannot forecast--the
mind cannot foresee its own advance... we can't forecast how we are going to forecast in the
future."** So the objection to a central planner is double: arrogant claims that may harm us,
and mistakes he cannot foresee and which "will be large."

He insists on Popper/Hayek together, notes Roberts's addition that this is Adam Smith's man of
system, and then delivers the irony that gives the section its teeth. The mathematics of
unpredictability in complex systems was developed largely **by the Soviets** — the tradition
that produced Yakov Sinai, an Abel Prize winner and "probably the most crowned mathematician
alive today". Their result: **"in a complex system you can't predict."** Financed by a social
planner. **"But nobody saw the contradiction. That if they are right, then they should have no
Soviet system."** He does not let the laugh stand: the real mistake is "not realizing that an
idea developed in one domain can apply to another one, while accepting that these two domains
have same operating mechanisms." Hayek's distributed knowledge is, in his reading, a claim
about *nature*, not just about markets.

Roberts extends it to macroeconomics and epidemiology — the paper claiming an intervention is
good for the economy is like the study claiming coffee is good for you — and **here Taleb
corrects him rather than agreeing**: **"I would say--it's benign to say coffee is good or bad
for you. But it is a benign claim. And some such claims can be rigorous."** The criterion is
never *is an expert overclaiming*. It is *does being wrong reach ruin*. Then: "let's say now, a
Soviet planner, one that comes to nature. Aha--GMOs."

## 8. 29:09 — what GMOs actually do differently: they skip steps

The positive account of how nature gets from A to B: **"it's by small tinkering, where mistakes
are kept small and local."** You cannot foresee interactions in a complex system without
experimenting. Such systems look opaque from a planner's standpoint but are "very
understandable if we look at them from the perspective of a complex system that has
evolutionary attributes" — you put things together, let them interact, and **"if the system
doesn't blow up, then it's a good system. If it blows up, then it's a bad system."** Time does
the work; the system cleans itself. He gives the layering from *Antifragile* — a fragile layer
at the bottom, then individuals, families, society, humanity, with "evolutionary mechanisms at
all levels of the hierarchies" — and credits the word: tinkering comes from **bricolage**,
"from the famous Monod and Jacob papers, two French people who got the Nobel in the 1960s."

And then the actual distinction, the sentence most GMO summaries of Taleb get wrong. He does
**not** claim a GMO tomato is chemically different from a bred tomato:

> **"A tomato, okay, there's a GMO tomato made according to the FDA (Food and Drug
> Administration) will be the same as a tomato--the same organically through natural
> mechanisms--or human breeding, even. But these steps are not the same as skipping zillions of
> steps to get to a tomato."**

**We are skipping steps.** The object may be equivalent; the *path* is not, and the path is
where the error-correction lives. "We don't know in the soil what's going to do to other
plants. We don't know what it's going to do to you. We have a lot of unknowns. So, when you
have a lot of unknowns like that, you put the precautionary principle until further notice."

That is also the answer to the standard rejoinder that humans have modified crops for
millennia: selective breeding is small tinkering with local, slow, correctable mistakes; a
transgenic organism is the same destination reached by skipping the process that would have
caught the errors.

## 9. 32:00 — the evidence fight, and the inversion

Roberts puts the industry's case properly, via his interview with Greg Page, the former CEO of
Cargill: people are eating tomatoes with a fish gene and are not dying, so where is the risk of
mass extinction? *So, what's the scientific evidence?* Taleb refuses the question's frame, and
the refusal is the paper's central move:

> **"No, no, that's exactly what we want to avoid, having to talk about scientific evidence
> when the burden of the proof is on the GMO people to show us that they understand anything
> remotely about the tail risk."**
>
> **"The tail risk is not someone dying from eating the tomato. That's not a big risk. No.
> That's not a systemic risk."**

The systemic risk is two things compounding: "a combination of monopoly of some plants over
others, that it's too large a system; and of course creation of other species that will
themselves also be too powerful" — imbalances in nature that can produce large deviations. His
complaint is not that the answer is wrong but that the question has not been asked: "we haven't
seen any paper looking at the risk from that standpoint."

On the demand for evidence, the historical rebuttal: **"what was the evidence that smoking
could cause cancer? What was the evidence that lobotomy was bad?"** — plus a list of withdrawn
drugs. **"Evidence showed up late. Sometimes--even in one case across a generation."** And the
statistical form of it again: **"No statistician would put his stamp"** [?] "that we have
evidence, that it is safe. They tell you failure to reject the null at this percentage."

## 10. 32:00 continued — where the principle does NOT apply

Roberts raises the obvious danger: **"if you are not careful, you can invoke that for lots of
things."** This is the most important exchange in the episode, because Taleb's answer is a
limit, and the limit is what makes the principle a criterion rather than a mood:

> **"So we are not invoking--for the nuclear, we cannot invoke the precautionary principle for
> the nuclear. Why? Because the nuclear will stay local. It doesn't mean it's not risky. You may
> want to ban nuclear, for risk purpose; but the nuclear, you cannot have, a Fukushima cannot
> lead to destruction in India."**

Read that twice if you have ever seen him summarised as *precaution about everything*. Nuclear
power may well be worth banning on ordinary risk grounds — a different argument, made with
different tools. The precautionary principle does not reach it, because a Fukushima does not
propagate. The GMO case differs **only** by propagation: "if you have now the same crops
invading the whole planet, it's too much." — **"Having GMOs on an island is one thing, and
generalizing"** [?] "to the planet in the name of science is another thing."

So the criterion is not *novel technology* and not *unknown mechanism*. It is: **can the
failure mode cross the barriers?** Scale is the risk; the gene is not. He closes with the
analogy he watched happen: people with "a zillion Ph.Ds." employed by Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac
or Morgan Stanley in 2007 saying "there is zero probability of a fail in that".

## 11. 38:12 — the burden of proof runs the other way

Roberts presses the hardest question in the episode, and it deserves credit for being the right
one: *where is the evidence that this GMO process is a fat-tailed process rather than a
thing-tailed [sic] process?*

Taleb's answer inverts it: **"look the other way and say, 'What is the evidence that we are in
a thin-tailed domain?'"** Thin-tailedness is the special case that has to be earned, and nature
grants it only within a species — human height follows a Gaussian. **"But nature doesn't deliver
thin tails between species. Look at an elephant versus a mouse."** Or a mammoth and a
bacterium. So nature is "effectively largely fat-tailed", defined rigorously in the paper as
the **sub-exponential class**.

Which raises the question the rest of the argument depends on: if nature is fat-tailed, why has
it not blown up? The answer is the mechanism, and it is the design principle:

> **"Because effectively you have circuit breakers that transform the fat tails into what we
> call 'modified thin tails.'"**

Islands, continents, oceans. Roberts states the consequence — there can be an extinction on an
island, one on a continent, so there is ruin, but *local* ruin — and Taleb agrees twice. The
lesson is therefore not to avoid fat tails, which is impossible because that is what nature is,
but to **keep the compartments**; hence the policy default, **"it's much healthier for people
to default to fat tails"**, demanding evidence for thin-tailedness rather than the reverse. The
mathematical backing is a chapter of *Silent Risk*, of which he says there is **"nothing in
there that's not said verbally."**

## 12. 38:12 continued — the Masquerade Problem, and then the tape runs out

The last complete argument on the page is the epistemic asymmetry between the two claims:

> **"you can always say the process is not thin-tailed; you cannot with any confidence say the
> process is not fat-tailed. Just from observation. Simply because fat-tailed processes can
> masquerade as thin tails. And that's a Turkey Problem."**

One of those two statements is falsifiable by a single observation; the other is not falsifiable
at all. That asymmetry, not any claim about genes, is what carries the paper.

Roberts then supplies the beginner's definition — thin tails means the probability of remote
events is vanishingly small, fat tails means small but not zero — and Taleb redirects it: **"In
other words, view it as not in probabilities but in consequences."** Weigh a thousand random
people, add the largest human being on the planet, and that person is under .3% of the total.
Do the same with wealth — 7 billion people, 3 or 4 billion very poor, a sample net worth of
maybe a million and a half — then add the richest person on the planet at $75 billion, "and
look what happens. There will be a rounding error." Hence **"fat tailed is how much the rare
event contributes to the total"**. Roberts jokes that Warren Buffett may be a listener — and the
transcript ends there, mid-thread, on `[more to come, 44:02]`.

## Where the two men actually diverge

Worth stating plainly, because both of them flag the risk at the top: Taleb says "Perhaps we
think too much alike, and it may be a problem from a scientific standpoint", and Roberts
answers "it looks like two data points; it may only be one." In the portion that survives,
that self-assessment is broadly accurate. The friction points are these, and they are small:

- **Roberts calls him the expert of experts; Taleb refuses the promotion.** "I'm not an expert
  on experts. Our job is the left tail, which is a sub-specialty." A limit on the paper,
  volunteered rather than extracted.
- **Roberts offers Schumpeter; Taleb declines the reference.** "I don't know about Schumpeter,
  but I know about the real mathematicians who worked on these problems and dynamical systems."
  A small moment, but it shows what he thinks the argument rests on: dynamical systems, not
  economics.
- **Roberts equates macro claims with epidemiological ones; Taleb corrects him.** This is the
  substantive one. Roberts's analogy would license precaution against any overclaiming expert;
  Taleb pulls it back — a coffee claim "is a benign claim. And some such claims can be
  rigorous." The trigger is ruin, not expert hubris: the principle is not scepticism about
  science, it is a rule about absorbing barriers.
- **Roberts twice asks for the evidence; Taleb twice refuses the frame** and inverts the burden
  of proof. Roberts does not push back on the inversion in the surviving text, and whether he
  does later is unknowable — the page stops at 44:02, and the closest thing to a hostile
  question in the excerpt is the Cargill CEO's position, which Roberts relays rather than
  defends.

Two things neither man says: the burden-of-proof inversion is powerful *and* not
self-limiting — the only thing stopping it applying to everything is the locality criterion,
which is why the nuclear exclusion carries so much weight. And the argument is entirely
structural: nothing in it depends on a single piece of biological evidence about GMOs, which is
its strength as logic and its weakness as persuasion.

## Synthesis: the criterion, in the order it has to be applied

The paper's argument, reassembled as a decision procedure:

1. **Ask what is at stake: harm or ruin?** Harm is variation — losses that can be repaid out of
   a reservoir. Ruin is absorbing. Only the second is the precautionary principle's business,
   and "there is a complete decoupling between variation and ruin."
2. **If ruin is possible, ask whether the exposure repeats.** A single small probability is not
   the object. A repeated small probability converges on certainty, because "ruin is not a
   renewable resource" — survivors take the bet again.
3. **Ask whether the failure stays local.** This is the whole test, and it is what separates
   nuclear (contained: a Fukushima cannot destroy India) from a crop planted across a planet.
   Nature is fat-tailed but survives, because circuit breakers turn fat tails into "modified
   thin tails". Anything that dissolves a barrier converts local ruin into global ruin.
4. **Only then look at evidence — and know which way the burden runs.** "What is the evidence
   that we are in a thin-tailed domain?" Thin-tailedness is the claim that must be established.
   And no statistician can certify safety; the strongest sentence available is failure to
   reject the null.
5. **Distrust the domain expert on tail risk specifically.** Carpenter Fallacy: the man who
   built the roulette table cannot tell you the probability of ruin over a long sequence.

The thread through all five is **connectivity, not novelty**. Taleb never argues that GMOs are
unnatural or that a modified tomato is poisonous — he says the opposite about the tomato. He
argues that the process skips the steps in which errors get caught, and that the result is
deployed at a scale which removes the barrier that has kept every previous biological error
local. The same argument licenses a GMO on an island and forbids globalising a crop; applied to
finance, it is the 2008 story he had already told.

## Solo-builder read (insights for Jens)

- **The three layers are a hiring and outsourcing rule.** The person who builds the thing is
  not qualified to price what happens when it fails — the Carpenter Fallacy applied to my own
  work. When I ship an agent that touches money, mail or a customer-facing surface, "what does
  the builder think the risk is?" is the wrong question, including when the builder is me.
- **"Risk of ruin needs to be zero, not small" is the only rule that survives automation.** A
  daemon that runs every five minutes converts a small per-run probability into near-certainty
  over a year. Anything where one bad run is unrecoverable — a delete, a send, a push to a live
  funnel, a credential rotation — must be structurally impossible, not merely unlikely. The
  mountain-climber sentence is the one to keep on hand: the per-episode probability was always
  small; it was the repetition that killed them.
- **Compartments beat correctness.** Nature is fat-tailed and survives on circuit breakers, not
  on being right. Translated: separate credentials per system, separate machines for build and
  production, a business whose income streams cannot fail together, and no single account whose
  loss takes everything with it. The engineering goal is not "no failure" but "no failure that
  crosses a barrier".
- **The insurance/reservoir distinction tells me which risks I may take repeatedly.** A risk is
  repeatable only if something refills the reservoir between attempts. Cash flow refills one;
  reputation with a small client base does not; a platform account with no appeal process does
  not.
- **The burden-of-proof inversion is a good habit and a dangerous one, and the locality
  criterion is what keeps it honest.** "What is the evidence this is safe?" can be used to
  refuse anything, which is how a builder talks himself out of shipping. The discipline is
  Taleb's own: apply the inversion only where the failure propagates. For everything local and
  reversible — a landing page, a price test, a new channel — the default should be the opposite:
  ship and observe.
- **"Skipping steps" is a real description of what AI-assisted building does.** The output can
  be indistinguishable from the hand-built version while the path that would have caught the
  errors is missing — no incremental testing, no accumulated small failures, no local
  correction. Not an argument against generating code; an argument for putting the
  error-correction back in explicitly. Tests first exist for exactly this reason.
- **And the disclosure norm to copy: he refused the "expert of experts" label mid-compliment.**
  Naming the boundary of your own competence in the moment someone hands you more authority
  than you have is cheap to do and almost never done.

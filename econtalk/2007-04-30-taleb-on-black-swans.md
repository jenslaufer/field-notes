# Taleb on Black Swans

> **"I want to turn lack of knowledge into action."**
> The earliest Taleb appearance on EconTalk, and its value is entirely in its date:
> 30 April 2007. *The Black Swan* had been published only weeks before — the conversation
> walks its chapters as brand-new material and a French edition is still being written.
> Nothing has happened yet. The banks are intact, and the man arguing that fat-tailed
> losses are structurally unestimable is doing it with no vindication behind him.
> Everything here is a *prediction about method*, not a post-mortem — which makes it the
> cleanest available test of whether the framework was real before it paid off.

Companion notes in this directory: the financial-crisis episode two years later, when the
tail arrives, and the antifragility episode five years later, when the missing noun gets
coined. Both linked under **Sources**, with the cross-source synthesis
[`../complexity/antifragility.md`](../complexity/antifragility.md).

## A caveat on quotes that matters here

**This page does not carry a transcript.** Under an `AUDIO TRANSCRIPT` heading that
promises more than it delivers, EconTalk published a condensed, timestamped set of
**Podcast Episode Highlights** — a listener's notes, telegraphic, no speaker labels, hardly
a complete sentence of actual speech. A representative line: `Confirmation bias is not
taking seriously what you don't see.` That is somebody's compression of a minute of talk,
not a sentence anyone said. So this note follows one rule, stated rather than left to be
inferred:

- **Quotation marks only around strings verbatim in the source file.**
- **Attribution to a speaker only where the source itself uses quotation marks.** There are
  about half a dozen such strings in the whole hour; they are the only places I write that
  Taleb *said* something.
- Everywhere else the attribution is to the document — *the notes read*, *the summary
  records*. The wording is the note-taker's; the argument is presumably Taleb's and
  Roberts', but the seam between them is invisible and I do not guess at it.

**The compression garbles things.** Three visible cases, quoted as printed rather than
repaired: an idiot savant is good at "a looting game" where the *ludic* game is under
discussion; the diversifiers are "book owners" where publishers are meant; and the
long-horizon point comes out as an error that "won't be just 25 your error from predicting
1 year forward", with the multiplication word dropped. The timestamps, by contrast, are
real — they are the segment structure, and the sections below follow them.

## 1. The epigraph, the bias, and the two provinces (0:37)

After Roberts on Taleb's library — large, though not as large as Umberto Eco's — comes a
thematic passage from *Fooled by Randomness*, printed in the summary without quotation
marks:

> We favor the visible, the embedded, the personal, the narrated, the tangible. We scorn
> the abstract.

Then the swan: we believe all swans are white because we have probably never seen a black
one. And the definition everything else follows from, sharper than the textbook version:
`Confirmation bias is not taking seriously what you don't see.` Two things are easy to
lose. **The bias is described as adaptive, not stupid** — the notes say it works well in a
primitive environment but not in less primitive ones, so the failure is a mismatch between
an inference machine tuned for one distribution and a world running on another. That is far
harder to dismiss than *people are irrational*. And **the trigger condition is one line**:
the real world has many observations dominated by fat-tailed distributions, which is to say
dominated by a small number of observations. Fat tails are not defined by a formula here —
they are defined by *who contributes the total*.

The two provinces then arrive fully named and fully worked, as a four-step thought
experiment. Two countries, each populated with a good sample of the world's population.
Measure **weight**, then add the heaviest person you can find: a trivial percentage of the
total, because "The exceptional is inconsequential for weight." The mechanism is the **law
of large numbers** — "as your sample becomes very large no single instance can influence the
total" — and the Gaussian characterises this world. Now swap weight for **income**, which is
not normally distributed, and add Bill Gates: "The exceptional in this case is not
inconsequential."

**The same operation on two variables gives opposite answers**, which turns *which world am
I in?* from a philosophical position into something testable. The notes extend it to
currency markets, the Reichsmark hyperinflation and a 1992 Israeli interest rate incident.
What is *not* here: any suggestion that Extremistan variables could be modelled with a
better-chosen distribution.

## 2. How much sample is enough — and the unknown upside (9:58)

The most operational part of the first half, usually dropped because it reads like
housekeeping.

- Novels: a handful dominate sales even in a world without J. K. Rowling. "Between 5 and 20
  will dominate sales in any given year."
- In Mediocristan, scorning the abstract works **because a small sample really is enough** —
  the rule of thumb given is around 30 observations to get the general properties. It is
  cost-effective not to sample everyone.
- In Extremistan the question has no such answer. The top 100 stocks will not be enough to
  protect you; you probably need a broader index, say 500; but you still do not need every
  stock in existence. Even the corrective is a judgement, not a computation.

And a line that does not fit the doom-laden reputation at all: "Not just the risk, but the
unexpected, the risk that is unknown. Risk of stock market is not just losing money, but
also missing an opportunity from the unknown." **Missing the upside counts as a risk.** In
2007 that is already in the argument, years before it acquires a name.

## 3. Short memory, narrative fallacy, and the blurry dog (17:01)

The Extremistan half opens on the psychological failure: we think about the last few days
rather than a longer sample and say — one of the few strings the source puts in a speaker's
mouth — **"What I've seen is sufficient."** The worked case is a single sentence carrying a
whole chapter: "In summer of 1982, U.S. banks suddenly lost more than they'd made
cumulatively in the past." Many good years in a row, small sampling, a forgotten small
probability; and the notes immediately generalise, this error is pervasive way beyond
financial markets. The remedy is a stance, not a tool — the **skeptical empiricist**, whose
demand is "don't just tell me the narrative, the ex-post story, but give me the data so I
can figure out the general distribution". Hold that against the crisis episode two years
later: here he still believes the data will yield the distribution.

Then the epistemology. **Narrative fallacy** and hindsight bias: we are suckers for stories
and ex post we always have one. The replication problem, in 2007: 80% of epidemiological
studies fail to represent real life, that is, cannot be replicated; what people with
statistical data do is process it and find accidental associations — "Can even find a study
where smoking lowers the risk of breast cancer." Hence the paradox, "The more data, the
worse our statistical knowledge will be!", with the consumer version attached, "The more
you read the newspaper the dumber you get." That is not a complaint about media quality; it
is the claim that **search volume over a fixed dataset manufactures findings**. The one
directly attributed sentence in the stretch: **"Once you have a theory in your head you are
going to just look for confirmation."**

And the experiment that makes it physical, credited to **Bruner and Potter**: show a blurry
picture of a dog, too blurry to discern. Increase the resolution *gradually* and you do not
see the dog; increase it *less* gradually and the viewer does. The person who forms a lot
of intermediate theories that it is not a dog then relies on his experience. **Slow exposure
to a signal is worse than fast exposure**, because slowness buys time to build wrong
theories that then block the answer — and it applies to anything you watch a little of
every day.

## 4. Induction, degrees of freedom, and the melted ice cube (28:32)

The riddle of induction is posed as a dilemma with no comfortable side: a **linear model**
has one or two degrees of freedom and so limited ability to predict; a **nonlinear model**
allows too many, so you can predict anything at all if you select the wrong one. Then the
asymmetry:

> "See an ice cube on the floor and you can predict how much it will amount to when it
> melts. If you see a puddle of water on the floor, it's harder to tell where it came from."

The forward problem is tractable; the backward problem is not. **Every explanation of a past
event is a backward problem** — which is why the narrative fallacy is not a bias you can
train away, but an underdetermined inference dressed as a memory.

The institutional version: publication bias, credited to **Ed Leamer** — "if you find
nothing it's hard to publish it". And the **George Stigler** anecdote, which is really an
argument about tooling. As a young economist he ran three or four regressions to test a
theory and spent a lot of time choosing variables, because the computation was done by
hand. Today you can run hundreds and easily convince yourself that the ones that worked out
are the good ones. **Cheap computation did not improve the inference. It moved the thinking
from before the test to after it**, where it becomes selection.

## 5. Skill or luck, and why we cannot tell (28:32)

The same selection logic applied to people: "If you have 1000 traders, you are bound to
have 3 or 4 who by pure randomness do well." They attract the capital, while those with
identical skills may never get a career started — the counterfactual population is
invisible, which is the opening definition applied to careers. The discriminating test says
*where* the ambiguity lives rather than declaring everything luck:

> "A good dentist who makes a lot of money is probably pretty good at being a good dentist;
> but a trader who makes a lot of money may be just lucky."

The difference is the payoff distribution of the trade, not the character of the person.
Asked whether a financial performer is skilled, the answer the source puts in quotation
marks is **"I don't know."** — and: "You can't infer skills from success; but we reward it
the same way." The segment closes on the direction of the error: **"We are more likely to
mistake the random for non-random than the non-random for random,"** because of behavioural
bias and statistics masquerading as science. We over-detect pattern; we never over-detect
noise. Any correction runs one way only.

## 6. The how-to: 80/20, the envelope of serenity, and serendipity (37:37)

Roberts asks how someone should arrange their financial affairs to sleep at night given
that the downside is unknown. The answer is the barbell, years before it is called that,
and it arrives in two parts.

**Sort the exposures first.** Three buckets — prone to negative black swans (banking,
reinsurance), black-swan neutral, and prone to positive ones (biotech, publishing; the
source garbles this as "positive black-swan error"). Characterise headlines into these
categories and select the neutral or immensely positive ones. **This sorting rule needs no
probability estimate at all**: it asks which side of the tail you are on, not how likely
the tail is.

**Then allocate**, from the how-to chapter, Chapter 13. People confuse the risky and
non-risky, and a Markowitz model aimed at medium risk is prone to model error. So: "Same
average risk can be achieved with securing 80% of your cash with security guards, and with
the other 20% taking all the risks you can." The argument is not a higher expected return;
it is that the medium-risk portfolio depends on a model and the split does not. And then
the instruction for the risky sleeve, which nobody quotes:

> "go to parties, try to learn about fat tails, small chance of something fascinating
> happening, create exposure to create an envelope of serenity"

**Going to parties is a risk instruction.** The speculative allocation is not primarily
financial — it is exposure to encounters whose payoff cannot be modelled. And the goal is
named as serenity, not return: the safe part exists to make the speculative part bearable.

The segment ends on the source of error the notes call more extreme than the one **Hayek**
described. Look at a dog and you can believe it was the product of randomness; look at a
car and it is hard to — "Yet the car was the result of a random process, and maybe even a
worse one than the dog." Scientific life reads as if it evolved by design. Hence: **"I have
the feeling that we are fooled by our own skills."** The evidence: "Most of what has been
discovered technologically was found when not looking for it, with minimum amount of
theories about what to look for" — the Internet, the computer, the laser, all found by
accident, plus penicillin. And the price tag, via Morton Meyers' *Happy Accidents*: the
National Cancer Institute went through 130,000 compounds before iterating in on the ones
for successful chemotherapy. "Very little is found by controlled experiment." — "Trial and
error is inherently hit and miss." **We cannot see the search that produced the hit, so we
credit the design.** 130,000 is what a positive black swan actually costs.

## 7. Separability, the ludic fallacy, and forecast error (50:08 / 1:10:24)

Is everything luck, then? No — the distinction is **separability**: "If I need a tie to
become a banker, does it mean that having a tie causes my success?" It is still necessary
to do research to find something, and most people cannot keep *necessary* and *sufficient*
apart. The generalisation, from Chapter 4, is the conditional-probability confusion: "All
tigers are killers is not the same as all killers are tigers." And the constructive answer:
**"You need to make your own luck, as Pasteur said."**

Then the **ludic** distinction, the episode's most durable concept:

| | Ludic games | Ecological uncertainty |
|---|---|---|
| Rules | clear, no ambiguity | unsure about the rules |
| Probability structure | known | unknown |
| Example | limit poker | no-limit poker |
| Where you live | textbooks | the real world, mostly Extremistan |

"Statistical books are all ludic games--rules are very clear." — "In real world, you don't
usually know the probabilities." The barb attached is that an idiot savant would be very
good at a ludic game, which is the point: **competence inside the rules is not evidence of
competence about the rules.** The Hayekian half follows — "Using Gaussian techniques in a
non-Gaussian world, or equilibrium techniques in an (unknown) non-equilibrium world, can
lead you to make errors" — with **James Buchanan** on prices that "emerge in part from the
process itself", Hayek's *Pretence of Knowledge*, Shackle, and Bastiat on the seen and the
unseen. Plus the wry note on this whole tradition: **"People who discuss this become
unknown."**

The final segment turns the critique on itself — "The Black Swan uses statistical arguments
against statistics itself" — through two mechanisms. **Tunneling**, via the casino:
"Running a casino sounds like an easy way to make money; but what about other problems of
running a business?" A tiger attack of the Siegfried and Roy kind produces lawsuits and the
need for a new show; "Risk can be outside your perception"; and "Same casino had multiple
problems not related to tiger incident." The risks that mattered were never in the category
the model covered. Then **self-reference**: "Gaussian distribution tells you how much data
you need to identify it, self-reference. Using the data you first have to discover the
distribution you need." Only with the Gaussian are the parameters easy to find; "Power
laws--cannot find the parameters easily." **The tractability of the normal distribution is
not a property of the world — it is the reason it keeps getting chosen.**

And then the concession that makes the rest usable: "But that doesn't mean you cannot
predict anything." **Error does not scale linearly with horizon** — a forecast 25 years out
has an error that "won't be just 25 your error from predicting 1 year forward" (the source
drops *times*) — and "Prediction is a kind of therapy", so you have to look at your error
after the fact. Which yields the reframe worth more than the critique: **"Forecast error is
central to decision-making."** The decision variable is the error bar, not the forecast, and
the suitcase makes it usable: "Traveling to France, you know the size of suitcase for a
given time of year; but need much bigger suitcase if traveling to Mars."

The close is Socrates in the *Apology* and a graded ignorance — "There are degrees in your
ignorance about what you don't know" — then the line that names the enterprise, set against
Marx critiquing Hegel: "I want to turn knowledge into action" versus **"I want to turn lack
of knowledge into action."** We should embrace the phrase "I don't know." And the last note
in the file is the limiting condition on all expert advice: "We like experts, but not all
domains have experts."

## What is already formed here, and what is missing

**Complete in 2007, with no crisis behind it:** Mediocristan and Extremistan as a *test*
rather than a metaphor; confirmation bias as blindness to the unobserved, with the
narrative fallacy as its retrospective twin; the ludic fallacy, fully argued; positive and
negative black swans as two directions of exposure you sort yourself into; the barbell in
substance and with a numeric split; serendipity as the mechanism of discovery, priced;
model error as the reason to prefer a strategy that needs no model.

**Absent — and measurably so, because these words do not occur in the source at all:**
*fragile*, *robust*, *antifragile*, *convex*, *option*, *barbell*, *via negativa*,
*hormesis*, *skin in the game*, *tinkering*. No Seneca, no Hammurabi. Two gaps matter:

- **There is no word for the good thing.** The vocabulary is entirely defensive even though
  the positive tail is present in the argument. You are told to seek positive black swans
  and build an envelope of serenity, but there is no noun for the property you are trying
  to acquire. The later coinage is exactly this gap being filled.
- **There is no ethics.** Nothing about who bears the downside. The trader who does well by
  randomness is an inference error, not a transfer of risk to someone else. The moral
  argument arrives only once the crisis makes the transfer visible.

## Synthesis: the 2007 position, stripped

1. **Which world you are in is testable; what happens in it is not.** Ask who contributes
   the total. If one observation can dominate, the sample-size question has no answer. A
   classification available today, needing no forecast.
2. **Evidence accumulates asymmetrically.** We over-detect pattern, never noise. More data
   searched by more people yields more accidental findings, so a bigger dataset degrades
   inference unless the search is constrained in advance — which expensive computation used
   to do for free.
3. **The backward problem has no solution.** Puddles do not tell you about ice cubes, so
   skill cannot be inferred from outcome wherever the payoff is fat-tailed.
4. **Therefore act on the exposure, not the estimate.** Sort every position by which tail it
   faces. Secure the bulk. Spend the rest buying exposure to unmodellable upside, including
   social exposure — and the purpose of the secure part is that it makes the speculative
   part bearable.

The through-line to everything afterwards is the last move: **the decision does not run
through the probability.** In 2007 that is argued epistemically — the probability is not
knowable. Later it hardens into a claim about payoffs. That the shape was already here,
before any of it paid off, is why this recording is worth more than the summaries of it.

## Solo-builder read (insights for Jens)

- **The classification move is free and I do not run it.** Sorting exposures into
  negative-tail, neutral and positive-tail needs no forecast, no data, ten minutes. A single
  large client is negative-tail; a monthly SaaS floor is roughly neutral; a published
  artefact in a store is positive-tail. The useful shock is not how many negative-tail items
  there are — it is how much of my week goes into *neutral* work that cannot ever produce
  the outcome I am working for.
- **Go to parties is the instruction I would edit out if I were summarising, which is
  exactly why it stays.** The speculative sleeve is exposure to encounters whose payoff
  cannot be estimated, and for someone with no network that sleeve is near zero regardless
  of what the portfolio looks like. Harder to follow than any allocation percentage, and
  unambiguously the underweight one.
- **The Stigler story is about my tooling, not about econometrics.** When each test was
  expensive the thinking happened before it; when tests are free — and an agent can run
  fifty variants overnight — the thinking migrates to after the results, where it is
  selection. The countermeasure is not fewer experiments but writing down what counts as a
  positive result before launching, and keeping the failures visible.
- **The blurry dog describes how I watch metrics**, and the dentist/trader test says which
  of those numbers I may learn from. Checking a slow-moving series daily is gradual
  resolution increase: it buys time to build intermediate theories that block the pattern.
  And thin-tailed feedback — a feature someone uses, a bug fixed — is real evidence of
  skill, while a launch or a store listing is not: building a method out of one good
  fat-tailed outcome is the 1000-traders error committed on a sample of one.
- **The envelope of serenity is a better objective than growth.** It makes the secure part
  psychological infrastructure: it exists so the speculative part can be held without panic.
  That is why losing the floor costs more than the money — it forces the speculative sleeve
  to be liquidated first.

# Taleb on Skin in the Game

> **"This is why you cannot disentangle risk management from ethics, you cannot
> disentangle economics from moral philosophy. Some people try it; they can't."**
> The technical version of an idea that later became a mass-market book. The episode is
> pinned to a specific paper — Taleb and Constantine Sandis, *The Skin in the Game
> Heuristic for Protection Against Tail Events* — which Roberts names in his first sentence,
> so the argument arrives in the order a paper makes it: mechanism, probability claim, scope
> condition, objection. The book *Skin in the Game* would not appear until 2018. What you get
> here is the load-bearing structure before the aphorisms were built on top of it — and
> Roberts, who shares the politics but not the reasoning, spends the hour looking for the
> edges of the claim.

## The document: what it is, and where it is unreliable

## 1. (0:33) The definition, and the oldest document we have

> **"Meaning I cannot take risks entailing that might harm others without being subjected
> to them myself."** [*sic* — garbled around "entailing"]

The restatement is cleaner: "you cannot possibly make a bet entailing a random variable that can
harm others without yourself being somewhat harmed. It doesn't need to be as harmed; you need to
incur some personal harm, enough to be a deterrent." Three constraints are fixed there and hold
for the whole hour: it is about **bets that harm others**, not sincerity in general; **symmetry
of exposure is not required**, only enough harm "to be a deterrent" — the escape hatch that
answers Roberts' objection eighteen minutes later, planted in the definition itself; and it is a
**deterrent**, an instrument rather than a virtue. Then the claim of scale: "probably the
earliest idea that ever decimated a society" [*sic*], "definitely behind the first document, I
mean the oldest document we have, Hammurabi's Code", which "allowed society to function for
several thousand years" — though "unfortunately it's gone today from consciousness because of
modernity and all these fancy ideas."

**Hammurabi:** "The architect, or the engineer--can't translate Babylonian--if he builds a house
and the house collapses and kills the owner of the house, the architect shall be put to death."
If it kills the owner's firstborn son, the architect's firstborn son dies. The *reading* is the
contribution. Taleb explicitly rejects retribution — "Babylonia didn't have much against
architects. They liked architects." The law is an **information mechanism**:

> **"the architect can hide their risks, delayed blowups, because the cheapest, the place where
> you can hide the risk is by cutting corners in the foundation. And of course he is not going
> to be there when the thing collapses."**

Hence: **"The Babylonians detected it; they understood that no inspector, no regulation, will
ever ever outperform that simple rule."** Not regulation-is-bad as ideology — regulation is
*epistemically outmatched*. The risk is buried where one person can see it, and the only lever
reaching the foundation is bolted to him.

Then the "cultural archeology" he and Sandis did: Hammurabi → *an eye for an eye* in Exodus →
*love your neighbor as yourself* in Leviticus → Hillel, asked to explain the Torah standing on
one leg ("Don't do to others what you would not have done unto you; and the rest is commentary")
→ **Isocrates**, in the chain because he applied the rule **to states**: "States should not do to
other states, weaker states, a strong state should not do to a weaker state what he wouldn't want
a stronger state to do to it." Negative rule first, positive Golden Rule after. And the punchline,
which is why the paper exists: what looks like the foundation of ethics is also the foundation of
risk management.

## 2. (6:43) Roberts corrects the record, then opens the maths

Roberts spends his first long turn on a pet peeve rather than an objection, and the instinct is
good: **"an eye for an eye"** was not applied literally in Jewish law. The Talmud substitutes
"the physical eye for the economic value of the eye" — damages "equal to the loss of an eye".
Note what that does to Taleb's genealogy: the tradition he cites as the ancestor had already
**converted the deterrent into a price** two thousand years ago, the same move Taleb himself is
forced into at 18:18. Roberts does not press it. Taleb then corrects *himself*, unprompted —
Isocrates and Hillel were not contemporaries, "it was still several centuries separating them...
I just realized" — a small sign the recording is unedited. The chain closes on **Kant**, "and we're
still stuck in Kant", with Roberts adding **Adam Smith's impartial spectator**: "Smith wanted you
to think about your behavior as if someone were watching you. He said that's the way we actually
do behave." Hold that — at 21:17 Taleb argues the spectator must be *physically present in a
village* to work.

Roberts then asks the right question: the builder's incentives run on **opacity** and
**information asymmetry**, so generalise those. Taleb says there is a third thing, and it is the
only new thing in the paper ("our paper doesn't bring anything new except for this concept behind
it"): the **shape of the payoff distribution**. "you make a penny, you make a penny, you make a
penny, and then you lose dollars." Under a Pareto 80/20 process — which he glosses, garbled, as
"80-20 means 80% of the company should reap 20% of the profit", with Roberts supplying the
cleaner inventory version — you get the property that breaks careers:

> **"in any given year, you will have 90% of the observations above the true mean"**

You never observe the mean; you observe profits. "in fact the mean can be negative" — "Banks lost
$4.7 trillion during the Crisis." Therefore **"if you make small gains and large losses, you
cannot observe the statistical properties from a small sample."** So: **"The most misunderstood
thing in society is the law of large numbers"** — most distributions "have slow numbers", the mean
"doesn't reveal itself very easily" — and for an agent without skin in the game, **"My optimal
strategy is to shoot for small steady gains and rare losses."**

Roberts names it — **"This is picking up nickels in front of the bulldozer"** — and runs it out
into a career: "most of the time, the bulldozer is moving slowly; there's plenty of time to pick
up the nickels; you look like you are really smart because you've got all these nickels. And then
one day you get run over." With the alibi Taleb quotes from the paper: *based on the information,
I did the right thing.* Taleb's technical version is the sharpest line in the stretch: **"in a
fat-tailed series, eventually the maximum you will have will be equal to the mean. To the
totals."** Trade 10,000 days and "everything will be dominated by a single observation." Then the
career arithmetic — nine years of bonuses, then year ten; "if you make money 4 years in a row, in
New York people start saying hello to you. They eventually laugh at your jokes" — with the 1982
precedent, Walter Wriston of Citibank: "oh, it's only one bad quarter, what is everyone talking
about? Yeah, the one bad quarter, that wiped out everything made by Citibank."

**The structural point, easy to miss:** the problem is not dishonesty. The compensation clock is
faster than the clock on which the distribution reveals itself, so an honest agent with a
nine-year record and no skin in the game is *indistinguishable* from a fraud — including to
himself.

## 3. (15:32) The roller coaster, and Taleb's correction to it

Roberts brings an example from dinner the night before: the rickety 1924 roller coaster at the
Santa Cruz boardwalk with its spotless record. "you could have 365 days a year for 90 years with
no accidents, and it's obviously very safe; and then one day, it's possible for 150 people to be
killed". Taleb **rejects his host's example while accepting the shape** — "But that's mild.
Because you don't have as big a problem for the rest of society." Roberts gets there at once
("That's because you don't kill all the people who rode it on the other days"), and Taleb
confirms: **"The analogy is it killed everyone who ever rode on it. This essentially would be a
fat-tailed process."** A bounded disaster is not a fat tail; the rare event must be able to reach
back and cancel the entire record. That is what separates a bad day from an uninsurable exposure,
and Roberts' intuitive version failed the test.

The rest is the mechanism in modern dress. **"what is happening in today's modern society is you
have invisibility of the risks"**, so **"someone is shooting of course for a high probability, low
impact benefits and low probability, high impact losses. If he doesn't bear the losses, that's the
optimal strategy."** Three riders on that:

- **The capital feedback loop**, usually left out: a good run does not only pay bonuses, it
  attracts money. "if you make money for 2 or 3 years you are going to get a lot more capital. So
  you are going to get bigger and bigger and bigger and bigger until you blow up. So when you blow
  up, you'll get your maximum." The blow-up is *sized by* the record before it. Success is the fuse.
- **Reputation is not a substitute** — and here sits the garble: "So your reputation doesn't
  matter. Repetitions [*sic* — Reputations] are very easy to game; you just shoot for a high
  probability, low impact event."
- The instruction to an investor: **"You lose money, I want you to be harmed a lot more than
  me."** Note *more*, not equally.

## 4. (18:18) Roberts' real objection: you can have too much skin in the game

The first hard pushback, correctly aimed at the paper rather than the podcast: **"You can have too
much skin in the game. And I don't think you deal with this in the paper. It seems like an issue
you should deal with."** His chain: execute every builder whose house collapses and "there are
going to be a lot of costs to being a builder"; houses sometimes collapse through **bad luck**,
and "there's a continuum on which we would judge irresponsibility"; therefore the liability rule
prices the product — "if we say to the builder: You have to build the house safe enough so that if
someone dies you'll be killed, you have to build a very very safe house--it could be that house is
so expensive I would really rather take a little more risk than that."

That is the standard economist's answer to strict liability, and it lands. Taleb's reply
**concedes and reframes** rather than defeating it: **"There's got to be some equilibrium. People
will ask the builder to cut corners, otherwise they can't afford the house"** [printed twice — see
caveat]. Then the retreat to the definition planted at 0:33: **"'skin in the game' doesn't mean
matching the exposure of others. In other words, you don't need to kill the owner of the plane
company every time there is a plane crash. But on the other hand there has to be a painful
disincentive beyond the cosmetic."**

The supporting observations are better than the concession. **Capitalism already contains the
asymmetry as a feature** — "Capitalism has a built-in asymmetry, in the sense that bankruptcy has
a zero in it, there's no negative for a company" — so you keep skin in the game "by forcing people
to lose a little more money. It doesn't have to be unlimited." And **the doctor case is the modern
Hammurabi**: "if the doctor amputates someone and he takes the wrong leg, you don't take the doctor
and amputate his leg in return. But there is a penalty, you see." Malpractice liability is the
Babylonian rule converted to money — precisely the conversion Roberts described from the Talmud
twelve minutes earlier. Then the scope condition, which stops this from being a demand to execute
contractors:

> **"we're not worried about places in which this equilibrium has been discovered heuristically,
> bottom up. I am worried about modernity. I am worried about bureaucrats causing hyperinflation,
> affecting savers and citizens but not harming them at all."**

**Honestly read: the objection is not answered, it is scoped out.** Taleb's position after this
exchange is not *more liability everywhere* but: where private contracting has already found the
trade-off, leave it; the target is where the decision-maker is structurally unreachable. Far
smaller and far more defensible than what the book is usually read as claiming — and it is Roberts
who forces it out of him.

## 5. (21:17) Three levels — contract, morals, and the state

Roberts separates what Taleb runs together: skin in the game as **positive** economics, as a
**moral** claim, and as a **regulatory** principle ("Regulation should ideally put skin in the game
rather than take it away. And it often takes it away."). Taleb interrupts the third before it is
finished, and the interruption is the politically decisive sentence of the episode:

> **"Skin in the game, to me, is a heuristic. That heuristic can be enforced between two
> individuals engaging in a contract. Regulation requires a state. This is the main difference
> here, is that we are bypassing the state. In the contract."**

Followed by the image the idea could have been named after: **"I can eat your food if you taste it
in front of me."** Two parties, no inspector, no disclosure document. The verification *is* the
transaction.

Roberts pushes to level two — "you are saying that if I'm the cook, even if my customers don't
insist that I eat my own food, I should eat my own food." Yes, and the moral version gets its
cleanest form: **"I should not make a forecast unless I am harmed by it."** Expanded: "if someone
asks me for my opinion, it is immoral for me to say, well, the market is going up or the market is
going down or this will happen, unless I stand to lose on that advice. Because people take risks
based on other people's advice." Roberts supplies the analogy — **"We all understand it's immoral
to review a book you haven't read."** And then the scope condition nobody quotes: **"I am confining
it to moral philosophy in the negative tail domain."** A pleasant lie about tomorrow's weather is
outside it — "There's no harm. If there's no harm, that's not my space." Skin in the game as ethics
governs only situations where someone can be hurt; it is not a theory of sincerity.

### The government argument, where they half agree

Roberts states the libertarian version bluntly: **"what government's really good at is getting rid
of skin in the game. That's kind of their specialty."** Taleb refuses it — "Not all governments" —
and splits the term. What both "tend to call governments, because we have the same political
colors" is the *centralized* one; "government can be a local, neighborhood union." In Switzerland
or Sweden the decision-maker is "usually embedded in a community": **"their skin in the game is
typically shame. Because they are socialized by the community."** A Washington official who errs
has "a spreadsheet looking at him. It's not someone in church on Sunday looking at him and making
him feel shame."

**Roberts tries to rescue the national level, then demolishes his own rescue** — the most honest
twenty seconds in the hour. His candidate substitute for shame is history: Bernanke's "name will go
down in history, to some extent, for his decisions." Then: policy is opaque, "we don't have the
data to evaluate whether he's done a good job", so "the shame part of a blemished historical legacy
is very limited in economic policy actions." Taleb explains why, in the best-argued line of the
section: **"the law of large numbers works much faster at the micro level than at the macro
level."** At the macro level "you need thousands of years to figure out if someone, if for a
repeated event someone made the right decision. At the micro level, you know, we have thousands of
experiments every day." Shame is not a moral technology that stopped working; it is a *statistical*
one that needs a fast feedback loop, and macro policy has none.

The counter-model is the army: **"people who repair helicopters are sort of forced to take rides on
them"**, and in parachute folding, "if you fold someone's parachute you may be asked to jump in
it". Small systems generate the rule spontaneously, because "when they are small, you can identify
cause and effect very easily, and then you can force the cook to eat his own cooking." Whereas
**"when things get large there is a size effect. Large is ugly. And large doesn't have skin in the
game"** — and large "likes regulation rather than the simple heuristics."

**Where Taleb turns out less libertarian than his host.** He calls government a last resort —
**"As a libertarian I like the last resource"**, "not the first" — then names exceptions: law
enforcement, market failures, and "systemic inability to protect individual against larger threat".
His example is GMO food: "the person producing them may threaten my backyard... I can't fence it
against the strangeness[?] in ecology". So "maybe the regulation is something to consider in these
exceptional cases", with the triage metaphor: **"Emergency room medicine is completely different
from cosmetic surgery."** Roberts concedes nothing and goes further: **"if government stopped
bailing out banks, there would be a natural skin in the game."** No regulation needed — remove the
option. Taleb agrees and adds the mechanism, **"This is where we have problems, metastatic
government"**, which attracts "people who have everything to benefit from causing you harm, namely
the lobbyists."

So: a disagreement about *layers*, not direction. Roberts wants one deletion. Taleb wants a
heuristic at the contract layer, a size limit, and a narrow regulatory carve-out for risks an
individual cannot fence. Both call themselves libertarians; only one ends the section arguing for a
GMO rule.

## 6. (31:12) The formal core: who is long the option, and who is short it

Asked what skin in the game shares with an eye for an eye and the Golden Rule, Taleb answers
**symmetry** — then converts the ethics into option pricing. More upside than downside is positive
asymmetry, "I am what's called 'antifragile.'" More downside than upside is fragile. And the
unification: it is **"the same equation that explains them all. And the equation that we use in
option trading when you short volatility."** Then the ledger at the heart of the episode:

> **"the class of people who are long an option, namely bankers, their volatility is either break
> even or make money, they are not harmed; and society is short an option"** — "if the bankers
> make money we don't get anything; if they lose money, we end up paying for it via taxes, more
> regulation, more burden of having people to understand 2500 pages of code."

Two consequences, both underrated. First, **regulation is part of the payment, not the cure**:
what society pays when the tail lands is taxes, *and* more regulation, *and* the cognitive burden
of 2500 pages of code. That is his strongest anti-disclosure argument in the episode, made in a
subordinate clause. Second, **the clock mismatch is the mechanism**: "there's a mismatch between
compensation periods, whatever you want to call compensation, and the time it takes, because of
large numbers, for the variable to show its properties." Fix the clock or fix the exposure; nothing
else touches it.

**His charge against economics is precise, and not the usual one.** He does not say economists
missed agency problems: **"economists understand the agency problem, they understand moral hazard.
But they don't understand moral hazard with respect to fat tails"** — because they never thought
much about fat tails "other than thinking, well, it's a peso problem and we can't talk about it
because we don't understand it; thanks, bye." Moral hazard on a thin-tailed variable is a nuisance
you can price; on a fat-tailed one it is a transfer of something unbounded. Same name, different
object.

The generalisation is the tool: **"You can look at any individual and say, this guy is long
optionality at the expense of others, or this guy is short optionality, being used by others."**
Roberts asks who the short side is — the taxpayer, "or sometimes the investor." Fund economics as
an option: "Because the fund manager makes money, it's his money. And if he loses money, you pay
for the losses. So you get smaller share of profits than you do of losses." The one correction he
credits as working: hedge funds "hasn't corrected, thankfully, by people forcing hedge fund
managers to have a lot of their money in the funds."

## 7. (36:10) The other column: honour as the positive asymmetry

The paper's second half, and the half the book is remembered for.

**Honour is a risk position, not a sentiment**: "honor is someone who has courage... And using that
to save others or help others", so **"you are only as good as the risks you are taking for the sake
of others"** — "in other words, you are not transferring risks to others. You are the one who is
taking the risks." **Status was the price of that risk** — **"the prestige that we have gotten has
almost always been proportional to the risks that you take for the sake of others."** Medieval
lords "were forced to protect the peasants. And they had the high rank, but it came as obligations.
You took more risks; you were the first person to die." Hannibal first in battle, Caesar the same.

**The modern counter-example is Petraeus**, the most concrete moment in the hour. Taleb reads the
Wikipedia page: "Hundreds of decorations... Turned out, the fellow had never been in battle. He had
never been in battle." Decorations as the *simulation* of a position nobody held. George W. Bush is
in the same paragraph, with Roberts noting that avoiding the draft used to be dishonourable: "It
was dishonorable to find a way out. It wasn't considered clever or smart."

The paper's thesis in one sentence: **"society cannot function when you have an imbalance between,
like in the first column is people who make others take risks for them. And then you have in the
right column people who take risks for the sake of others. It can't function that way. It
cannot."**

### The disagreement they do not resolve

Roberts asks *why did that change?* and they give incompatible answers, then stop. **Taleb:
technology and modernity** — "modernity is causing disruptions in the entire system"; "modernity,
put the bureaucrat in place of risk-taking[?]". **Roberts: wealth** — "Staying alive is definitely
what we call in economics a 'normal good,' meaning we want more of it as we get wealthier. So I
think we value our lives and our health a little higher than we used to, and so our willingness to
sacrifice it is a lot harder." **Taleb rejects it on timing**, which is a real argument rather than
a dodge: "I think this is probably the culture. Because the change is very recent. It just took
place very abruptly." Wealth rose gradually; the culture flipped fast. Roberts refuses to let go —
"Right, but why did that culture change?" — and Taleb's answer begins "[?] look for an explanation"
and dissolves back into the technology claim. Left standing: Roberts' explanation is cheaper and
fits the economics, Taleb's fits the tempo, and neither is tested.

**Then the pivot that matters most for anyone building something:** risk-taking is not only
physical. **"Risk-taking is entrepreneurship."** Entrepreneurs "take risks for society...
collectively we need them because otherwise we can't advance", and instead of honouring them "you
put on a pedestal Harvard grads" (Roberts asks him to repeat it; Taleb says he means Harvard
graduates "as a metaphor"). Then: **"England was built and America was built by adventurers, in the
economic sense, what Adam Smith called adventurers. Not by bureaucrats. And then after on the
benefits are reaped by the class of bureaucrats who come and try to control the process."** Roberts
illustrates with Amazon's loss-making years — "they were bearing all the costs and I was getting
all the gains" — and Taleb with: **"Open a restaurant in New York City, is like suicide. But yet
without these people, we wouldn't be where we are."** The transferable heuristic here is Ralph
Nader's, cited approvingly: "if you are going to vote for war, you should have a member of your
family--a descendent, a son or grandson--on the draft. And then you can vote for war."

**And the personal disclosure, the best argument in the episode because it is about the
relationship rather than the incentive.** Taleb managing money: "I lose every battle but not many
wars." So when a client calls after a bad day, **"you pick up the phone, and you are not
uncomfortable because I lost 10 times more, at least 10 times, up to 50 times more, share of my net
worth that day than he did from the loss. So, it's liberating on both sides."** — **"You don't feel
guilt if you have skin in the game."** The claim is not only that alignment improves behaviour, but
that it removes the *emotional labour* of managing someone else's perception: you stop performing
and start deciding. The norm he cites: "You have half your net worth in your fund, that's the rule
of thumb today."

## 8. (45:03) Binary vs vanilla — the paper with Tetlock

The second paper, with Philip Tetlock, and the part that explains *why* reputation systems fail
rather than asserting it. A **binary event** is up or down, war or no war, 0 or 1. A **vanilla**
(natural, "ecological") event has a magnitude — "you can predict the market is going to down, but
it can go down 20% or 0.1%." The pathology: **"people who forecast are judged on how frequently
they are right, when in fact how frequently they are right doesn't matter."**

The anecdote from *Fooled by Randomness* is the origin of the most famous thing Taleb ever wrote
about advice — and note that the aphorism itself, *never ask anyone for their opinion, forecast or
recommendation; ask what they have in their portfolio*, **is not spoken in this episode.** What is
here is the mechanism it compresses. Someone asked him where the market was going: "I said: Of
course it's going to go up, with high probability. And then the person saw my trading account and
realized that I was exposed to the market going down big time." Both were true at once. **"The real
world is about expectation, not probability."** Probability times magnitude: "the rational thing is
to be short the market but it's more likely to go up; but if it goes up, it will go up small; if it
goes down, it will go down big." Opinion and position **cannot be inferred from each other** — so
asking for the opinion is asking for the wrong variable, even from an honest person.

Historical case: some did predict the first war, "But they imagined it would last 2 weeks" — the
binary was right, the magnitude was the entire event. Hence prediction markets do not hedge you:
"A prediction market is a binary; and an exposure is open-ended" (Roberts: "And life is
open-ended"). Taleb says he opposed them at the time and wrote the Tetlock paper to have "grounds
to discuss" the distinction. The constructive fix is to stop asking *will this happen* and ask by
bucket — "What's the probability of having an earthquake of 3 on the Richter scale? or 4 on the
Richter scale? or 1?"

And the join between the two papers: **"if you have skin in the game, it's never binary"**, because
what matters is the amount. Conversely: **"If you don't have skin in the game, you are going to
game the reputation system, and the reputation system is based on binaries. Perception of others is
more binary."** That is the cleanest statement of the whole thesis. **Reputation is a binary
variable attached to an unbounded exposure**, and the mismatch *is* the agency problem — not greed,
not stupidity. Roberts tests it by building a binary punishment: "if you lose money, I'm going to
slap you on the wrist; and if you make money, I'm going to give you an ice cream cone." Taleb:
exactly — "then you make 5000 ice cream cones... then you get slap on the wrist once."

The Black Swan correction lands here too, reversing how he is usually read: **"they are not more
frequent; they are less frequent. But when they happen, they are deeper. So they have a lower
probability than you think, but a deeper effect. Which is what makes them vicious."**

## 9. (53:25) Why the people without harm get the science wrong

Here the epistemic claim becomes explicit: absence of exposure is not neutrality, it is a
*measurement error*.

> **"people who talk without harm, who talk about events without harm with impunity, write papers
> and do this, people who are not active, don't really understand the real texture of reality."**
> **"When you have skin in the game you are much more in a way scientific about things. You are
> much more rigorous about things, much more rigorous about risk."**

He then attacks a specific literature — the **long-shot bias**, the finding that people overweight
small probabilities and overpay for lottery tickets and insurance. The objection is methodological
and clean: the experiments use **bounded payoffs**, while real financial tails are **open-ended**
(in the 1987 crash "the option can cover 30, 40, 50, 100 years of p&l[?], in some cases 300 years
of p&l[?]"), therefore **"you cannot generalize from an experiment that is not natural to natural
settings"** — the error is "making a statement derived from a variable that has a bounded payoff
and generalizing to things that have open-ended payoffs". And the empirical refutation of where
that generalisation led — sell remote probabilities, people overpay for them: **"And they lost $5
trillion in 2008. More money than they ever made in the history of banking."** So the claim that
people overpay for protection in finance "is false".

Roberts tries to help with aviation — people overreact to plane crashes relative to cars — and
Taleb declines it ("for plane crash maybe you may overreact but it's not a big deal"). Roberts
withdraws it himself: **"My example of the airplane is a bad example."** Two host examples rejected
in one hour, both because they were bounded.

The closing generalisation is symmetric — the same tail mathematics condemns one activity and
blesses another: **"bets on small probability events by financial firms have proven disastrous in
history. And wealth came from bets on open-ended remote-probability events, namely
entrepreneurship."** Selling the tail destroys; buying it builds. Identical distribution, opposite
sign.

## 10. (58:09) Parenting, and the closing argument about economists

Roberts closes on parenting: small children should *not* have skin in the game — we keep them from
the stove — and good parenting means handing it over, "letting our children have their own skin in
the game rather than the skin of the parent." Taleb agrees and offers the Lebanese formula: **"the
first 7 years, you play with them; the second 7 years you let them get in trouble"**, and the third
seven you advise them on how they got into trouble. His gloss maps it onto the triad: **"The first
7 years you protect them, because they are fragile. The second 7 years are antifragile; they need
to get in trouble because they never learn unless they have skin in the game."**

Then he takes the last word back to his own trade — "Because we are both academics." (Roberts:
"Sort of.") **"The only way you can reform economics is by installing some kind of skin in the game
mechanism. Because it looks like now the system on its own allows them to be wrong with total
immunity."**

**The final disagreement is about remedy, not diagnosis.** Roberts raises the identification problem
from his own side: in 2008 one camp said spend $2 trillion, another said $0, "then we spend about $1
trillion--we spend $800 billion roughly--and things didn't come out so well. But it's very possible
that's not proof that they were wrong". No counterfactual, no verdict, no liability. His conclusion
is individual and passive: **"I think we just have to accept the fact that economists can't have
skin in the game and therefore we should discount what they say."** ... "Yeah, or ignore them."
Taleb refuses the passivity, in the most characteristic sentence he says all hour: **"But you cannot
ignore them. You have to build a system."** Ignoring is a private act; the exposure is public. So:
**"build a society in which mistakes made by economists stay on campus."** "if Larry Summers wants
to make mistakes, more mistakes, let him make them at Harvard where we are insulated from it. It's
like the ivory tower; it's because we are protected from them, not because they have to protect
themselves from us." The ivory tower **inverted** — containment rather than privilege — and Roberts
turns it into the closing line: "What happens on campus, stays on campus."

## Synthesis

Read as an engineering document rather than a moral one, the hour has four parts that only work in
this order:

1. **The exposure, not the person, is the unit of analysis.** Every actor is either long optionality
   at someone's expense or short it, being used. The question is never whether someone is honest; it
   is which side of the option they hold.
2. **The mean is unobservable on a career timescale.** Under fat tails the maximum equals the total,
   so a track record is not evidence. This is what makes the agency problem unfixable by
   measurement: no amount of monitoring recovers the missing information, because the information
   does not exist yet.
3. **Therefore verification must be structural, not informational.** Disclosure, ratings, inspection
   and reputation all try to *measure* the hidden risk. Hammurabi's rule instead attaches the hidden
   risk to the only person who can see it.
4. **But only where the loop is short.** Shame is not morality, it is a fast law of large numbers:
   it works in a village or a platoon and not in Washington. Size does not weaken the heuristic, it
   removes the conditions it runs on.

Two scope conditions the later, more famous version tends to lose, both extracted by Roberts:
**symmetry is not required, deterrence is** ("It doesn't need to be as harmed"), which is why
malpractice damages count; and **the moral rule is confined to the harm domain** — not a theory of
sincerity, a rule about who eats the left tail. And the sentence tying the two papers together, the
most portable thing in the episode: **reputation is a binary attached to an unbounded exposure.**
Any system that rewards frequency of being right rather than magnitude can be farmed indefinitely
by someone who does not hold the downside — and will be, without anyone deciding to cheat.

## Solo-builder read (insights for Jens)

The setup fits this episode almost too well: no employees, so no classic principal-agent problem
inside the company — but a permanent, growing perimeter of people and systems acting on my behalf
that hold none of my downside. Agencies, recruiters, vendors, platforms, and now AI agents that
write and ship code. Every one of them is long an option I wrote.

- **Run the column test on the perimeter.** For each counterparty ask only: if this goes wrong in
  the worst plausible way, what do *they* lose? A recruiter loses a fee they never had. An agency
  loses a replaceable retainer. A platform loses one seller out of thousands. A coding agent loses
  nothing at all — it cannot. In each of those I am the taxpayer. Not a reason to stop using them;
  a reason never to buy their forecast.
- **For AI agents the fit is exact.** An agent optimises for the appearance of a finished task — the
  binary — and the review surface is a PR that looks complete. That is literally "shoot for a high
  probability, low impact event", and it is why the recurring failure here is the agent that commits
  three of eight files and reports done, or writes a PR message describing work it did not deliver.
  The agent holds no tail, so the fix cannot be a better prompt asking it to be careful — prompts
  are disclosure, and disclosure is what Hammurabi replaced. The fix is structural: gates that make
  the *absence* of the claimed change fail loudly — a bugfix test that was red before, a
  partial-commit guard, a quality gate it cannot talk past. Verification attaches to the artifact
  because it cannot attach to the agent's downside.
- **Never buy an opinion where an exposure is available.** A vendor's stated confidence and a
  vendor's actual position are different variables, and neither predicts the other. So rewrite the
  question. Not "will this integration work?" but "what does your invoice look like if it doesn't?"
  Not "is this a good channel?" but "will you take part of the fee on performance?" The second
  answer is information; the first is weather.
- **Cashflow work is picking up nickels — fine, as long as I know where the bulldozer is.** A day
  rate is high-frequency, low-magnitude income with a rare correlated loss: the one client, gone in
  a month. The warning is not against the strategy, it is against reading a good run as evidence.
  Nine quiet quarters of single-client concentration look exactly like the nine quarters before a
  blow-up. Same for the capital feedback loop: every success invites more of the same commitment, so
  cap any single dependency while things are going well, because it cannot be capped afterwards.
- **My own skin is a sales asset, and I under-use it.** Taleb's line about picking up the phone
  without discomfort is a *sales* argument, not an ethical one: a solo founder who ships his own
  software, runs it against his own money and eats his own outages holds a position no agency and no
  offshore team can hold. That differentiator survives commoditisation by AI, because it is not
  about the code. It belongs in the offer explicitly — shared risk, a fee that moves with the
  outcome, a guarantee that costs me something real.
- **Read the buy-side of the tail as the strategy, not the risk.** The distinction is sign, not
  size: selling small probabilities (one client funding everything, a no-fallback dependency, a
  personal guarantee) versus buying them (a published artifact, a store listing, a cheap paid test).
  Both look like risk from outside. Only one can pay off unboundedly, and only one can end me.
- **Shame works locally, so build local loops deliberately.** What keeps a Swiss municipality honest
  is a person in church on Sunday; working alone with remote vendors deletes that surface. The
  substitutes are structural — short contracts, small first orders, repeat business as the real
  incentive, payment in tranches against delivered artifacts.
- **And the honest counterweight, which is Roberts' point rather than Taleb's.** Too much skin in
  the game prices things out: demanding downside from every vendor loses the good ones and selects
  for the desperate. The usable form is Taleb's own retreat — "a painful disincentive beyond the
  cosmetic", not symmetry. A deposit at risk, a milestone unpaid, a renewal not granted. Enough to
  change a decision, cheap enough that the deal still happens.

# Taleb on Nations, States, and Scale

> **"I've spoken to a lot of political scientists over time and not one of them was
> interested in scaling."**
> That arrives ninety seconds before the end, and it is the episode read backwards. In every
> earlier Taleb appearance, size is an aside — one bullet next to debt and overspecialisation.
> Here it is the subject, and everything else is downstream: what a nation is, what a state
> is, why Russia behaves as it does, why Switzerland works, why a policy that succeeds in a
> canton fails in Brussels, why a mouse outlives an elephant. This is Taleb's **tenth and so
> far final** EconTalk conversation, and the only one where he applies the nonlinearity
> machinery directly to political units. He says out loud what he wants from the format:
> *"thanks for allowing me to test my ideas on you before completing my books."*

Companion note: the antifragility episode in this directory, linked under Sources — the same
man ten years earlier, with *Antifragile* still an open manuscript. Its closing section on size
and localism, headed with the line about a policy that works in Bern and fails in Brussels, is
one paragraph long. This episode is that paragraph, expanded into an hour.

## A caveat on the source, and what colours it

**This transcript is the good kind.** EconTalk's page carries a full verbatim transcript with
clean speaker labels — every turn prefixed `Russ Roberts:` or `Nassim Nicholas Taleb:` — inside
the site's timestamped "Podcast Episode Highlights" wrapper, with markers at 0:37, 2:00, 6:09,
7:43, 11:01, 19:14, 27:04, 34:56, 40:20, 43:33, 48:45 and 54:50. Those markers are the spine of
the sections below, and attribution is read off the labels, not reconstructed. The recording
date is stated in the transcript — `[Recording date: June 20, 2022.]` — and it published three
weeks later, on 11 July. Roberts confirms on air that this is the tenth appearance and that the
previous one was July of 2020, on the pandemic.

**The transcript still carries ASR damage,** marked `[?]` and `[inaudible …]`. Several garbles
are load-bearing enough that I quote them as printed and mark them `[sic]` rather than repair
them: an elephant *"falling by one mirror"* (almost certainly *one metre*, and the hinge of the
biomechanical argument); NATO as *"some kind of self-protection mechanism via some kind of
leak"* (reads as *league*); Napoleon preventing *"trips from carrying merchandise"* (reads as
*ships*); two contradictory probability figures inside three sentences; and one answer that
inverts its own meaning — asked whether the problem is skin in the game, Taleb replies *"but
skin in the game, that's not one of them"* and then argues from skin in the game. Section 10
treats that as the transcription problem it probably is, without smoothing it.

**Where Roberts is sitting matters here.** The transcript establishes it on air: he is in
Israel, "a few times zones away", and Taleb uses him as a live exhibit for the etymology of
*Medinat*. External context the transcript does not state: by this recording Roberts had left
the Hoover Institution to become president of Shalem College in Jerusalem — running an
institution inside a small, young, contested nation-state while arguing about whether
nation-states should exist.

**What is not in this episode**, despite being adjacent to Taleb's writing at the time: **no
bitcoin**, **no ergodicity by name**, and Lebanon appears once, as a one-line witness that
anarchy is not the alternative. The closest thing to ergodicity is the trader's life-to-date P&L
passage in section 12 — a time-averaging argument in everything but name.

## 1. (0:37) What Taleb says the format is for

Not throat-clearing. He states, unprompted, why a conversation beats a book, and it is a
striking admission from a man who mostly presents himself as self-taught:

> **"a lot of my economic education comes from EconTalk, the education of the economic
> reasoning, because you read stuff in books, you learn it at school, it doesn't work. In a
> podcast forum, because you have a conversation between two people, somehow it helps the idea
> sink in and stay there."**

Roberts jokes that "two or three people" complain about having him on. Taleb: *"I guess if it's
only two or three people, I may be doing things wrong. You need to have more enemies, you need
one needs to have more enemies."* [sic — repetition in the transcript.]

## 2. (2:00) The nation-state is modern, and it was built by destroying everything else

Roberts sets the frame: **a nation in the ethnic sense** versus **the state as an administrative
entity**. Taleb's first move is dating. *"The notion of nation-state is extremely modern. I think
it's not until 1780 that people started talking about it."* Before that, territories of kings;
then German and Italian unification follow the fashion.

**France is the worked example, and he names the process deliberately ugly:** *"what I call
domestic colonization when they just realized that they were a state and about 50, 60 years into
their idea that now we're a state, they decided to destroy anything that was not French in
France."* French meant *"the upper class language that was spoken in the area near Paris"*;
children were punished at school for Patois, Provençal, Breton and the rest. The taxonomy that
falls out is the sharpest line of the section: **"a dialect is something that doesn't have a
nation-state and language has a nation-state."**

Roberts offers the standard definition back — homogeneous ethnicity, common language — and Taleb
refuses it: *"Not necessarily homogeneous."* Ethnicity is an output that keeps being
manufactured, not a fixed input: *"you have recombination or creation of ethnicities every day,
just like you have languages are born every day."*

**Turkey is the demonstration.** The Ottoman Empire held Armenian, Greek, Kurdish, Turkish and
Aramaic speakers — *"they had all these mosaic of people"*. The nation-state arrived and the
mosaic was destroyed; he names the Armenian and Syriac massacres. The conclusion is stated as a
definitional property, not an accusation about Turks:

> **"it became intolerant, because the nation-state by definition is something intolerant."**

And the punchline undoes the ethnic premise entirely: *"when you do the DNA [Deoxyribonucleic
Acid] of Turkey today, you realize that maybe about 7% Turkic, at the most. The entire Western
Turkey is Greek."* The nation purified in the name of ethnicity was not ethnically what it
claimed. (Stated from memory in conversation — his order of magnitude, not a citation.)

## 3. (6:09) The answer to *what is a nation, then* — the smallest possible unit

Asked in what sense an ethnic nation is meaningful, Taleb does not defend ethnicity. He converts
the question into one about size:

> **"One is very small. One is very small. To me, if you're going to have a nation-state, it must
> be the smallest possible unit because if you make it too big, then you start having minority
> problems. You start having conflicts."**

The mechanism arrives as a housing problem — the cleanest statement of the argument in the hour:

> **"When two people are roommates they could have fights. They may not get along. But then,
> instead of giving them, say, a thousand square feet for two people together at school or you
> break it to 500 square feet for each, and each one has an apartment, they'll get along a lot
> better."**

Same floor area, different partition, different outcome; nothing about the people changed.
*"There have been studies showing that good fences make good neighbors."*

**Yugoslavia is his evidence, used the opposite way from most people:** the break-up is the
success. Croatia, Montenegro, Slovenia, North Macedonia — *"They get along because each one has a
little home."* Trade survives the partition. Switzerland enters for the first time as *"in these
cantons which effectively are sort of states, small states under some kind of umbrella."*

Roberts separates two claims Taleb has been running together, and it is a real sharpening: (a)
small works well, and (b) clumping smallness *with people like me* solves problems that
cross-group negotiation cannot. Taleb answers (b) with the **Copts in Egypt**: persecuted as a
distributed minority, *"if they had their own state within Egypt--if they were not geographically
distributed, but they were concentrated in a small state--it would be a lot better for them."*
Concentration is protection; dispersion is exposure.

## 4. (7:43) City-states flourish, nation-states are fragile, and the empire is a tax farm

The state-as-person is named as the intellectual error: *"The state as an entity, that's kind of
Hegelian idea of reification of states."* Before that reification, the administrative unit was
the city.

> **"City-states flourished, whereas nation-states historically--and I discussed that in
> Antifragile--turned out to be fragile."**

Then the definition of empire, offered without moral colouring:

> **"The difference between empire and a nation is that an empire has absolutely no interest in
> your life other than collecting taxes and making sure you don't wage wars."**

He calls it a Mafia scheme, a *pizzo*, and states the survival condition: **"the empire lasts
long when they don't overcharge the people there."** The Phoenicians are the case study, and
their strategy is an insurance decision: no big nation, *"They were small cities, city-states."*
Persians coming? Ten percent — *"That's cheaper than having an army."* Alexander, then the
Romans, same price. Tribute is not humiliation; it is an outsourced defence budget at a known
price, the shape of paying a premium instead of holding a reserve.

The historical control experiment closes the section:

> **"after the Bronze Age collapse where all these big states--Hattusa vanished; the big,
> important states, Egypt was brought to its knees--the city-states of the Eastern Mediterranean
> flourished."**

Big units died; small units grew. He returns to the Bronze Age collapse at the very end, aimed at
China.

## 5. (11:01) Land versus commerce, and the state as a body of laws

Roberts pushes the obvious objection: city-states are rare now and the modern impulse is to
*expand*. His example is the Sudetenland — the population actually mixed and scattered, exactly
like the Copts — and his generalisation: *"nation-states tend, in my historical observation, my
observation of history, to expand to try to grab more of the local ethnic group rather than to be
more pure and homogeneous."*

Taleb concedes the pattern and gives the discriminating variable: **not urban versus rural but
land versus commerce.**

> **"Take Singapore today. You see, they're not interested in conquering land. They're not
> interested in geopolitics. They're interested in making money."**

Phoenicia, Venice, Singapore — all trade-based, none territorial. Territory-hunger is what a
polity does when its livelihood comes from land.

Then the etymological set piece, run live on his host. Taleb asks what the state Roberts sits in
is called *in its own language*. Answer: Medinat Israel. Taleb: *"in Arabic, it's the same word:
medina means city. It means din. Din is law."*

> **"It means, I live under the laws of Israel. And, that's a city-state. City-state is the body
> of laws, if you look at it. It's laws. I live under the laws of that place. So, a state is a
> body of laws, basically, that you're accepting."**

That is the definitional pivot of the episode: a state is neither a people nor a territory but a
**legal jurisdiction you consent to** — which is why it can be small and need not match an
ethnicity.

Roberts adds the enforcement dimension: states vary in how far their writ reaches, naming Somalia
and China. Taleb accepts it "on paper" and adds what has genuinely changed — the state's *share*
and its *tools*. In France he estimates **50 to 70% of GDP** flows through the state depending on
how you count education, *"whereas a years ago it was almost another magnitude lower."*
Historically the French language followed the tax routes, the only places the king could reach:
*"They didn't have radar, they didn't have satellites. They didn't have all these tools of
enforcement."*

## 6. (still 11:01) Why size is central: one person a thousand times, and the elephant

Here the episode turns from history to mechanism, and Taleb says so: *"let me make a comment here,
why size is central to what we're discussing. Because people keep using names--state, nation--the
size is central."*

> **"You do a lot better, I think, meeting a person a thousand times than meeting a thousand
> persons once."**

Same number of encounters, completely different object. In New York you meet different people
daily; in a village, the same ones. And the general form: **"Things don't scale properly. There
are things that work differently at a lower scale."**

Then the image Roberts keeps returning to, credited to Taleb's own technical work rather than to
biology:

> **"what I've discovered while working on volatility models show that why an elephant, for
> example, is not a large mouse. An elephant is vastly more fragile"** [sic — syntax garbled.]

Roberts: "Explain." Taleb: *"because on a risk scale, an elephant falling by one mirror [sic — one
metre] would break a leg and would never recover. A mouse visibly is vastly more robust."* Source
of the effect: *"it comes from the nonlinearity of shocks."*

**The rabbi and the stone**, retold from *Antifragile* because it is the cleanest statement of
nonlinearity available: a king must crush his son with a large stone and asks for a way out; the
rabbi says break the stone into pebbles.

> **"falling 10 meters once is vastly worse than falling 10 times one meter."**

Taleb dates his own programme from this — *"most of my work since 2009 has been on this--finding
the effect of these nonlinearities in places."* — and applies it to states in one step:

> **"whereas a large state is actually more fragile--it requires an extraordinary, an increased
> expenditure in monitoring."**

**Russia is the worked case:** always large, always centralised, always curating an identity,
across three regimes — Czarist, Communist, current — with the same apparatus renamed, *"almost the
same apparatchiks."* *"that big sprawling country; and of course it has to be aggressive because
that's the mode on which Russia was built."* The supporting observation is that regime change never
reaches the administration, and his example is not Russian: *"as we know from Trump's experience,
he went to Washington. He had to face an infrastructure that was entirely hostile."* Same in Iran
after the revolution. **"It takes a long time to change an administration. When it's large, it is
a severe problem."**

## 7. (19:14) New York is already a city-state — and optimal size is domain-dependent

Roberts calls the thousand-people idea *"a very deep idea"* and then says plainly that he does not
see how it applies: New York would seem to make a good city-state, but even Brooklyn is not a
village. *"I don't see how it works in this case, though."* Taleb separates label from function —
**"We have to understand that the United States is not a republic. The United States is a
federation where you have a lot of power to the local municipalities and local cities."** New York
*"is operating like a city-state"*; *"They say that the optimal size of these units is about 8
million"*; and the method is *"let's not go by labels. Let's go by the function."*

Roberts supplies the objection that has haunted small polities since antiquity: *"it's a way of
overcoming the economies-of-scale problem. If you're small, you're vulnerable."* Taleb's reply —
*"You stay small, exactly"* — introduces the most operational idea in the episode:

> **"when you have economies of scale, that notion, one that I keep arguing that it depends on the
> domain. It's domain dependent."**

- **Size has no absolute meaning across domains:** *"a restaurant with a hundred tables is large.
  But, a company that manufactures, I don't know, chemicals needs to be a hundred times the size to
  be large or a thousand times the size. So, it depends on the domain."*
- What genuinely requires centralisation: *"It looks like the thing that requires centralization is
  a military. Of course, and I will argue also pandemics."* — though even there he notes Al-Qaeda's
  decentralised model, and the US replicating it.
- And he claims the classical liberals *for* this position: *"people like von Mises and Hayek, said
  the state is needed for centralized activities, such as epidemics and wars--that's Hayek. And von
  Mises accepted the state. They didn't hate the state."*
- Which lands on the EU's own founding principle: *"that's the notion of subsidiarity under which
  the European Union was built. Unfortunately, execution is not in line with the claims, the initial
  claims."*

**Roberts stops him** — *"We got to back up"* — and names what he takes to be the real argument,
running back through *Skin in the Game* and *Antifragile*: the value of small scale is that it
maintains skin in the game. Taleb agrees and gives the mechanism in one image:

> **"the bureaucrat in Brussels is not going to be punished if the bridge doesn't work well. But,
> the local mayor, particularly if elected among the citizens, will have to encounter at a cafe on a
> Sunday the afternoon would feel shame if she or he fails in the project."**

The enforcement technology is **shame**, and shame requires a village-sized encounter surface.
Roberts is not satisfied — *"That's a very lovely idea. But it doesn't--I don't know how you get
there from here."* — so Taleb stops arguing and points at working systems:

| Country | What Taleb says it demonstrates |
|---|---|
| **Sweden / Scandinavia** | *"It worked very well in Sweden and the Scandinavian countries that are already small; and they got smaller."* Swedish and Norwegian are close languages; the split was cheap |
| **Middle East** | The opposite case — states holding together dialects called Arabic which, in his words, *"they're absolutely not connected. I mean, the dialects are not mutually understandable."* |
| **Switzerland** | *"a country that is built to be bottom up and to stay bottom up, where you paid most of your taxes to the municipality"* — then *"to the canton and to the local unit, and the residual to the state."* The tax flow is the architecture |
| **Germany** | *"Germany has always been bottom up. There were 300 states before the French Revolution, 39 states when it came to unification"* |

**The German irony is the best-told story in the episode.** After the war the French wanted to
punish Germany, reasoning that unification had produced Bismarck and then Hitler — so they imposed
a federation. *"So, they wanted to punish Germany by making it the federation"*, and the verdict on
the punishment: *"that's what made them strong economically."* Roberts: "Distributing their power."
Taleb: **"Distributing the power allows for things to work better."** Asked *why*, he adds a second
mechanism beside skin in the game — a difference in what the two kinds of official think about at
all: *"the bureaucrat thinks in terms of geopolitics and abstract matters, whereas the local person
thinks in terms of water, bridges, cleanliness"*.

## 8. (27:04) Would the United States break up? — and Ukraine

Roberts reframes the argument in club-theory terms, and it is the most rigorous statement of the
case in the episode — from the host, not the guest. You would ideally be in a club with people who
share your preferences, *"But, that's going to be a very small club."* So you enlarge it, sacrifice
autonomy, accept restraint *"because we understand that the gains from banding together are
sufficiently large."* Then the failure mode:

> **"there's a potential cross-subsidization and the political process can start to devote itself
> to rent-seeking, to exploiting certain groups at the expense of others. And, you're stuck. You can
> leave. To leave the country is relatively costly."**

Stated as economics: **exit cost is what converts scale into extraction.** Roberts then says the
thing he calls *"unimaginable 25 years ago"* — that the United States will divide into more than one
country. Taleb's answer is the one this episode gets misremembered for:

> **"the United States is designed for that. The United States is designed to be divided into more
> than one country. All you have to do is weaken the federal government's role in some affairs and
> increase the role of the state."**

No secession required — the machinery is installed. **And he immediately flags the hypocrisy in the
coalition that claims to want this:** *"the Republicans love the idea of a strong state, weak central
government. On the other hand, they want a strong state over the municipalities, as we saw with
COVID."* Devolution is a principle only if it goes all the way down.

Roberts raises the remaining objection — the *national narrative* four successor countries would not
share. Taleb dismisses narrative as a foundation, and the reason is scale again: **"the narrative
should not be the driver because national narratives change all the time. This is why, when you're
small, it's easier."**

**Ukraine is the piece he came to discuss, and explicitly as a model:** *"Not because we care about
Ukraine--well, we care about Ukraine, of course; we care about Russia--but because it's represents
the model."* He had given a talk in Ukraine the previous summer as a guest of the government, and
rewrote the essay after the war started. The idea he says he brought there is a decoupling:

> **"When I explained first that you can speak Russian: is not a problem. You don't have to create a
> new category, a linguistic category. You can speak Russian and not be part of Russia, as Russians
> couldn't get it."**

> **"But, the Swiss get it. You can speak French, be culturally linked to France, but
> administratively linked to Switzerland where it works better."**

Language, culture and administration are three separate axes; collapsing them is the disease:

> **"you had to break up that notion of 'state equal nation, equal people'--that equality. And then
> effectively, you can start having pathologies with the nation-states where the whole nation as an
> entity, on balance acts against the interest of the individuals--see, where you have, you have
> these things that start emerging from bad scaling."**

That last clause is load-bearing: a polity acting against the interests of the people composing it
is presented as an **emergent property of bad scaling**, not as bad leadership.

The war, on his reading, is between two models rather than two countries — *"We have a war not
between two countries, not between East and West. Between a model, the new model, which is
NATO-based"* — where defence is confederated and everything else left alone: *"Exactly. And then,
you do whatever you want outside of that."* He rejects the geographic label outright: **"What we
call the West is not the West versus Russia."** If it were the West, Taiwan would not be in it.

The classical-liberal coda: *"When you let nations start working as nations, they focus on
prestige"* — Napoleon chasing prestige while the English chased commerce and could not understand a
man hurting his economic interest in its name, *"simply to prevent trips from carrying merchandise
across his territory"* [sic — *ships*]. On globalisation he refuses both poles: *"Nobody really
wants autarchy. So, what we're disagreeing about is the degree of the limits of that
globalization."* National identity as an organising obsession is *"very archaic. But, it was already
archaic 225 years ago."*

## 9. (34:56) The minimum deliverable unit, and the stone that doubles

Roberts brings the strongest counter available — Roger Scruton's *Where We Are*, via his Brexit
conversation with Megan McArdle: *"don't people have a sense of self, part of their identity, that
comes from where they live and pride they have in their country?"* Taleb concedes the phenomenon and
attacks its stability — *"It is, but it changes all the time. That's the problem."* — which produces
his design principle:

> **"This is why I like the minimum deliverable--the minimum deliverable unit in size--because when
> things are small, they can change more easily than when things are big."**

Then the nonlinearity stated as a formula and applied to governance:

> **"running Russia is much more difficult, much more than 10 times more difficult than running a
> country a tenth of its size. It's disproportionate. You have to curate an identity. You have to
> keep curating an identity."**

The maintenance list is concrete — *"the flag, the anthems, the history, the language"* — and the
cost curve is the rabbi's stone again: *"just like the stone harms you more and more as it gets
bigger, disproportionately. So, if you double the stone, you have four times the harm."*

Asked whether the US at its size can still curate a shared anthem and narrative, he says it can, and
names the reason as an exception to his own rule: **"In the United States, you realize the federal
government doesn't enter your life that much. The municipality, the county, the state, the smaller
units enter disproportionately."** Russia is the inverse — Putin names the governors of provinces as
the czars did, so *"you end up having a large state with at least a hundred ethnicities of Russia
that needs to be curated all the time, because otherwise it will break apart."*

**Venice is the cleanest test of the land-versus-commerce split:** a flourishing republic for *"1100
years--more: 1100-some years"* which *"did not try to acquire territory. That's not the business they
were in."* Russia, by contrast, *"has businesses to acquire territory"*, and he roots that in a late,
effortful identity: migration west to east, the Eurasian Steppe settled latest, Kiev in the 13th
century under a grandson of Genghis Khan, the identity built with Church Slavonic as a beacon.
**"creating identities and things that formed late requires a lot of work."**

## 10. (40:20) The disagreement: skin in the game, or multifactorial?

The one place Roberts refuses the framing, stated as a correction rather than a question:

> **"isn't that the essential point? That the lack of skin in the game of a large centralized
> top-down place is the problem, not this curation identity-thing you're talking about?"**

Taleb does not concede. His answer as transcribed: **"No, no. There are a lot of things together,
but skin in the game, that's not one of them. It's multifactorial."** [sic] Read literally that
contradicts everything he said at 19:14 — and his own next two sentences, which argue *from* skin in
the game. The likeliest reading is that he said something like *that is not the only one* and the
transcript garbles it. **I flag it rather than repair it**, because the disagreement is real either
way: Roberts wants a single mechanism (accountability), Taleb insists on at least two, and the
second is purely mechanical:

> **"it is communication-wise because things grow, the connections grow non-linearly when you have a
> large country. So, having this communication network, all that requires more and more effort to
> just keep the thing centralized."**

Connections grow faster than nodes. That cost is there even with perfect accountability. His
summary: *"centralization has not worked in practice aside from skin in the game."*

Pressed again on the American exception, he gives the sharpest characterisation of the US in the
episode — **"The individual has a big role in the United States. You don't have a national narrative.
You don't have a national language"** and **"The common narrative is the law. People talk about the
Constitution as if it were--religiously."** A polity glued by a legal text rather than by blood or
language: exactly the *medinat* definition from section 5, and, he notes, the model Europe is now
copying. His marker for how strange this once was: *"At the Congress of Vienna, there were only three
democracies. And, people didn't understand: Where's the king? Who does it belong to?"*

## 11. (43:33) Mouse versus elephant, ruin, and Tocqueville inverted

Roberts gives the argument its best short form and its best objection in one breath:

> **"It's better to be a mouse than an elephant in many situations, because you're antifragile. But,
> of course, there are times you'd rather be an elephant--when there's a lot of cats around."**

Taleb concedes the local advantage and changes the measurement window from individual to species:
*"Yeah, but look at survival. Let's look at numbers. You don't have a lot of elephants left."* —
**"The large animals disappear quickly. They go extinct very quickly."** Roberts converts it into the
ruin principle, which he calls one of the deepest things he has learned from Taleb: a single death is
a tragedy, but *"the death of a people--the death of the species, the death of a humanity on the
surface of the Earth--that's apocalyptic."* An individual mouse is more vulnerable than an individual
elephant; mice as a population are not. **"We have more mice in New York than humans, they claim.
Can't say the same about elephants in Africa--even if you scale by the size."**

Taleb then reverses on the American break-up, and the transcript is ambiguous about which side he
lands on: *"In fact, I just thought of contradicting your idea. Maybe the United States should break
up. The rest of the world is trying to resemble."* The argument that follows is clearly *against*
breaking it up — the rest of the world is copying the American structure, Europe most of all, and
inside it *"you don't need to belong to another country because you don't feel the need."* Felt need
is the test, and where it is felt the structure has failed: *"you want to be out if you're a Tatar.
You want to be out of Russia if you're Ingush. You want out of Russia if you're Chechen."* Chechnya
was tried before Ukraine, *"with even more disastrous consequences, more lives lost per square mile."*

**Then a separate idea, borrowed and extended, with the widest application outside politics:**

> **"Tocqueville realized that the degree of dissatisfaction in a country is proportionate to how good
> things are."**

Before the Internet people were not obsessed with being spied on — *"but the state had J. Edgar
Hoover."* So: **"the more people talk about loss of liberty and stuff, the more liberty there is in the
system."** And: *"At no point in history, have we had given more rights to people who are
underprivileged. But, at no point in history, have people complained more."* The mechanism is
visibility, not virtue — *"transparency can be a problem because you tend to see the bad things more
easily in the Western World."* — and that asymmetry is an attack surface: **"states like Russia and
China have tried to exploit that attribute of the Western system by creating distrust of the government
on the part of citizens."** By cherry-picking real malfunctions, because *"of course you have
malfunction everywhere. It's not perfectly free, but it's free."*

## 12. (48:45) Federal police, self-correction, and a theory of propaganda

Roberts builds the counterfactual that makes decentralisation legible, and it is his best contribution
of the hour: **imagine all police in the United States were federal employees.**

> **"suppose all police were employees of the United States. That's a remarkably enormously different
> system than San Francisco having police or New York City having police."**

The last decade's police scandals produced enormous backlash *"but it's localized and the people who
are accountable have had trouble."* At federal level accountability would evaporate — firing the chief
of police of the United States over one city's misconduct *"would be unimaginable; and it would just
persist."* Taleb agrees flatly, four times, and generalises it into a definition of the West with
nothing to do with geography or values:

> **"Things that are imperfect but functioning well--self-correcting mechanism, I call the West a
> self-correcting mechanism, was complaints causes self-corrections."** [sic]

> **"So long as complaint leads to something... Whatever leads to correction, there's a good system."**

He cites Snowden as a case where he doubts the motive but observes that the correction happened anyway
— the test is the correction, not the intent.

**Then the connection that gives the second half its spine:** *"this is very similar to fooled by
randomness--the problem of fooled by randomness."* Show a trader his P&L live and *"he or she would go
nuts. They would magnify it."* Daily beats intraday. His figures from *Fooled by Randomness*, for one
unit of standard deviation per unit of return:

| Window | Probability of showing a profit |
|---|---|
| Intraday | *"nothing: 50.001%"* |
| One day | *"51% chance of being profitable on any given day"* |
| One year | *"a 90-some percent chance"* — then, three sentences later, *"The 85% probability being profitable over a year"* [sic — the transcript gives both] |

Same process, same edge; only the observation frequency changed. This is a time-averaging argument, and
the closest the conversation comes to ergodicity without the word. **The propaganda theory falls
straight out of it:**

> **"what disinformation does is provide people with a lot of details rather than the [inaudible]. It
> will drown you in details. So, you don't see the thing at scale. We don't see what we have at
> scale."**

Roberts gives the personal version: a feed full of *"these little nagging thorns of dissatisfaction, I
might start to think I have a bad life, when in fact I have a great life."* Taleb's countermeasure is
imported from trading and stated as therapy, not insight:

> **"The therapy you use as a trader is you look at your life-to-date P&L... when you focus on your
> life-to-date P&L, it puts things in perspective. You say what? This is tiny."**

*"They're drowning in news. They're drowning. And, we're not good at scaling the news."* He names the
literature — *"the Kahneman/Tversky approach"* — and gives a live example: people *"managed to make
them believe that truckers are trying to block roads in Canada are equivalent to fighters in
Ukraine."* [sic.] Roberts adds the academic version: the student who complains about two points on a
homework worth 10% of the grade and never comes in when the grading erred the other way. Taleb: **"It's
a question of scaling... There's a law of large numbers in the question of scaling, of how randomness
washes out at a large scale."**

## 13. (54:50) Not an anarchist — Dubai, the war data, and Belloc's objection

Roberts invites him to make fun of libertarians. Taleb positions himself precisely — *"libertarian at an
Italian school, where I think that nobody should coerce me into something. But, at the same time, like
von Mises, I believe it has limitations."* — and then, with first-hand authority:

> **"I'm not an anarchist. I saw anarchy in Lebanon. We see it in Somalia. We see it in Libya. We know
> what happens when you have no states."**

Roberts asks the direct question — is this a political programme or a description? *"What is the
appropriate scale of the state? What is the appropriate scope of the state?"* Taleb answers with
existing counter-examples rather than a programme, and this is the practical payload:

- **Dubai and Abu Dhabi are city-states**, inside *"a Confederation of City States. They share an army.
  They share an honorific ruler and they share ambassadors."* Three shared functions; everything else
  local.
- **Singapore** — his answer to what the most successful modern country of the last fifty years is, and
  also a city-state.
- **China:** *"if the Chinese want to survive, they should read more about the Bronze Age collapse."*
  The prescription: *"try to build themselves as a confederation of Hong Kongs and Singapores."*

Asked what stops it, the answer is two words: **"Culture. Blind culture."** Then education: French
schooling teaches that Napoleon was great because he made France great — *"are you interested in France
or Frenchmen? Okay? There's a difference."* And the moral cost as an accounting identity: **"the deaths
you have in another country don't count on your battle sheet."** — **"a step in the moral field is a
step backwards in the nation-state."**

**The war data** is the one piece of measured evidence in the episode, from his statistics work with
Pasquale Cirillo: *"We looked, Cirillo and I, we study wars. We studied the dynamics and properties of
wars. How many people died in Italy before unification?"* Hundreds of small wars among the
pre-unification states — *"Some killed three people, sometimes the same area, and then they settle."* —
some killing 15,000. Then:

> **"over 500 years, the number is somewhere between, we think 15,000, under 80,000 people died in wars.
> The first war cost Italy 680,000 soldiers."** [sic — the range sentence is garbled; Roberts confirms
> "First World War".]

Five centuries of parochial wars against four years of one large one, roughly an order of magnitude
apart. Conclusion: **"you realize that nation-states, they like war."**

**Roberts objects immediately, and it is a good objection:** *"aren't you confounding the advances in
technology that made the First World War much more lethal? It wasn't merely that it was waged between
nation-states, right?"* Taleb's answer is one word — **"Maybe."** — followed by a weaker claim than the
one he started with: city-states did wage wars, *"but nothing central."* The confound is not resolved. I
note it as unresolved, because the 680,000 figure is the most citable thing in the episode and it rests
on a comparison Roberts has just holed. What survives is the propagation mechanism, which Roberts
supplies using Taleb's own drink-driving example: harm from a small unit *"is limited"* and does not
spread. Taleb: **"It doesn't generalize quickly... In Italy, it's always over something small and it's
never systemic."**

**Belloc's objection closes it.** Roberts invokes Hilaire Belloc's two-line poem "Pacifism", in which
the man who thought fighting wrong is killed by the man who thought it right, and draws the moral: *"in
a world of nations, a city-state is very vulnerable and there's a natural tendency towards forming a
nation."* Taleb's answer is the one he gave the Phoenicians — buy the defence, keep the sovereignty:
**"I told you NATO. NATO is there. That's the purpose of NATO."** Ukraine before the war was *"like
Hannibal ad portas--Hannibal at the gates. They all saw this monster. Silent monster."*

Two closing observations: small does not mean isolated — *"nationalism works at small scale because you
may not like your neighbors, but you end up dealing with them."* — and Yugoslavia again as the success
nobody scores as one: *"People forget to look at Yugoslavia as the best experience we had in years."*
He then plays devil's advocate against his own answer — *"a lot of people say NATO is an extension of
United States using their colonies, militarily speaking, to do work for them"* — and Roberts adds the
parallel accusation about the EU and Germany. Neither is resolved; both are left on the table.

**And the closing complaint, which is the reason to keep this episode:**

> **"I've spoken to a lot of political scientists over time and not one of them was interested in
> scaling. You just introduce the notion of scaling... and you come up with all these results that are
> obvious and answer a lot of questions that are obvious."**

The example he leaves on the table is the largest possible one: **"Communism failed in the Soviet Union.
Is it because Communism is bad or is it question of scale? Communism works in a kibbutz."** Roberts:
"Yeah, or in the family as we talked about many times." Taleb's last substantive sentence is a statement
of method, modest in a way he rarely is: *"we just translate some of our modest knowledge about
nonlinear response to questions of foreign affairs, political science, and international relations."*

## Synthesis: five claims, in dependency order

The hour is not a theory of politics. It is one property — nonlinearity — pushed through political
units, and it decomposes into five claims that depend on each other in order:

1. **A state is a body of laws, not a people or a territory.** The *medinat/din* etymology is the pivot.
   Once the state is a jurisdiction you accept, it need not match an ethnicity, a language or a landmass
   — and the habit of collapsing state, nation and people into one equation is exposed as an invention
   with a date on it (1780) and a body count.
2. **The cost of governing grows faster than size.** Identity curation, monitoring and the communication
   network all scale super-linearly: connections grow faster than nodes, double the stone and you get
   four times the harm, running Russia is *more than* ten times harder than running a tenth of Russia. A
   mechanical claim, independent of who governs.
3. **Therefore errors do not scale linearly either.** A small unit's mistake is a small mistake; the same
   mistake at national scale is systemic. Italy's five hundred years of parochial wars against one World
   War is the measured version — with Roberts' technology objection still standing over it.
4. **Skin in the game is the local enforcement mechanism, and it only works at village distance.** The
   mayor meets you at the café; the Brussels bureaucrat never does. Shame does not scale, so
   accountability does not scale, so nothing above a certain size is corrected by embarrassment.
5. **But optimal size is domain-dependent, so the answer is confederation, not fragmentation.** Military
   centralises; pandemics centralise; water, bridges and schools do not. A hundred tables makes a
   restaurant large and a chemicals firm tiny. The working models — Switzerland, the American federation,
   the UAE, NATO, the empires at their best — share one shape: **a small number of pooled functions,
   everything else local.**

The line that ties it to the rest of Taleb's work is the survival record. The elephant is individually
stronger and collectively extinct. Big units win encounters and lose centuries.

## Solo-builder read (insights for Jens)

*My own words from here down.*

**Staying small is a position, not a stage.** Size is a risk parameter you set, not a score you
accumulate. Solytics is one person; the default story about that is "not yet big". Taleb's framing says
it is a chosen point on a curve where error cost is low, correction is same-day, and no identity has to
be curated. The honest version of the trade is the one Roberts forces out of him: small units are
individually vulnerable and collectively durable. The goal is therefore not to grow out of small — it is
to buy the protections that make small survivable, the way the Phoenicians did, as a priced premium
rather than as an army.

**The mayor test for every autonomous agent I run.** The Brussels/mayor distinction is directly
implementable in this repo. For each automated thing — the session daemon, the watchdogs, the agent-task
runner, Fabrik — ask: when it fails, who meets the consequence at the café on Sunday? Where the answer is
"nobody, it just logs", that is a Brussels bureaucrat with a bridge. The unit-health-check and creds-check
tools exist precisely to manufacture that encounter: they push the failure onto my phone, where I have to
look at it. Every new automation should ship with its own café.

**Errors that do not scale linearly is a build rule, not a metaphor.** A bug in a one-tenant deployment
costs one correction; the same bug across a shared multi-tenant platform costs a migration, a
notification and a trust repair. That is the doubled stone, and it argues for the confederation shape in
the software: one shared army (auth, deploy, infra), everything else per-tenant and separately breakable.
The Launch Kit tenant model already is that architecture; the discipline is to resist centralising
anything that is not military.

**Domain-dependence kills the generic "should I scale this?" question.** The right question is always
per-function: what is large *for this thing*? Support at solo scale is large at a few dozen paying users;
infrastructure at solo scale is not large at all. A better sizing instrument than headcount or revenue,
and it explains why some parts of the business feel strained long before others.

**The life-to-date P&L is the most portable idea here, and I violate it daily.** Checking Stripe, install
counts and search impressions on a short window is exactly the trader watching an intraday screen: noise
dominates, the emotional swing is real, the information content is near zero. The remedy is stated as
therapy, not insight — look at the life-to-date number. For FinGrab that means the cumulative install and
subscription line, not this week's; for the funnel work, the multi-month view where the signal lives. A
dashboard that defaults to a short window is a machine for manufacturing dissatisfaction.

**Tocqueville inverted, applied to my own reviews.** The parts of the operation I complain about most are
usually the parts working well enough to be visible. The genuinely broken parts are opaque and quiet — no
complaint arrives from a channel that produced nothing, because nothing happened. That maps onto the
"funnel intact but silent" pattern already in memory. Loud dissatisfaction is a transparency signal;
silence is the thing to audit.

**The minimum deliverable unit as a spec heuristic.** The right size for a first release is the size that
can be withdrawn or reversed within a day. Anything larger has begun curating an identity — a migration
path, a documented promise, a user expectation — and identity maintenance is the cost that grows
super-linearly.

### Closing the arc: what survived, what hardened, what he dropped

Against the antifragility conversation ten years earlier
([`2012-01-16-taleb-on-antifragility.md`](2012-01-16-taleb-on-antifragility.md)), in his own material:

- **Survived unchanged: nonlinearity as the single diagnostic.** In 2012 it was the car hitting a wall
  once at speed versus a thousand times slowly, and the coffee cup with no material fatigue. In 2022 it is
  the rabbi's stone, ten metres once versus ten times one metre, and the elephant that is not a large
  mouse. Identical structure, new domain. He dates the programme himself: everything since 2009 has been
  finding these nonlinearities in places.
- **Survived unchanged: skin in the game as a *local* technology.** In 2012 the counter-example to
  reputation-at-scale was the village and church on Sunday; in 2022 it is the café on Sunday and the
  elected local mayor. No better mechanism in a decade, and he does not claim one.
- **Hardened: size moved from bullet to thesis.** In 2012 Switzerland and "forecasting errors grow with
  size" were one item in a list beside debt, overspecialisation and leverage. In 2022 they are the entire
  argument, with a definitional apparatus (nation vs state vs empire), a dataset (the Cirillo war
  statistics) and a positive programme (confederation).
- **Hardened: the prescription turned institutional.** In 2012 the constructive move was the barbell and
  via negativa — personal, portfolio-shaped. In 2022 it is structural: pool the few functions that require
  centralisation, localise everything else, and the model is not a thought experiment but a list of
  existing places — Switzerland, Germany's federation, the American states, the UAE, NATO, Singapore.
- **Dropped, or at least absent: the finance apparatus.** No barbell, no via negativa by name, no options
  talk, no tail-probability mathematics, no Fat Tony, no Seneca. *Fooled by Randomness* returns only as a
  psychology of news consumption. This is Taleb as political theorist rather than risk trader; the
  technical scaffolding stays in the garage.
- **New in 2022: the concession.** He allows that centralisation is genuinely required for military and
  pandemics, cites von Mises and Hayek approvingly rather than as foils, states plainly that he is not an
  anarchist because he has seen anarchy, and answers Roberts' best objection with "Maybe." The 2012 Taleb
  was already self-critical about selection bias; the 2022 one concedes to his interlocutor in real time.
  That is the most interesting change in ten years, and the reason to read this episode rather than
  summarise it.

# Rory Sutherland on Alchemy

> **"I like markets because they're inventive."**
> Sutherland's complaint about economics is not that it defends markets — it is that it
> defends them for the wrong reason. Efficiency is the wrong compliment: competition
> looks wasteful over any short horizon, and what markets actually do that nothing else
> does is **find more than one answer to the same question.** "if you approach business
> problems with the mentality of someone who is trying to make it look like physics, then
> one of the dangers is that you're always trying to optimize something or find the single
> overarching solution that works for the average." Everything else in the hour — Uber's
> moving car, the £15m airline question, the London Overground, the Soviet chandeliers —
> is a worked example of value that a model built for the average cannot see, and
> therefore cannot capture.

Companion note: [`2012-01-16-taleb-on-antifragility.md`](2012-01-16-taleb-on-antifragility.md).
The two men are closer than their subjects suggest and Sutherland says so on air: he
imports Taleb's **minority rule** directly, and his defence of loss aversion is an
argument about **non-ergodicity** — the same asymmetry Taleb builds antifragility on,
arriving from advertising rather than from options trading. Where Taleb says *clip the
left tail first*, Sutherland says *consumers are already doing that, and you keep mistaking
it for irrationality.*

*Transcript note: unlike the 2012 Taleb episode, this one carries clean speaker labels, so
every attribution below is the transcript's own.*

## 1. Why the standard model deletes his entire profession

Roberts opens by admitting the book "takes a lot of cheap shots at economists" and that
he liked it anyway. Sutherland's defence is precise and worth keeping, because it explains
*why* an advertising man ends up arguing epistemology:

> "the assumptions of economic models, which is that people have perfect information and
> make decisions in an atmosphere of perfect trust, are creating a kind of fantasy world
> where marketing and advertising needn't exist."

If desire were independent of context and meaning, "Things would simply sell themselves."
His animus is aimed not at economists but at **"the extent to which a kind of shallow
economic thinking has permeated Business School curricula"** and become the default frame
for every decision.

Roberts concedes the substance and sharpens the tool-vs-description distinction himself:
efficiency is "a tool. And if you take the tool as a perfect description of reality, you
will be grossly misled."

Sutherland's positive claim — the one the rest of the episode keeps cashing out:

- Markets solve by **market-tested innovation**, i.e. trial and error, "But it's a bit
  more than that too... quite often what markets find is more than one solution to the
  same problem. ... They solve the same problem for different people in a different way."
- And the invention "does not only apply to goods themselves but to the marketing and
  presentation of those goods." He goes explicitly Austrian on it, citing Mises: no useful
  distinction between the value created by the cook and by the man who sweeps the floor —
  and he extends it to the man who designs the menu and the signage.

**The Uber case, which is the book's signature:** Uber "is very largely a brilliant
psychological innovation which changes the nature of waiting." Watching the car approach
makes ten minutes "inordinately less frustrating than if you're in a state of ignorance"
— and that is "as significant to Uber's success as anything to do with kind of scale or
economies." Roberts confirms it from his own behaviour: when recommending Uber to his
parents he listed the rational features, then said with far more excitement, "And while
you're waiting, you can see the car getting closer!"

Two refinements that make it usable rather than cute:

- **It is variance, not delay, that hurts.** Waiting for a cab you expect ten minutes but
  carry "a haunting fear in the back of your mind... 'What if it's 25?'" — Sutherland
  adds the worse one: "'What if they canceled me?'"
- **Therefore a pessimistic estimate beats no estimate.** His advice to British Airways:
  any estimated departure time, "even if it's slightly pessimistic, it is inordinately
  better than the single word" *Delayed* — because "'Delayed' effectively makes me think,
  'It's probably canceled but they're trying to break it to me gently.'"

## 2. The intellectual core: variance reduction is rational, not a bias

This is the part that makes the book more than a collection of advertising anecdotes, and
it is the bridge to Taleb.

- **The Control Heuristic** (he credits Luca Dellanna): a very deep part of human
  motivation is maintaining a sense of certainty. "We have an incredible human urge, we
  really, really hate being in a position of uncertainty." It explains the Ellsberg
  paradox — people prefer known bets to unknown ones.
- **Ole Peters and non-ergodicity** (London Mathematical Laboratory): "in a non-ergodic
  environment where utility is to some extent multiplicative, not additive, variance
  reduction, of course, is perfectly rational."
- **Therefore sunk-cost bias and loss aversion may not be biases at all.** Sutherland's
  own prior, stated as a hunch he had before he found the maths: "evolution would have
  corrected them surely if they'd been that consistently bad." And: "if you think of
  fortune as being intertwined and path dependent, then the avoidance of significant
  misfortune is a perfectly rational fear."
- **The arithmetic, in his words:** "If you think about it in very childish mathematics,
  2 x 2 x 2 x 2 x 2 is a bigger number than 1 x 3 times 1 x 3 times 1 x 3. If you're
  adding, variance doesn't matter. If you're multiplying, it does."
- **And the sequencing matters too:** "in a non-ergodic environment, two or three bad
  outcomes in a row are significantly worse than spaced misfortune."

The illustration is the best story in the episode. Francis Fulford's family has held the
same ~10,000 acres in Devon by direct descent since roughly 1240. Asked how, he pointed at
the ancestral portraits: **"we've had loads of idiots. We've had drunken idiots, we've had
gambling idiots, philandering idiots, idiots who get in prison for treason,' he said,
'But we've never had two in a row.'"** Sutherland's gloss: when we look irrational
worrying about a downside, "we're not just factoring in the risk of that outcome but the
risk that it's accompanied by several other downside outcomes."

**The commercial consequence — brands are a variance-reduction instrument.** Nobody buying
a Samsung television believes it is the best possible set at the price; "what I think we
are paying a premium for is the fairly reliable certainty that however good it turns out
to be, it won't be dreadful." Habits and social copying follow from the same motive. The
brain accompanies every question with an unspoken *what's the worst that could happen?*

Roberts reads out the passage that makes it concrete, and it is the best single paragraph
of the book quoted in the hour: men would not order a cocktail because they had "no
foreknowledge of the glass in which it would be served" — a slight chance of a hollowed-out
pineapple and they order a beer. Fixes: pictures on the menu, or serve everything in mason
jars. Same mechanism for why nobody switches bank account or broadband:
**"A 1% chance of a nightmarish experience dwarfs a 99% chance of a 5% gain."**

## 3. Choice architecture: the question you ask determines the answer you get

- **Hire one person vs hire ten.** Ask ten people to make one hire each and you get ten
  conservative, blame-avoiding hires. Ask one person to hire ten and "you're going to go
  much wider and you're going to look for complementarity rather than conformity." So you
  can get **diversity without quotas** by changing the mode of choosing. His evidence: a
  large consulting firm whose graduate intake — hired in groups — is "spectacularly
  diverse," while its partnership, appointed one at a time, is "fairly conformist."
- **Same shape everywhere.** Forced to buy one house you buy a conventional suburban one;
  allowed two, you buy a crash pad by the office and a place on the beach. When two- and
  three-car households became normal, the all-purpose saloon collapsed and people bought
  "more extreme cars." When people shop more often, they go up-market *and* down-market
  and the mid-market retailer gets squeezed.
- **The methodological defence, which is also good advice:** "I make no apology, by the
  way, for looking at trivial consumer behavior: my argument being, you know, nobody
  criticized Darwin for looking at finches' beaks."
- **The £15m question.** Airline booking forms ask destination, date and **class of
  travel** — before showing any prices. "Asking consumers what class of travel they want
  before they know the price difference is, when you think about it, utterly insane." If
  business is £5 more, he is at the front; if it is four times as much, he is in 23D.
  And consumers never say *I don't have enough information* — "They simply go, 'I better
  put "Economy" down.'" One airline Ogilvy worked with was losing **about £15 million a
  year** to that single field. Kahneman's WYSIATI — *What You See Is All There Is* — is
  the underlying mechanism: people answer the question in front of them rather than
  refusing it.

## 4. Minority rule: design for the veto, not for the average

Sutherland takes the idea from Taleb by name and gives it better examples than the
original:

- "Unless you're by the sea, don't open a fish restaurant because there's always one
  person who doesn't feel like eating fish out of any party of six."
- "one of the reasons pizza is so successful as a food is not only that people love it;
  it's that very few people veto it."
- All New Zealand lamb is halal: non-Muslims are not bothered, Muslims will only eat
  halal, so the whole supply goes halal "for purposes of simplicity."
- **McDonald's vs KFC** as the clean contrast: almost nothing on the McDonald's menu is
  polarising, whereas of KFC: "it's much more authentic as a fast food but it has that
  slight discriminating thing, which is, it's harder to find a large party who will all
  agree to go there because there'll always be one or two rejectors."

Roberts supplies the historical version from broadcasting: with one television per
household you had to make something "neutral enough that no one would veto it," and only
multiple sets let content specialise into highbrow and lowbrow instead of "the average
sort of dull choice." Sutherland complicates it nicely — the big flat-screen
*re*-centralised viewing, and now main sets are so large there is no second room for the
old one, which has stalled the upgrade cycle.

He then extends minority rule to speech norms, and this is the most contestable stretch of
the hour: his claim is that a universalist standard demands "you can't serve any food to
which someone somewhere might be allergic," where he would argue for context (a comedy
club is not a job interview). He concedes the counter-case himself — he is happy to forgo
peanuts on a flight for someone at risk of anaphylaxis — so the argument he is actually
making is about *scope* rather than about permission. Recorded 2019; flagged here as
opinion, not as a finding.

## 5. Hedonic opportunity cost: 20% faster vs 20% more enjoyable

Roberts reads the line: **"Making a train journey 20% faster might cost hundreds of
millions, but making it 20% more enjoyable may cost almost nothing."**

The origin is Sutherland's TED joke about the £6bn spent on a faster London–Channel Tunnel
track for Eurostar. His argument was never that speed is worthless — "speed matters in a
very nonlinear way" (he wants Austin–San Antonio, currently one train a day taking over
three hours for 85 miles, done in an hour) — but that spending *all* of it on duration
"suffered very badly from quantification bias. It's very, very easy to quantify and model
time and duration." Cheaper alternatives with comparable effect on whether people take the
train: WiFi, at ~0.1% of the cost. And the joke that made him famous: spend a billion on
supermodels walking the train handing out free Chateau Petrus — "not only would you have
saved £5 million pounds, but people would ask for the trains to be slowed down." (The
figure is garbled in the transcript: against a £6 billion budget the saving he means is
£5 *billion*.)

The commercial history behind it: transatlantic liners competed on the Blue Riband until
the jet engine made speed irrelevant, so **Cunard invented the cruise industry** — "We
basically can't sell on speed anymore. We have to find some other comparative strength."

Which is his definition of what advertising does: **it moves attention, and attention
creates importance.** "what we pay attention to becomes, in our minds, more important
because we're paying attention to it." Hence the alchemical trick of turning a weakness
into a strength by telling a different story about it:

- **Avis, "We're Number Two, so we try harder."** Sutherland's dissection is the sharpest
  piece of craft in the episode: "'We're number two in rental cars' ... **is an ad for
  Hertz**." Availability, outlets, obscure airports — all of that argues for the leader.
  Add four words about effort instead of scale and the same fact reverses sign.
- Roberts: "'With a name like Smuckers, it must be good jam.'"
- Stella Artois in the UK: "Reassuringly expensive."
- And a nice piece of trivia: **Salman Rushdie, as an Ogilvy copywriter, wrote "Fresh
  cream cakes. Naughty but nice."**

Related irritation with pricing: a missed train makes the ticket worth zero, "I think
humans perceive as an injustice," and it creates anxiety people would happily pay to
avoid. His Houston anecdote — empty business seats on tonight's flight priced at $6,000
when he'd have taken $300 — is the same failure: an unsold perishable seat earns nothing,
but the pricing system cannot meet a willing buyer halfway.

## 6. Chesterton's fence, TRIZ, and the transfer of solved problems

Roberts raises **Chesterton's fence** as the counter-argument to Sutherland's own train
proposals: if the improvement is so obvious, why hasn't it happened? Maybe the fence has a
reason. He then supplies the escape hatch himself — rail in the US, UK and Japan is "not
very competitive," so you cannot rely on trial and error having already found the
evolutionarily satisfactory answer.

Sutherland's obverse of Chesterton's fence is **TRIZ**, the Soviet-era inventive
problem-solving method (he credits Altshuller, "a Stalinist era scientist" whose advice
earned him 25 years in the Gulag). Its useful claim, which Ogilvy calls *lateral category
analysis*:

> **"problems have mostly been solved somewhere else, but no one's transferred them from
> one domain to another."**

Worked examples:

- **Biomimicry:** the nose of the Japanese bullet train is modelled on a kingfisher,
  because a birdwatching engineer noticed kingfishers dive without a ripple — it was
  borrowed to stop the trains booming as they entered tunnels.
- **Yield management** is solved in aviation and untouched in rail, and on French
  motorways, where the Calais–Lyon toll is the same at 2 a.m. and at peak.

Roberts adds the economist's joke about the $20 bill on the pavement and the correct
gloss: "someone has to be the first person to pick it up... In less competitive
environments, they can sit around for a little bit longer."

## 7. The strongest idea in the episode: look at what the map leaves out

This is the section to reread. Sutherland generalises from house-hunting into a rule about
all models:

> **"the more people start using them, the more you'll find gains, not in looking what
> the model tells you, but in looking what it leaves out."**

- **The London Tube Map.** Everyone chooses where to live using it, so the arbitrage is in
  what it omits: South London's rail network is dense and invisible on the Underground map,
  so you can buy "insanely undervalued property next to a railway station south of the
  river" with half the commute of somewhere fashionable that *is* on the map. "the reason
  you're getting that bargain is partly because you're using a different model of choice
  to everybody else."
- **And he can prove it.** A set of lines that had run for ~25 years as Silver Link Metro
  was rebranded the Overground and **added to the Tube map**. "When those lines appeared on
  the London Underground Map, usage went up by 400% in the first month alone." His summary:
  **"You've created something like three billion pounds' worth of infrastructure, mostly
  with ink or with the digital equivalent."**
- **His own house:** he lives in a grade-one listed building "partly because architectural
  quality comes very low down the list of parameters people use when searching for
  property. So, I see that as a kind of decision-making arbitrage."
- **The price-demand ziggurat.** You reportedly cannot sell a UK house at £825,000, because
  the portals step in £50,000 increments in that range — searchers at £800k and at £850k
  both miss it. "So you've ended up not with a price demand curve but with a price demand
  ziggurat."
- **The self-description worth stealing:** "I often call what we do 'the science of knowing
  what economists are wrong about.'" Worked instance, KFC Australia: a product wasn't
  selling, the reflex was to drop the price, he suggested raising it — **"Weirdly, when you
  put the price up, demand went up."** His explanation is positional, not magical: people
  visit fast food either seeking a bargain or seeking a treat, and a price in the middle
  satisfies neither. "an economist would never even look there because dogma suggests that
  that's impossible."
- **seat61.com** as the human counter-example to algorithmic routing: every engine sends
  you London→Bordeaux via Paris because it minimises time, ignoring that you must change
  stations, find a Paris taxi and haul luggage; changing at Lille costs an 80-minute wait
  and a 50–100 yard walk. "the reason his website is so essential is he tells you that.
  Whereas, the algorithm never will."
- **And the systemic worry underneath all of it:** are markets intelligent *because* human
  decision-making is messy? "When you actually make choice uniform by dint of forcing
  everybody to go through the same set of questions in the same order, do you actually make
  markets stupider?" His fix, offered half seriously: randomise the Tube map every few
  years; make property portals throw up wildcards that don't match your filters — mimicking
  1978, when you found a house by driving around, seeing a sign, or hearing that "some guy
  called Dave was thinking of selling his house."

## 8. Measurement as the enemy: the McNamara fallacy and the Soviet chandeliers

Roberts names it filters; Sutherland names the pathology **quantification bias**, and
Roberts supplies the eponym: **McNamara**, who optimised the Vietnam kill count "simply
because it's the thing that you can measure not because it's the thing that matters."

The failure mode stated exactly:

> **"you can be left thinking you've made a wonderfully intelligent decision because at
> every point in the journey, you made what seemed like a decision that reflected your
> preferences. But the real preferences you may have were never even asked about it in the
> first place."**

- **The car formula thought experiment:** if everyone bought cars by a single formula
  (economy ÷ acceleration × seats — Roberts: "Number of cup holders"; Sutherland: "That is
  the American heuristic, emphatically"), then within a few years manufacturers would game
  the formula and "cars would be truly awful," because they would reflect the algorithm
  rather than the preferences.
- **The Soviet evidence, which is the punchline:** rivet factories measured on quantity
  produced as many bad rivets as possible; **"Stalin himself actually complained about the
  metrics problem because all the chandeliers in the Soviet Union were absurdly--indeed,
  dangerously--heavy because lighting factories were actually measured on output by weight."**
- **Therefore commodification is not neutral.** "commodities actually, totally inhibit
  innovation. They destroy brands and they also destroy trust." Once beef is *certified*,
  what incentive is there to make better beef? Whereas a brand is **"reputational skin in
  the game that's attached to the thing you make"** — Samsung cannot afford a terrible
  television because the reputational cost outweighs the short-term gain.
- **Trust is an information technology, and scale destroys it.** His reading of
  *It's a Wonderful Life* banking adds the link people miss: not only did the manager know
  every customer and they know him, **all his customers knew each other**, so cheating two
  trivial customers ended him. "Game over."
- **Which rehabilitates intermediaries**, whom economics writes off as friction: "there's a
  difference between 10 people buying from someone once and one person buying 10 times over
  time. And so the intermediary knows that his long-term interest is served by square-dealing
  with the customer." His case: the Marseille fish market, where an attempt to replace
  dealers with a central auction was opposed **by the buyers as well** — the restaurants
  wanted their guy, repeatedly. "Once you make everything a one-shot transaction, it looks
  more efficient than an economic model, but actually once you factor in the informational
  costs, it's not a good idea at all."

## 9. Advertising as costly signalling — the flower and the bee

The closing argument, and the best defence of advertising in the hour, is biological:

> **"a flower is effectively a weed with a marketing budget."**

The bee cannot verify the nectar without visiting, so the plant must emit a *reliable
signal of promise*. **"the very fact that advertising is an upfront cost is a reliable
indicator of seller confidence, because if the flower wasn't expecting the bees to come
back for a second visit, it wouldn't pay it to grow these huge great petals."** Spend only
pays off across repeat visits — so the spend itself is the evidence.

Around it:

- He credits **Robert Frank's *The Economic Naturalist*** for the frame: looking at economic
  behaviour "through the eyes of a naturalist" beats looking at it through the eyes of an
  engineer.
- **Perception is the medium, so stories are part of the product.** TV manufacturers don't
  reproduce the spectrum, they stimulate three cone types — and generate colours "that
  don't exist in physics--of, like, magenta." Since behaviour runs on perception, "designing
  things around stories is as important as designing things."
- **The double standard he wants named:** "we tend to regard any kind of perceptual hack as
  cheating," while "government in particular will resort to economic incentives or law--i.e,
  compulsion or bribery or fines--before they'll even try persuasion." His order:
  **persuasion first, then incentives, then compulsion.**
- **Why norms beat law — the best argument in this stretch.** A price penalty on running the
  tumble dryer in the evening unfairly hits night-shift workers, and people whose neighbours
  sleep under the washing machine. "the glorious thing about persuasion and indeed, social
  norms is that people who have a good reason not to comply don't get vilified." A norm has
  an exemption clause built in; a rule does not.
- Roberts' summary, which Sutherland accepts: **"norms have a flexibility that legislation
  doesn't."**

And the thesis of the book, in Sutherland's own closing words: "the whole book is really
about Chestertonian fences in that it's about behaviors which actually may have a value
even though it's very difficult to make sense of them when looked at through a very narrow
lens... it believes that you should pay regard for long established things" — Roberts:
"Traditions" — "even if you don't fully understand them. Don't chop people's appendixes out
willy-nilly because there probably is a function, as they later discovered."

## Synthesis: the four moves

1. **Ask what the measure leaves out.** Every widely-used model creates a blind spot, and
   the blind spot is where value sits unpriced — the Tube map, the property portal's
   £50,000 steps, the routing algorithm's time-minimisation, the kill count. The move is
   not *measure better* — it is: find the metric everyone is optimising, and go stand where
   they are not looking.
2. **Reduce perceived variance before you improve the average.** Customers are not being
   irrational when they refuse a 5% gain at a 1% risk of a nightmare; in a multiplicative
   world that is correct arithmetic. So: show the car approaching, give a pessimistic ETA,
   put a picture of the glass on the menu, put the brand on the box.
3. **Audit the questions before the offers.** A form, a filter or a menu is a choice
   architecture, and a question asked at the wrong moment silently destroys revenue — £15m
   a year for asking about cabin class before showing prices. People do not object to a
   badly-timed question; they answer it badly and leave.
4. **Buy the cheap perceptual change before the expensive physical one.** 20% faster costs
   hundreds of millions; 20% more pleasant may cost nothing. And an entire rail network can
   be created "mostly with ink."

## Solo-builder read (insights for Jens)

- **"Look at what the map leaves out" is the sharpest version of an idea already in the
  strategy notes, and it is testable this week.** Where everyone searches the same way —
  the Chrome Web Store's ranked list, a job board's filter set, a portal's price brackets —
  the arbitrage is in the attribute nobody filters on. Concrete: which of my products' real
  strengths has no field in the surface people browse? That is either a positioning
  opportunity or a reason nobody finds it.
- **The £825,000 ziggurat is a pricing warning, not a curiosity.** If the surface a buyer
  browses quantises price (store tiers, portal brackets, marketplace filters), a price
  between the notches is invisible regardless of how correct it is. Check the *steps of the
  browsing surface* before choosing a number.
- **KFC Australia: raising the price raised demand, because the price is a position.** A
  product priced in the middle is neither bargain nor treat. Worth holding against a
  27 USD/month subscription and a lifetime tier — which of the two is the item being read
  as, and does the price say the same thing?
- **Advertising as costly signalling reframes the ad spend question.** The point of a paid
  channel is not only the clicks it buys; visible spend is itself the signal that the
  seller expects repeat visits. For a solo product with no brand, that argues for
  *visible* commitment (a real listing, a real site, a price that isn't apologetic) over
  quietly efficient acquisition.
- **The variance argument explains a funnel that is intact but silent better than "weak
  demand" does.** If a prospect's real question is *what's the worst that could happen*,
  then every missing reassurance — no reviews, no refund line, no screenshot of the actual
  output — is a 1% nightmare risk dwarfing a 99% gain. Cheaper than more traffic, and it
  is the same lever as the picture of the cocktail glass.
- **The intermediary passage is an argument for repeat relationships over one-shot sales.**
  "one person buying 10 times over time" is worth more than ten one-shot buyers *to both
  sides*, because it is the only structure where square-dealing pays. Relevant to anything
  sold once and never followed up.
- **Persuasion → incentives → compulsion is a usable order for any behaviour change**,
  including internal ones: try the norm before the rule, because a rule punishes the people
  with a legitimate exception, and a norm doesn't.
- **The trivial-example licence.** "nobody criticized Darwin for looking at finches' beaks."
  Small consumer behaviours are legitimate evidence; a note about why someone didn't click a
  button is not beneath analysis.

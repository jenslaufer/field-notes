# Taleb on the Pandemic

> **"In hindsight, of course, you know what happened. You know that Ebola was not a big deal. ...
> You know that SARS was not a big deal, in hindsight. In foresight, when you're standing facing
> uncertainty, you should behave according to a protocol."**
> This is the episode where the precautionary machinery gets used in public, on a live event, with
> the outcome still unknown. Recorded 3 July 2020 — Taleb dates himself as "what, five months into
> the thing?" and puts the global toll at "about, what, 500,000 people died, today?" Nothing here
> is retrospective. That is its value: a dated position, readable for three separate things — what
> he claimed at the time, what he said was knowable at the time, and where he explicitly refuses
> to say anything.

Companion note: [`2012-01-16-taleb-on-antifragility.md`](2012-01-16-taleb-on-antifragility.md) —
the same speaker eight years earlier, building the framework this episode spends an hour applying.
Together they show the gap between owning a risk model and running it while the event happens.

## 1. Risk of variation is not risk of ruin (1:22)

Roberts opens with his father, and it is a real opening position. Ted Roberts used to say that
"even though there are snakes in the world, it's good to go barefoot so you can feel the grass
between your toes"; he never wore a bike helmet. Roberts is his father's son but also Taleb's
student, having "learned from you, Nassim, about the risk of ruin--that trade-offs become relevant
when you're out of the game, either literally, as in wiped out in gambling, or figuratively, as in
being dead." At 65 he has been "exceptionally cautious", and he asks straight out whether he was
right.

Taleb denies the two are opposed — "caution and prudence are perfectly compatible with your
father's statement" — and the distinction that dissolves it carries the rest of the hour.
Variation is not ruin: "the central idea that risks of variation is extremely different from risk
of ruin, particularly at a systemic level" [sic]. Car accidents moving up or down does not change
society's ruin risk. The *Antifragile* image: "if I jump one meter, I'm probably going to get
stronger. Actually, if I don't jump often, I will get weaker. But, if I jump 20 meters--you know,
it won't happen." And the absorbing barrier, from the last chapter of *Skin in the Game*: "there
is a difference between medium-size variations at individual level and systemic at the system
level. You must survive."

Then the sentence almost nobody quotes — the licence half of the doctrine, not the prohibition
half:

> **"what I've tried to do with my collaborator is figure out what are the systemic risks we
> should be avoiding, which is liberating because it allows us to take a lot of risks elsewhere."**

Precaution here is not a disposition toward safety. It is a *classification job* whose output is a
very short list, and the point of the list is everything left off it.

## 2. Why contagion breaks the individual/collective split (~3:00)

Roberts presses again on his own risk, not society's. Taleb denies the two can be separated for a
contagious disease, pointing at a follow-up piece written with Joe Norman and naming Sam Harris —
unprompted, against Roberts trying to keep names out — as an "archenemy" who read it and agreed.

**"It is very selfish for you to not reduce your risk, because a contagious disease has different
properties."** Somebody had produced the statistic that road accidents were the larger risk; Taleb
concedes it was true at the time, but the comparison is invalid: "if you don't behave
conservatively, you will increase that risk dramatically because the collective risk doesn't scale
linearly from individual risk, unlike other risks that do effectively have some scale
transformation."

Masks are the example, and at this stage the argument is arithmetic and ethical, not
epidemiological: "I wear a mask not for myself, but because a person I'm going to infect will
infect maybe 10 more", possibly twenty. Roberts supplies the variance — "Depends if they sing in a
choir or go to a urologist" — and Taleb closes it: "you wear a mask not just to protect yourself,
but for the systemic effect." Underneath sits the scaling claim from their 2018 conversation:
**"some things may be perfectly rational for society, but totally irrational for any individual in
it."** Hence "it becomes a duty, sometimes, to act against your own interest". Roberts dates the
episode as precisely as any timestamp by noting a "moment in American history deeply depressing to
me, where wearing a mask has become a partisan, ideological issue rather than a scientific issue."

## 3. The two convexities the bureaucracies missed on masks

The first concrete technical accusation, with two parts worth grading separately — one arithmetic,
one an empirical conjecture in the subjunctive.

**Barriers multiply.** The WHO and CDC were initially against mask-wearing, and "they did not think
that if I wear a mask and you wear a mask, we're not reducing the risk by P, the protection, by a
mask, but by P-squared." His illustration: "if I have 50% probability of being infected wearing a
mask, and you have 50%, the total would be .25". This needs no data, only independence, which he
does not defend.

**Dose-response is convex at the bottom** — "even more central": "if I reduce the viral load by
half, I don't necessarily decrease the probability of infection by half. I may decrease it by 99%".
The mechanism is the curve's shape: "There is an S-curve. So, if you're in a left side of the
S-curve, it must be that you're convex. Everything is convex initially, and then concave at the
end." His charge: "these are the things that were missed by people who analyzed masks, initially,
scientifically. They just looked at viral load reduction."

Note what is *not* claimed. He produces no measurement of mask efficacy. He says a linear reading
of a partial-protection number understates the effect *if* the response is convex, and the "may"
in the sentence about 99% is load-bearing. Roberts's cost side belongs here — the mask "is a little
bit uncomfortable. It's a little bit hot. It costs $8. It's a bargain." — and Taleb objects to the
price: "You can make masks for 45 cents", "coffee filters work".

## 4. The January note, and why the team was already watching (9:21)

Roberts dates it: "back in January of this year, January 26, you wrote a paper with Yaneer Bar-Yam
and Joe Norman warning about the pandemic." The answer establishes why this episode is a *test*
rather than a reaction — the warning was the output of a standing watch, not a hunch.

- **"Our team was basically monitoring for pandemics because, in my opinion, they are the fattest
  tails"** — because "we haven't had many pandemics in history, but we've had very lethal ones.
  They represent an existential risk."
- The instruction that follows from membership in that class: **"you have to stop some things in
  the egg, when it's cheap to do so."**
- The precedent is Ebola, where the public argument was lost: "They were comparing a process that
  is multiplicative to risks that were in a Gaussian distribution" — pandemics set against "people
  dying in their swimming pool, people falling from ladders, and stupidities like that."

Roberts produces the cleanest statement of multiplicative versus additive risk in the hour, and it
is his: **"when I drown in my bathtub, you don't drown in your bathtub as a result."** Taleb's own
test runs through the extreme value instead of the mechanism: "If I tell you a billion people died,
you know they did not die drowning in their bathtub in any given year." Given only the magnitude,
"odds are it's going to be either a nuclear war or a pandemic. More likely to be a pandemic."

His complaint about the literature is structural, not a disagreement over findings: "this is
completely absent: the fat-tailedness and the scaling are absent from the Decision Science
literature, which effectively makes people doing the right thing look like they're irrational."
Then the dated sentence at the centre of the episode: **"we knew that from the Ebola days, four or
five years ago. And, we were waiting for the next pandemic. And, sure enough, we saw the sign. In
January."** Alongside it, the paper with Cirillo — "in preparation for a paper that came out in
Nature: Physics with Cerillo, showing, effectively, that pandemics are the fattest-tailed thing,
and that you got to kill them in the egg" [sic — *Nature: Physics*, *Cerillo*].

## 5. Quarantine as 300-year-old standard operating procedure

The history does two pieces of argumentative work: it makes the protocol old and tested rather than
novel, and it isolates the one variable that has actually changed.

Reading history for the Cirillo paper, he found the Old World "dealt with pandemics in a very
effective way. It was part of the economics of the time." Against the picture of a plague as an
event that arrives and vanishes: "The previous plague stayed. And, it was accelerated by commerce
along the Silk Road." It stayed 300 years, and people built around it. The worked case is one ship.
In 1720 a vessel left Smyrna with cargo from the East and sailed for Genoa. "The Genovese didn't
let it in because they had something called lazaretto, which was a quarantine for all ships." The
ship was owned by the Mayor of Marseilles, who took it home and bypassed his own city's rules.
**"Half the population of Marseilles perished."**

The mechanism, in four sentences, is as close to a protocol as anything in the hour: **"The ship
comes in. You have suspicions. You don't take chances. You refuse the ship."** Roberts supplies the
name — a standard operating procedure — and Taleb takes it. Black Death cities shut out foreigners
and put returning residents in lazarettos; the Ottoman-Habsburg border quarantined in both
directions; *quarantine* is forty, and meant forty days. Told modern practice is two weeks, Taleb
waves the difference away: "two weeks works. I mean, two weeks is good enough. I mean, whatever
reduction of connectivity is very good."

**What changed is connectivity, not the pathogen.** "we have more connectivity today than we did
before, so therefore things that are multiplicated will be vastly more multiplicated today" — the
winner-take-all dynamics that made a few opera singers rich, running now on disease: "you would
have a winner-take-all disease taking over the planet. And, we have super-spreader events that
cause even more fat tails." His image is a urology conference in Las Vegas, where "what happens in
Las Vegas doesn't stay in Las Vegas, gets redistributed to 200 countries." The Silk Road moved at
"30 kilometers a day maximum". Conclusion: "you don't have natural limits to things."

## 6. What he asked for in January — including what he got wrong (18:03)

- **Systematic quarantines, not targeted ones:** "We wanted systematic quarantines, because the
  fact of having quarantine for people coming from China is foolish, not knowing that they can come
  from somewhere else."
- **Remove the WHO** — the harshest institutional line in the episode: "It's a bureaucratic
  organization that has been devastating for mankind because of its incompetence. They prevented
  people from wearing masks."
- Roberts raises the CDC's later admission that its anti-mask advice was driven by fear of a
  shortage, which if true means they lied. Taleb rejects even that charitable reading: **"It's not
  just that they lied. They're incompetent. They didn't understand the compounding."** The second
  failure is an economics one rather than a virology one — they assumed only government could
  supply masks, when "people would find stuff in their closets that would work perfectly as a
  barrier, and we wouldn't be here."

**Then he indicts himself, unprompted — the most important passage for anyone reconstructing what
he actually said at the time:**

> **"we thought of the mask situation late in the game. Initially we bought the argument that it
> was not transmitted by that mode. We thought it wasn't contagious."**

He was not an early mask advocate. What turned him was not a study but a statistical oddity: "why
is it that Taiwan, that has a lot of traffic from Wubai" [sic — *Wuhan*] "was completely spared?"
Roberts adds Hong Kong. Only then did he apply the convexity argument and conclude the analysts had
missed it — "So, was very embarrassing."

## 7. "There's no evidence masks work" — and what science is for (~20:00, 22:41)

Against "there's no evidence masks work. Don't use them.": **"Hence, wear it, if you don't know."**
The general principle: "The whole idea of the Incerto, the central idea of the Incerto, is that when
you have uncertainty in a system, it makes your decision-making much, much easier." Taleb
demonstrates with the water in front of him — if "I'm not certain about the quality of this water",
do you drink it? — and Roberts converts it: "if there's a 1% risk that the water has the bubonic
plague in it, you don't drink 1% of it" — **"You just don't drink it. You go to zero. You go to the
corner, we call it in economics."** Which produces the line about expertise: ordinary instinct is
"vastly superior to what the CDC had, what a lot of scientists, what these decision-making
professors, the Nudge group in the United Kingdom."

**Roberts's own contribution is sharper than it looks.** He points at an inconsistency in how risk
compensation gets deployed: "If you tell people that if you wear a seatbelt you might drive less
carefully, they laugh at you" — and yet the same people reached for exactly that argument against
masks (you will touch your face, you will feel safer than you are). His question is the one the
section turns on: "Why is clinical data the only kind of evidence that is acceptable?"

Taleb's answer is the sentence he says summarises twenty years of work, produced in his debate with
John Ioannidis: **"Science is not about evidence. Science is about properties."** Unpacked: "if you
have absence of evidence, all right, it's still scientific to describe a process." Roberts
describes the process — it enters through the nose, it leaves through the nose, cover both noses —
and Taleb accepts it as science: "It is scientific. It's scientific to say, 'Let's wear masks.'"
What fails is an impoverished version of science: "this naive, evidence-based science is not there
in sophistication yet, to grasp that." **"Science is about understanding the properties of stuff"**
— "which may or may not include forecasting."

Roberts supplies the reductio: "There's no clinical data on whether it's unsafe to jump from an
airplane without a parachute at 35,000 feet." Taleb converts it into a decision rule — "And, you
understand asymmetry" — spelled out as "the harm you're going to get from wearing a parachute is
vastly inferior, potentially, than the benefits you may get for it." The transcript prints his term
as "payoff assymetry" [sic].

**The distinction to hold:** he never claims evidence that masks work. He claims the decision does
not require it, because the cost is small, bounded and known while the payoff is unbounded and
unknown. That is an argument about the shape of the payoff, and it survives being wrong about the
mechanism.

## 8. Two graphs: why the average is not the typical (26:23, 32:24)

Roberts sets up the forecasting section with a casualty: Neil Ferguson in England — he stops to
distinguish him from his Hoover colleague Niall Ferguson — who "said that up to 2 million people
could die from the pandemic". Roberts dismisses the hedge himself: "Which is meaningless, of
course. It doesn't mean anything." Taleb shares his screen and answers with the paper from the
Ioannidis debate, "On Single-Point Forecasts for Fat-Tailed Variables".

**Graph one, a log-normal**, deliberately not an extreme case: "I have a probability distribution
that is very skewed. Actually, it's not an unusual probability distribution. It's common,
log-normal, which is not the worst." The average is slightly above 7 — but "you only observe the
average 15% of the time. Something above the average", 85% falls below it, half of all observations
below 1. Roberts names where the wrong intuition comes from: "Most of our casual, non-real,
academic explorations of risk are based around classroom exercises like flipping a coin." In that
world "if you flip a coin a thousand times, you're going to get between 400 and 600 heads most of
the time." Carry it here and "you're going to make some horrible mistakes."

**Graph two, fatter still.** "the average for this distribution" — "if it exists, assuming it
exists" — "will come from one observation. So, you can have a million observation. One will
determine the average." Wealth is the intuition pump: "if you take the wealth in America, you
realize that 1% of Americans own 50% of the wealth". Pandemics share the shape: "These pandemics
have effected, are very, very concentrated" [sic], and "only one single observation is above the
average". *(Here he says 85% are above the average, inverting the first plot — a slip.)*

The consequence bans two operations at once: **"It's important to realize that you can't work with
averages, and you cannot work with forecasting."** Extreme value theory still gives "representation
of reality, but it's not describable by an average, nor can it be captured by testing people's
forecasting." Roberts's river is the folk version: "you don't care about the average depth of the
river if you're going to walk across it and you can't swim. You care about where it is deepest."
The punchline for pandemics: "if the real average that can come from pandemics is something like 2
billion, more; or 3 billion" then "99% of the time you will observe something under a million." —
**"Which is why we should not forecast numbers."**

## 9. The symmetric verdict — and it cuts both ways (35:46)

The passage most often mangled in Taleb's favour. Roberts builds the scenario: a modeller
simulates, reports a mean of a billion deaths, and then only a hundred thousand die. Is he an
idiot? Taleb condemns the forecast **and its critics in the same breath**:

> **"We said, 'You're an idiot to forecast,' and 'He's an idiot to critique your forecast.'"**

He is not saying the modellers were wrong because the deaths came in low. He is saying that
producing the point estimate and scoring the point estimate are both invalid operations on this
distribution. Anyone quoting him for *the models were wrong, look at the numbers* is using him
against his own sentence. Roberts's restatement is the practical one: **"'Expected' doesn't mean an
'average'; don't mean 'typical,' here."**

What replaces forecasting is not silence but a different measured quantity. "Science is not about
accounting. It's not bean counting. Science is about understanding a phenomenon." The existence
proof is Dutch: living below sea level, "they are worried about the level of the water. They are
not interested in the average. They're interested in the maximum." The discipline is "a whole
science of extrema coming from statistics, applied in insurance, applied in risk management,
applied in finance"; the quantity is the expected maximum, "which is different from the average and
different from the maximum" — the technique he and Cirillo applied "to some classes of
phenomena--namely, wars and the fattest of fat tails of all, pandemics."

He also pre-empts the cry-wolf charge, and that pre-emption is the best definition of the
precautionary principle in the hour. "when we started warning about pandemics in January, it was
not the cry-wolf variety" — the message was to worry about *this* and stop worrying about
everything else. Against the objection that his rules mean never leaving the house: **"The entire
point of the precautionary principle is to replicate what that old trader told me"** — "Take all
the risks you want, but make sure you're in tomorrow", which is "how Goldman Sachs, taking a lot of
risks, have survived, so far, 161 years." He points at Ole Peters and ergodicity for the formal
version, and at the gap it fills: "Standard economics has flaws in it because they don't taken into
account this absorbing barrier" [sic].

Roberts adds why bad practice persists — "a lot of modeling takes place because it's convenient,
not because it's right" — and Taleb blocks the anti-quantitative reading: **"We're not against
modeling. We're against naive modeling."** The technical volume published the day of recording,
*The Statistical Consequences of Fat Tails*, argues that "a lot of things that people claim come
out of statistical models, don't come out of statistical models. For example, linear regression.
You cannot do it with fat tails. So, it's not science." He counts "18 violations", including
regression without Gauss-Markov and "Psychologists use correlation. They cannot use correlation
when you have non-linear effects". Roberts connects it to Ed Leamer's "Let's Take the Con Out of
Econometrics" and Taleb accepts the link.

## 10. The thousand-death class boundary (41:59)

Having refused forecasting, he needs a trigger that requires no forecast. This is it — a
*classification* rule, not a prediction:

- **"It means that you don't have to worry about anything that hasn't killed yet a thousand
  people."** Below the line, nothing: every virus in town is irrelevant.
- Above it everything changes at once: "The minute it kills--if it's in the category that it has
  killed more than a thousand people, then you have to worry about that." Roberts offers "it could
  be a fat-tailed monster"; Taleb upgrades the mood: "It is a fat-tailed monster."
- The action attaches to the crossing, not the estimate: "You have to kill it in the egg" — "as
  we're approaching a thousand."
- **The number is admitted arbitrary, and that is a feature.** "There's this magic number, whatever
  it is. It could be 200, 300 people." The heuristic is "let's study the class of those that have
  killed more than a thousand people in today's population equivalent in history." It exists to be
  cheap and unambiguous, not accurate. It classifies downward too: "the reason we use the number
  killed, because you have a lot of pandemics that are not that consequential, like the common
  cold."

**What he says is knowable about COVID at this date is almost nothing**, and he says so four times:
"First of all, we know nothing about COVID-19. We're, what, five months into the thing?" — "most
infected people were infected recently because it's still growing" — "We know nothing about
morbidity." — "But that's the whole idea, is we know nothing. We know very little about COVID
today." On long-term harm he is agnostic in the direction of caution: "A lot of young people have
damaged lungs. We don't know", and "you cannot rule out the effect on young people."

Roberts confesses the error the heuristic prevents: Ebola and SARS came and went, and "it lulled
people like myself into a false sense of security." Taleb removes the discrimination entirely —
"The idea that you should treat, as we warned in January, absolutely all pandemics the same way",
because "you should treat them all as something that can potentially mutate, once you enter that
class". Asked what was different about this virus, he denies the premise: "It was not. It was not
different." Ebola "kills people faster and more effectively", but that was not knowable in advance
and is not stable — "this and Ebola can change." Where the argument lands:

> **"In hindsight, of course, you know what happened. You know that Ebola was not a big deal. ...
> You know that SARS was not a big deal, in hindsight. In foresight, when you're standing facing
> uncertainty, you should behave according to a protocol."**

Roberts closes the statistical loop: "The average of the past 12 pandemics is a poor predictor of
what to do, to expect of the next one."

## 11. "It's not fighting the pandemic that's costing money. It's the pandemic." (~45:00)

The most contested claim of the hour, and the place to be most careful about what is argument and
what is assertion.

> **"One thing I question is that people believe that fighting the pandemic is costing us money.
> It's not fighting the pandemic that's costing money. It's the pandemic."**

The argument is a counterfactual: strip out the mandates and the contraction happens anyway,
because people are rationally afraid. He builds it by interrogating Roberts — restaurant in New
York City? bar? — and Roberts says no to both, then qualifies (masked, at distance; outdoors at a
brewery the day before, which Taleb accepts). The mechanism runs through operating leverage, not
fear alone: "So people have risk aversion. They don't want to get on a plane that has 1%
probability of crashing or an unknown probability of a crash", and margins are thin enough that
mild aversion is fatal — **"all it takes in New York City for some restaurants is a drop of 20% of
revenues. That's all it takes for them to go bust."** The airline version, attributed to the
chairperson of Delta: "the plane is full, we only make money on the last four passengers."
Conclusion: "you realize that a lot of the economic contraction that we have is a result of
uncertainty."

**Roberts agrees on direction** and supplies the strongest version himself: "Not so important. We
shut it down. Most people shut down. We shut ourselves down." Where he *does* push back is
magnitude, later, when Taleb overreaches. Taleb: "the idea that it's cost you money is bogus
because you can do it at very cheap costs." Roberts: "Well, it does cost you money, but there's
different ways--there's different amounts, depending on what you choose." Taleb concedes, and the
concession converts a claim into a preference: "It does cost you money, but I don't think it
should."

Note the evidentiary base: two anecdotes and an appeal to the listener's introspection. The
risk-management claims earlier do not rest on data by design. This one would need data and does not
have it.

## 12. The protocol, and why it is explicitly not a lockdown (49:11)

Roberts asks for the protocol directly, and the answer contains the sentence that most cleanly
contradicts how Taleb's 2020 position is usually summarised: **"mechanism for quarantine, reduction
of--and identify super-spreaders. You don't have to lock down things."**

- **Target the super-spreading settings, not the population.** "Super spreaders are subways,
  elevators, and big gatherings like the Trump convention." Roberts adds weddings and rallies.
- **Masks buy the activity back:** "you can attend a game, but everybody should wear masks." The
  blunt default applies only while ignorant — "when you don't know, you have quarantines and
  elimination of super spreaders, till we figure out what's going on."
- **The federal job is the border, not the interior:** "the Federal government's job is not domestic
  quarantine. It's international travel. At least check people coming in." Then "you quarantine
  people who are suspicious cases." He notes they were not doing it, and still were not.
- **States and towns do the rest.** "states should probably do the same. You're allowed to block New
  York City", as some Italian towns had; New York was quarantining arrivals from Florida and Texas,
  partly in reaction to Florida quarantining New Yorkers. **"The Plague was a victory of localism.
  Some towns fared a lot better than others."**
- **The budget argument.** "We spend a trillion dollars in America, okay--give or take a hundred
  billion or so, no?--on defense", which shows "we are not willing to gamble with certain things
  without security"; yet "we don't have to spend as much for pandemics. And, pandemics are vastly
  more dangerous than wars." Roberts's own complaint lands in the same place — "The government did
  an atrocious job in the United States" — and Taleb sharpens it: "we are still not able to have
  proper testing and/or proper quarantine."

The objective function is not elimination: **"Small, little measures like these--you don't need to
bring cases to zero. You just want to make sure they don't overwhelm your system and that they
don't collapse your infrastructure."** He expects it to end as the old ones did — "pandemic will be
imploding just like the Plague or the Spanish Flu, naturally" — but not by the mechanism then being
debated: **"I don't believe in herd immunity as much as in reduced connectivity."**

## 13. Antifragility applied: what he predicts will change (53:58)

The one stretch where he forecasts freely, and it is about institutions and behaviour rather than
the disease. "We're antifragile. I mean, we learned from this. It's like when a plane crashes, the
probability another plane crashes drops." The scale marker is easy to miss: "this is not the big
one, by the way. The big one can be some antibiotic-resistant bacteria coming out of hospitals,
something crazy. So, now we know how to handle it." Asked how confident: "I am very confident."

His evidence is the call he is on — Roberts had never wanted Zoom — plus his own institute going
online and, generalised, education: "The university system is going to adapt to it, and it'll be
lot cheaper to get an education, because you can stay in India and get a class from any person in
the world." Damage assessment: travel, tourism, recreation — but "some industries are suffering,
but not too many industries are suffering." This is the least hedged material in the episode and
the only part making a checkable prediction about the world rather than about the shape of a
distribution.

## 14. The question he refuses to answer (~56:00)

Roberts asks the question a listener would actually have: he wants to visit his 87-year-old mother,
who lives alone in Alabama, and "I feel very uneasy about getting on an airplane, and potentially
picking up the virus in that airplane, and killing her." Will two masks make it safe?

**Taleb declines to say.** "I've been looking at data. And, again, I don't want to make a statement
now" — and Roberts names why: "That would go against what you just spent an hour talking about."
What he offers instead is explicitly a hint, not a finding: a salon case where "two infected people
and then, an exposure of 15 minutes or however it takes, that led to zero contagion", plus the
observation that aggregation has not happened — "soon we will have more data, when we examine those
employees who spend a lot of time on airplanes." His refusal is about the state of the evidence,
not about the world: **"It's just it's not yet put together in a way that I'm comfortable
discussing."**

Then he acts anyway, with the exposure on himself: he is flying to Lebanon to see his own mother,
"which entails a much longer exposure--I'm going to hop on a plane." Two masks, an N95, other
measures. On taking the mask off to drink: "If you're seated far from other passengers, the
exposure is not linear." Roberts stops the show to disclaim it — "Do not accept this as medical
advice", "Nassim is not a doctor. He only plays one on EconTalk". The scaling point returns, aimed
at clinicians: "people think that understanding medicine, which is understanding a human, single
human, allows you to understand the collective." Roberts gives the image: "If you want to figure
out how to gamble, don't talk to the carpenter who built the roulette wheel."

**This is the clearest demonstration in the hour of the separation he has insisted on throughout.**
On the risk-management question — take a cheap precaution under uncertainty — he is absolute. On
the adjacent empirical question — is this flight safe for your mother — he will not answer, names
his evidence class instead, and takes the risk himself rather than recommending it.

## 15. Geronticide: the trade-off he refuses to price (59:00, 1:04:21)

Roberts tries to summarise and close; Taleb says he has two more points, and the second is what he
came to say. Roberts flags dissent in advance — "Although I think I disagree with you on this" —
which makes the passage worth reading carefully.

The claim: "The ancient world not only did not favor geronticide, but, to the contrary, they
worshipped the elders." The etymology carries it — "the Senate is a council of elders. Senatus is
an old person", and *sheikh* means old. In such societies "they had values that, basically, for
them, geronticide is never an option. Never." The demonstration is a negotiation designed to be
refused:

> **"if I give you a thousand dollars, would you kill your grandfather? Of course not. Now how
> about if I give you a billion? Okay. So, now let's negotiate."**

The offence is not the price, it is entering the auction. Against the calculus that "if we let old
people die, it'll save us x in economic terms": **"That's not a question that you're allowed to ask
in a classical society. It's offensive."** And flatly: "There are trade-offs you don't make."

He then supplies a second, consequentialist argument for the same conclusion — the more interesting
one, because it rests on no values at all: "for the tail risk, you have no trade-off", and "even if
you look at it from a utilitarian standpoint, it's not a trade-off, because you don't know where
it's going to stop." You cannot price the exchange because you do not know the size of what you are
buying. Then ergodicity applied to people rather than portfolios, the sharpest turn in the section:
"it's not the elderly, not a different class. The young people--you got to think ergodically--will
be the elderly." Protecting the old is therefore not a transfer between groups but a message across
time — "we're telling the young, by taking care of the elderly" that the same will be done for
them. The closing image is the Athenian mules: "the Greeks let the mules that were used in building
the Parthenon--they gave them privileged grounds instead of letting them die because you don't need
them anymore." What is transmitted is "a transmission of intergenerational gratitude that people
don't get."

**On the announced disagreement, be honest about the record: it never happens.** Roberts says twice
"There's no trade-off", which reads as restatement rather than rebuttal, then moves on. The episode
has one declared disagreement and it goes unargued.

## 16. Coda: authenticity as a habit (1:04:21)

Roberts links the mule to "your insight of soul in the game", defending "the idea that you honor
everything that is worth honoring" even when it is not, in his word, rational. Taleb's version is a
film prop: "There's an idea that Visconti, the filmmaker--when he handed the box that contained
jewels, made sure there were jewels inside of it." Nobody could see them. "You're not supposed to
see them, but he made sure it was authentic." The rule — "You should never be an imposter" —
extends to writing about probability without knowing it. Roberts's gloss is the transferable form:
"practicing authenticity, even in places where it's not observable, builds a habit that will
protect you."

## Synthesis

**The load-bearing move is a sort, not a forecast.** Everything operational happens before any
measurement: put the risk in a class (multiplicative or additive, ruin-shaped or variation-shaped)
and let the class determine the action. "you have to stop some things in the egg, when it's cheap
to do so" is executable only because the classifier runs without data. The thousand-death line is a
classifier with a deliberately arbitrary threshold. The Genoese lazaretto is a classifier —
suspicion, not diagnosis, refuses the ship. Accept that the sort comes first and the rest is
bookkeeping.

**Three registers, graded differently:**

| Register | Examples | How firmly asserted |
|---|---|---|
| **Risk management** | ruin vs variation; collective risk does not scale linearly; no single-point forecast on a fat tail; act on asymmetry, not evidence | flatly, needing no COVID data, unchanged from his earlier work |
| **Empirical** | convexity of dose-response; that fear rather than mandates drove the contraction; that institutions will handle the next one better | hedged ("I may decrease it by 99%"), sourced by anecdote, or confident without support |
| **Refusals** | how bad COVID will be; whether Roberts should fly to his mother; morbidity in the young | explicit, repeated, defended as a position rather than an evasion |

Reading a claim from one register as if it sat in another is where the misquotation starts.

**What he actually said, against what he is popularly said to have said:**

- *That he called this a Black Swan, or spent the episode insisting it was not.* Neither: the phrase
  never appears in that connection here. The substance is a standing watch on pandemics as the
  fattest-tailed category, published on in January — a monitored risk, not a surprise.
- *That he demanded lockdowns.* "You don't have to lock down things." The targets are
  super-spreading settings, borders and localism; the objective is explicitly not elimination —
  "you don't need to bring cases to zero."
- *That he said the modellers were wrong because the deaths came in lower.* The opposite,
  symmetrically: "You're an idiot to forecast," and "He's an idiot to critique your forecast."
- *That he was an early mask advocate.* He says on air that he was not: "we thought of the mask
  situation late in the game."
- *That precaution means minimising risk generally.* It is the trader's rule — "Take all the risks
  you want, but make sure you're in tomorrow" — and its purpose is permission: identifying the
  systemic risks "is liberating because it allows us to take a lot of risks elsewhere."

**The sentence that generalises furthest** is not about pandemics: "when you have uncertainty in a
system, it makes your decision-making much, much easier." Uncertainty removes options from the
table rather than adding them, provided the downside is ruin-shaped. You do not drink 1% of the
water.

## Solo-builder read (insights for Jens)

*(My own words from here down. The transferable core: cheap early action against a ruin-shaped risk
beats accurate late measurement — plus the corollary, that most risks are not ruin-shaped and
deserve no precaution at all.)*

**Do the sort first, and keep the list brutally short.** The liberation clause is the part to steal.
Almost everything in a one-person software business is variation-shaped: a dead month, a channel
returning nothing, a churned subscriber, a failed launch. None of it is ruin. A very small set is
ruin-shaped and worth writing down rather than carrying as unease: a store account ban on the
platform carrying a product's entire revenue; loss of the only copy of production data; a
credential expiring unnoticed and taking a payment path with it; a tax or legal exposure that
scales past the cash balance; a health event with no cover. Four or five items. Everything off that
list gets no precaution, no hedging and no worry budget — that is the trade the doctrine actually
offers, and taking only the caution half is the standard misreading.

**Cheap-and-early beats accurate-and-late, and the cost curve is the argument.** The reason to act
in the egg is not that early action is more likely to be right; it is that early action is nearly
free and late action is not. The health checks already running in this repo are exactly that shape —
credentials, units, and the paywall check that asks whether a FinGrab user can still buy. Each was
bought before there was evidence of the failure it prevents, and each costs almost nothing per day.
The right question for the next one is never "has this failed before?" — that is the evidence-based
error — but "if this fails silently, how many days until I notice, and what does the delay cost?" A
silent failure with a long detection lag is the ruin shape in miniature, and the watchdog is the
lazaretto.

**Set the class boundary in advance, then stop re-estimating.** His thousand deaths is a tripwire
chosen for cheapness, not accuracy. The solo equivalent is defining, while calm, the number that
moves something from "watch" to "today's whole day": a revenue drop of X% sustained for N weeks, a
client above some share of income, a dependency with no fallback. The value is not the number — it
is that crossing it triggers action without another round of analysis, and the moment of crossing is
exactly when judgement is worst and the data thinnest.

**The asymmetry test, with both halves.** Is the cost bounded, known and small? Is the payoff
unbounded and unknown? Both yes: act, and stop arguing about the evidence. That is parachute logic,
and it covers backups, a second payment path, a written runbook, a second income channel, a cheap
listing on another platform. But the first half does the real work: a rewrite, a new framework, a
new platform commitment all have *unbounded* cost, so the same argument does not license them. The
test lets very few things through, which is why it is useful.

**Compute the one number in the hour that is actually computable.** A New York restaurant dies from
a 20% revenue drop because the margin is thin. The equivalent question for Solytics has an exact
answer and needs no forecast: what percentage drop in monthly income, sustained for how many months,
ends the runway? That is a measurement of present fragility, available today, and worth more than
any projection of next year's revenue. If the answer is uncomfortably small, the fixes are
subtractive — remove fixed costs, extend the buffer — not additive.

**Judge past decisions by exposure, not by outcome.** When a launch, a channel or a client outcome
is drawn from a fat-tailed distribution, "did it hit the number?" teaches superstition from a sample
of one — and one observation determines the average, which is precisely why it teaches nothing. The
retro question that survives: was the downside bounded, and was the option cheap? A cheap bet that
paid nothing was still correct; an expensive bet that happened to pay was still a mistake. Applied
to the agent-task and Fabrik pipelines: a PR that shipped nothing useful is not evidence against the
channel, but a process that can lose work silently is — regardless of what it produced last week.

**Refuse forecasts out loud, and say what would change your mind.** He will not tell Roberts whether
the flight is safe; he names the evidence class he is waiting for and then takes the risk himself
rather than recommending it. A usable template for status reporting: state the action taken, state
the evidence that would move it, and do not manufacture a point estimate to sound decisive. A number
invented for confidence's sake outlives the conversation and gets quoted back as if it had been
measured.

**Where the dated position can now be scored** — the calibration worth keeping. The structural calls
held: reduced connectivity mattered more than herd immunity; the protocol he described (borders,
targeted measures, localism, cheap mitigations) is roughly what worked where anything worked; and
his insistence that this was not the big one was correct. The institutional calls did not: "we're
antifragile, we learned from this" and the confidence that the next one would be handled competently
have not obviously been earned, and "not too many industries are suffering" reads badly against what
followed in travel, hospitality and commercial property. **Claims about the properties of a system
aged well; claims about how institutions would respond to those properties aged badly.** Discount
confident predictions of organisational learning — including my own about my own processes.

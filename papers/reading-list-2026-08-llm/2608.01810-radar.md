# RADAR — a rubric's criteria are fewer numbers than they look

A rubric with ten named dimensions is assumed to deliver ten signals. It usually does not.
Two criteria can be semantically distinct and still **behaviorally coupled**: push one and the
judge moves the other, so the aggregate double-counts one latent property. The paper's own
example is exact — "conciseness" and "being to the point" are different criteria, but concise
answers also read as focused, so scoring both counts one response pattern twice.

The existing fixes all look at the rubric *text* (de-duplication, entailment matching) or
diagnose *after* scoring (factor analysis on an already-scored corpus). Neither answers the
preflight question: which criteria will behave as one dimension, and in which direction,
**before** the evaluation runs. The paper's argument for why observation cannot answer it is
the good one: correlation in observed scores cannot separate *criteria the judge treats as one
thing* from *criteria that merely co-occur in this data*. Separating them needs an intervention.

## The method, in three stages

1. **Criterion-conditioned generation.** For each criterion, ask a generator for responses that
   "strongly satisfy" (`d=+`) or "clearly fail" (`d=−`) *that criterion only*, explicitly
   instructed not to move the others. This is the intervention.
2. **Per-criterion scoring.** A verifier scores every probe on every criterion in **separate
   calls** — one call per criterion, each seeing only that criterion's definition. That removes
   within-prompt ordering and self-consistency effects, so leftover correlation is not a
   prompt artifact. Integer 0–4, normalized to [0,1].
3. **Coupling statistics.**
   - `SelfEffect(Cᵢ) = p̄ᵢ(i,+) − p̄ᵢ(i,−)` — how legible the intervention is at all.
   - `Leakage(Cᵢ→Cⱼ) = [p̄ⱼ(i,+) − p̄ⱼ(i,−)] / SelfEffect(Cᵢ)` — the effect on *another*
     criterion, normalized by intervention strength. 0 = separable, 1 = moves in lockstep;
     can be negative (a tradeoff) or above 1.
   - `Sym = ½(ℓᵢⱼ + ℓⱼᵢ)` and `Asym = |ℓᵢⱼ − ℓⱼᵢ|`.

The **asymmetry** is what a correlation cannot give you, and it is the paper's real
contribution:

| Benchmark | Pair | ℓᵢⱼ | ℓⱼᵢ | Sym | Asym | Human *r* | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| HelpSteer2 | helpfulness / correctness | +1.02 | +0.82 | +0.92 | 0.21 | +0.94 | one axis, double-counted |
| HelpSteer2 | correctness / coherence | +0.22 | +0.78 | +0.50 | 0.56 | +0.47 | coherence → correctness |
| SummEval | coherence / fluency | +0.64 | +0.51 | +0.57 | 0.16 | +0.67 | one axis |
| SummEval | coherence / relevance | +0.28 | +0.53 | +0.41 | 0.27 | +0.56 | relevance → coherence |
| SumPubMed | coherence / readability | +0.62 | +0.26 | +0.44 | 0.39 | +0.62 | coherence → readability |

Helpfulness and correctness at Sym 0.92 are not two dimensions. And the one-way cases matter
operationally: if coherence leaks into correctness at 0.78 but correctness into coherence at
0.22, the downstream criterion is inheriting the upstream signal — it is not independent
evidence, and the upstream one is what to scrutinise.

Cost is genuinely low, and the probe-budget sweep (Table 13) is honest work: subsampling the
existing N=20 probes 30 ways, agreement is **flat from N=1 upward** — HelpSteer2 0.940±0.025
at N=1 vs 0.957 at N=20; SumPubMed 0.872±0.052 vs 0.876. The 20× budget buys variance
reduction, not fidelity. Recommended operating point N=3–5.

## What the appendix does to the headline

The abstract says RADAR "recovers human inter-criterion correlation structure (Pearson
r ≥ 0.84)". That number comes from **one cell** of the generator×verifier grid
(GPT-5.5-R → Sonnet-4.6). **Table 9 reports all 36 cells, and it is a different picture:**

| Benchmark | cells ≥ 0.84 | range | median |
|---|---|---|---|
| HelpSteer2 | **12 / 12** | .87 – .97 | .92 |
| SummEval | 6 / 12 | **.62** – .96 | .87 |
| SumPubMed | **2 / 12** | **−.04** – .88 | .63 |

**20 of 36 configurations clear the advertised floor.** The worst — Sonnet-4.6 generating,
GPT-5-mini verifying, on SumPubMed — recovers **nothing**: Pearson **−0.04** against the human
structure. The appendix states the condition plainly ("agreement is high and stable *when
probes come from a capable generator such as GPT-5.5-R*"); the abstract, §5.1 and §6 do not
carry it. On SumPubMed the reported cell is the **maximum of all 12**.

That is not just presentation. It is a hole in the use case, and the paper's own design opens
it: RADAR exists precisely so that a team **without** human inter-criterion labels can audit a
rubric. But whether you are in a 0.96 cell or a −0.04 cell can only be told by comparing
against those labels. The method's fidelity varies by a full point of Pearson across
configurations, and the thing that would reveal which configuration you are in is the thing the
method is designed to do without.

The one built-in self-diagnostic does not close it either. The reliability gate (`SelfEffect ≥
τ = 0.40`) is reported for exactly one of 36 configurations — the best one — where all 13 axes
clear it comfortably (min 0.46). Appendix C says so directly: "every axis in our study clears
it comfortably … no annotated axis is dropped." **The gate never fires anywhere in the paper**,
so there is no evidence it is what separates the good cells from the bad. The most quotable
practical line — *"a criterion the generator cannot move and the verifier cannot read is too
ill-defined to score reliably"* — is a proposal, not a result.

Three smaller things, for anyone quoting this:

- **The correlations are over 6 to 10 points.** HelpSteer2 has 5 criteria → 10 off-diagonal
  pairs; SummEval and SumPubMed have 4 → 6 pairs. `r = 0.842` over six points is a wide
  interval. The bootstrap CIs in Table 12 resample *probes*, which tightens the coupling
  estimates; they do not address the number of pairs the agreement is computed over.
- **Table 13 contradicts its own setup note.** The setup says "SummEval is excluded from this
  ablation because probe-level scores were not retained" — and then Table 13 reports a SummEval
  block (marked †). It matters because §6's claim that agreement "stays ≥0.84 even at N=1"
  rests on the SummEval row (0.846) that the text says should not be there.
- **"380× savings" compares a diagnostic to an annotation.** ~0.4M tokens for a preflight
  against ~150M to annotate the full HelpSteer2 corpus. RADAR does not replace the annotation;
  it audits the rubric that the annotation would use. Appendix A addresses this; §6 reads as
  substitution. Also: the RRD baseline is a rubric *optimisation* method the authors themselves
  call "orthogonal" in aim, adapted here into a dependency estimator — which softens "the only
  method to do so consistently".

**→ hier:** this is the paper that lands most directly on machinery I already run. There are a
dozen `*-critic` agents in this setup scoring 8–10 dimensions each — `copywriting-critic`,
`webdesign-critic`, `vue-developer-critic`, `python-backend-critic`, `research-critic`,
`task-spec-critic`, `ticket-critic` — and **not one of those rubrics has ever been checked for
whether its dimensions are separate signals.** A 10-dimension score that is really three
dimensions read three times each is not a richer judgement, it is the same opinion with a
bigger number attached.

1. **Run the self-effect probe first — it is the cheap half and it answers a different
   question.** For each dimension: one deliberately good and one deliberately bad example
   targeting only that dimension, scored on the full rubric. If the critic cannot separate the
   two on the *targeted* dimension, that dimension is decoration. Per the paper's own sweep,
   **N=1 is enough** to see the structure, so a 10-dimension rubric costs 20 generations and
   200 scoring calls. That is an afternoon, not a project.
2. **Trust the coupling findings, not the clean matrices.** Given the 36-cell spread, a high
   leakage reading is strong evidence (the tension mass is ~0.00 with tight CIs everywhere) but
   a *clean* matrix is not evidence of independence — it may just be a bad cell. Asymmetric
   readings, where they appear, are the actionable ones.
3. **Score each criterion in its own call.** RADAR's Stage 2 does this deliberately, to kill
   ordering and self-consistency artifacts. My critics score all dimensions in one response,
   which is exactly the setup that manufactures the correlation being measured. Cheap fix,
   independent of whether I ever run the audit.
4. **This is the same result as Pivotal Vote from the other end.** There, nine judges carried
   the information of two, and raw accuracy tracked leniency at r = −0.99. Here, five named
   criteria collapse toward one latent axis. Both say: **the number of opinions in an
   evaluation is not the number of independent signals in it** — and neither the count of
   judges nor the count of dimensions tells you how many you actually have. [[rubric weights]]

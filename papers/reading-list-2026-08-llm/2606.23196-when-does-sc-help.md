# When self-correction helps — and the one benchmark that carries the whole result

The question is not "does self-correction work" but "on what shape of task". Five models
(Mistral Large 2512, Claude Haiku 4.5, DeepSeek V3.1, Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite, Gemini Pro) ×
seven benchmarks, **temperature 0 throughout**, no external tools, oracle labels or human
feedback. Two-step protocol (answer → revise) for SAT and the word games, three-step
(answer → critique → revise) for BBEH and HLE.

§4 opens: *"Pooled across models, SC significantly outperforms the Baseline on all
benchmarks."* That sentence is true and it is the one that will be cited. It is also produced
by pooling — and the paper's own Appendix A cites **Robinson (1950) on ecological correlation**
to explain why aggregates behave better than their parts. The caveat is in the appendix; the
pooled claim is in the results section.

## SAT is the finding. Everything else is noise around it.

SAT eliminates **65.66 % of baseline errors on average**, and Mistral gains **+30.3 points** in
one pass. It is also the only benchmark where regression is essentially absent: **C2W ≤ 2.1 %
across all five models**, against up to **13.25 %** on BBEH and HLE.

Why: SAT is the one task where the second pass is not a judgement but an **execution**. The
answer can be checked against the formula clause by clause, mechanically, with the ground
truth already in the prompt. The critic prompt says so literally — *"Check each clause one by
one … A clause (a v b v c) is satisfied if ANY literal is true."* That is a model running a
decision procedure, not a model assessing its own confidence.

Which makes this the third paper this week reaching the same place from a different direction:
**a second pass pays when it can verify, and not when it can only opine.** Pivotal Vote found
execution beat every judge panel; Calibration Floor found most measured self-correction was a
regex; here the effect concentrates on the single benchmark with a polynomial-time checker.

## What the four-way breakdown shows about the rest

Table 2 splits every result into W2C (fixed), C2W (broken), C2C, W2W. On the hard benchmarks
the net gain is a small difference between two large opposing flows:

- **HLE / Haiku:** 11.0 % fixed, 8.5 % broken → net +2.5. **78.0 % were wrong before and after.**
- **BBEH Choice / Mistral:** 8.5 % fixed, 9.75 % broken → net **−1.25**.
- **HLE / Gemini Pro:** 10.56 fixed, 9.32 broken → net +1.24, with W2W 55.28 %.

So outside SAT, self-correction churns roughly a fifth of all answers to move the score by two
or three points. If breaking a correct answer costs more than fixing a wrong one — which is the
normal case in production — that trade is not obviously worth taking, and the paper does not
frame it that way.

## The sub-task analysis, which is the paper's best work

Two LLM judges (GPT-5.5, Opus 4.6) scored each of 23 BBEH sub-tasks on *reasoning intensity*
and *verifiability*; inter-judge agreement ρ=0.97 / 0.86. Both dimensions correlate with the
average SC gain: **reasoning ρ=0.54 (p=0.007), verifiability ρ=0.65 (p<0.001)**. Per model it
holds for about half (verifiability significant for 3 of 5, reasoning for 2 of 5).

The confound check is the part worth copying. The obvious worry is that these scores are just
a proxy for baseline accuracy. Measured: **baseline accuracy vs Δ is ρ=0.05, p=0.83** — no
relationship. And reasoning and verifiability are not strongly correlated with each other
(ρ=0.19, p=0.39), so they are two dimensions, not one.

The usable artifact is the ranked table. The bottom is unambiguous — everything with a negative
Δ sits in the low-verifiability corner: **Movie Recommendation (reasoning 12 / verifiability 8)
→ −4.3 %**, Sarc Triples (22/18) → −3.4 %, SportQA (33/17) → −2.5 %. The top is messier than
the paper implies: Zebra Puzzles has verifiability **36** and the largest gain (+16.2 %), while
Dyck Languages has verifiability **83** and gains 3.1 %. **Geometric Shapes scores 90 on
reasoning and still loses 2.1 %.** The rule that survives is negative and one-sided: *low on
both → don't bother*. It does not run the other way.

## Three holes

**1. There is no matched-budget control — the exact omission that inverts the sister paper.**
Base is one call; SC is two or three. `2607.26117` (Try Again, Don't Look Back) shows that
against a no-retry baseline self-repair appears to gain 12.2 points, and against blind
resampling at equal budget it *loses*. §7 lists sampling and self-consistency as future work,
not as a threat to validity.

Here the picture is subtler than a straight refutation, and the paper misses its own best
defence: **at temperature 0 a re-ask with the identical prompt returns the identical answer**,
so the "extra lottery ticket" confound genuinely does not apply. What replaces it is procedural:
the SC prompt hands the model a verification *checklist* the base prompt lacks. **The missing
arm is one call, cheap to run, and decisive — put the checklist in the first prompt.** If
base+checklist matches SC, the second pass buys nothing and the finding is about prompt content,
not about revision.

**2. Missing responses are deleted, and never counted.** B.3: instances where the API stalled,
timed out or refused were "omitted". This is the mirror image of the Calibration Floor artifact
— that paper scored unreadable answers as *wrong*, this one *removes* them — and both leave the
same hole: no counts are reported. The exclusion is asymmetric by construction, because the SC
arm makes 2–3× as many calls and so has 2–3× as many chances to drop an instance. If stalls
correlate with difficulty, the SC arm is being graded on an easier set.

**3. The proposed diagnostic does not survive its own table.** From Haiku's collapse on
Codenames the paper suggests "unusually high revision rates may serve as a diagnostic signal".
Table 5: Haiku revises **98.7 %** and gets worse — but Flash Lite revises **85.0 %** and
improves significantly, DeepSeek **77.2 %** and improves. The threshold would have to sit
between 85 and 98.7, fitted on one point.

Also worth knowing before quoting the table: the **"Gemini Pro" column is two different
models** — Gemini 3 Pro Preview for SAT/BBEH/Codenames, Gemini 2.5 Pro for HLE/Hangman/Wordle,
after the preview endpoint was deprecated mid-experiment. Declared in B.1, invisible in Table 1.

**→ hier:** three transfers.

1. **Put the check in the first prompt before building a second pass.** This is the experiment
   the paper skipped and the cheapest one available to me: any place I run a two-stage
   draft→review, first try the single-stage version with the review criteria written into the
   instruction. Two calls have to beat one call *with the same checklist*, not one call without
   it.
2. **A revision stage needs something to check against, or it should not exist.** The usable
   rule is the negative one: **low verifiability + low reasoning → the second pass costs
   accuracy.** That covers most of what I actually ask critics to do — tone, "is this AI slop",
   "is this a good idea". Those are Movie Recommendation, not SAT. Where I *do* have a checker
   (test suite, `grep`, API response, `deadline-check.py`), the second pass earns its keep and
   barely breaks anything (C2W ≤ 2.1 %).
3. **Count what you drop.** Deleting unanswered instances and reporting the rest is a silent
   version of my own recurring failure — the measurement fails and the object gets the blame,
   or in this case the credit. [[unrunnable check]] [[false 429]] [[booking ≠ attendance]]

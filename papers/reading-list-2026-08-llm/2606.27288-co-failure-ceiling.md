# The co-failure ceiling — the number the field reports cannot see the number that binds

Everyone deciding whether to orchestrate models looks at **ρ**, the average pairwise error
correlation: low ρ is taken to mean diversity will pay. The paper's claim is that ρ is the wrong
diagnostic, and it proves it rather than arguing it.

**What actually binds is β — the rate at which *every* model is wrong on the same query.**

- **Ceiling (Prop. 1i).** Any selection policy whose output is almost surely one of the members'
  answers — router, weighted vote, cascade, debate, self-consistency — has accuracy at most
  **1 − β**. On the all-wrong event every member is wrong, so any selector is wrong. One line.
- **Gain localization (Prop. 1ii).** `G = Pr[single-best wrong] − β`. The co-failure tail
  contributes **exactly nothing** to the achievable gain.
- **Non-identification (Prop. 3).** For m ≥ 3, β is **not a function of the pairwise error law**.
  There exist joint distributions over {0,1}^m with identical marginals *and* identical pairwise
  correlations — Pearson and tetrachoric — yet different β. So no statistic built from pairwise
  correlations, single-factor copula included, can identify β even in principle. (Classical
  Fréchet-class fact, instantiated for the co-failure cell; they claim no novelty for the maths.)
- **Certificate (Prop. 1iii).** From *n* graded queries with *K* all-wrong, the Clopper–Pearson
  lower limit β_lo gives, with probability ≥ 1−δ, a bound on what **any** such policy can gain:
  `Acc − a_single-best ≤ (1 − β_lo) − a_single-best`. **If that bound falls below the
  orchestration overhead, nothing in the class can pay for itself — measured from one held-out
  set, before a router is trained, at $0.**

## The measurement: 67 models, 21 provider families

| | MATH-500 | code_contests | GPQA-Diamond (MC) |
|---|---|---|---|
| models / queries | 67 / 330 | 18 / 63 | 52 / 130 |
| single-best | 0.836 | 0.825 | 0.846 |
| per-query oracle | 0.948 | 0.921 | 1.000 |
| oracle gain *G* | 0.112 | 0.096 | 0.154 |
| **all-wrong β** | **0.052** (k=17) | **0.079** (k=5) | **<0.03** (0/130) |
| mean pairwise ρ | 0.53 | 0.27 | 0.25 |
| ρ underprices β by | **2.5×** | **3.1×** [1.5, 6.2] | — |
| regime | ceiling-bound | ceiling-bound | realizability-bound |

The underpricing is not copula misspecification, and they close that door properly. The
correctly **tetrachoric**-calibrated single-factor model predicts β = 0.021 against 0.052. The
**full 67×67 pairwise-tetrachoric Gaussian copula**, Monte-Carlo'd over all pairs, still predicts
only 0.023 — a 2.25× residual. An exchangeable **Clayton** copula with genuine lower-tail
dependence (λ_L = 0.69), calibrated to the same mean pairwise co-failure, predicts 0.026 — still
1.96× short. **The residual survives every exchangeable pairwise-calibrated copula they can fit.**
What is left is a *common-mode atom*: a positive-probability event of joint failure that no
pairwise statistic represents.

And it grows with the pool. Resampling random k-model subsets (60 per k), the underpricing ratio
rises **monotonically from 1.0 at k=2 to a median 2.5 at k=67** — isolating *size*, not *which
models*, as the driver, exactly as Prop. 2 predicts (a Gaussian copula with ρ̄ < 1 has zero lower
tail dependence, so β_sf(m) → 0 while β(m) → π > 0). Mean pairwise ρ is exact for pairs and
increasingly inadequate as the pool grows. **The newest frontier still co-fails.**

## The result that reframes everything: format, not content

Take the same 79 GPQA-Diamond questions. Ask them multiple-choice: β ≈ 0, the ceiling is wide
open. Strip the options and ask them free-response, graded by a five-judge panel (pairwise
κ = 0.73–0.92):

- mean accuracy **0.66 → 0.51**, best model **0.91 → 0.77**
- **β = 0 → 0.127** (k=10/79, CP [0.062, 0.220]) — comparable to math and code

Content held fixed, format changed, regime flipped. **Co-failure tracks open-endedness itself,
not subject matter** — and it appears on an LLM-judged generative task, not only on
programmatically verifiable ones. The multiple-choice guess floor over a broad pool is what was
making joint failure look rare.

## Routers capture almost none of the gain, and it isn't the router's fault

Four routers on the 15-model saturated mix:

| Router | share of oracle gain *G* captured |
|---|---|
| TF-IDF + domain logistic (held out) | **9 %**, 95 % CI [−0.67, +0.50] |
| Gradient-boosted per-model correctness predictor | **−0.09** |
| Direct multiclass best-model predictor | **−1.27** (actively harmful) |
| **LLM-as-router** (GPT-5-mini, shown each query + a capsule of every model's strengths) | **exactly 0** — it routes to single-best on **100 %** of queries |

Their diagnosis is the honest one: not a weak router, but that **the prompt carries little signal
about which model will be right when the frontier disagrees**. The small oracle bound is itself
largely unreachable.

## Two findings on fusion that cut in opposite directions

**Naive diversity is a liability.** Over all 455 three-model triplets, the mean unweighted
majority-vote gain over the best member is **negative** (−0.10 on the hard regime, −0.02
saturated, robust to a leave-one-model-out jackknife). Mixing unequal-quality models lets
diverse-but-weaker members outvote a strong one. The finer "gain rises with 1−ρ" prediction has
a positive point estimate (+0.13) but is **not significant** under model-clustering
(jackknife CI [−0.07, +0.34]) — and they note the naive i.i.d. bootstrap CI is **~3× too narrow**.

**At matched quality, diversity does pay — by +0.027.** Self-MoA (repeated draws from the single
best model, ρ_intra = 0.80) against an accuracy-matched 6-model band (ρ_inter = 0.42), with
distinct draws on both sides so the comparison is information-fair. The heterogeneous ensemble
wins, averaged over 60 resamplings of the split: **+0.027, range +0.010 to +0.050, positive in
all 60**. A single favourable partition gives +0.055 — and they report the partition-averaged
number instead, calling the mechanism "supported in one regime, not established". That is the
discipline the rest of this reading list has mostly lacked.

**Cascades collapse with their verifier.** As the verifier's AUC is degraded 0.899 → 0.510, the
cascade's advantage over random-mixing-at-matched-budget falls **0.121 → 0.012**. With a good
verifier and 5-fold held-out threshold selection it holds at **+0.114**. The cascade is worth
exactly what its confidence signal is worth.

## Two instrument bugs they caught in themselves — both mine too

1. **An answer extractor that mis-scored verbose models.** A first-letter extractor inflated
   Llama-4-Maverick by **+0.26 accuracy** (mean |Δ| = 0.05 across the pool). They re-graded every
   cached output. This is the **Calibration Floor mechanism found independently in the wild**:
   the parser's failure charged to the model.
2. **Their own order-of-magnitude artifact, published.** An earlier version of this analysis used
   the naive Pearson-of-indicators calibration (ρ̄ = 0.53), predicting β_sf = 0.0016 and a
   spurious **32×** underpricing. The correct tetrachoric transform gives 2.5×. They print the
   wrong number, label it "the calibration artifact we diagnose", and keep the modest one.

They also separate **$270 of itemized reported-experiment cost from $560 of total account usage
including exploratory runs**, and report the former rather than presenting the latter as
experiment cost.

**→ hier:** the most directly load-bearing paper in this list for the multi-agent work.

1. **Run the certificate before building any panel, and it costs nothing.** Grade one held-out
   set with every candidate, count the queries where *all* were wrong, take the Clopper–Pearson
   lower bound. If `(1 − β_lo) − best_single` is smaller than the overhead, the machinery cannot
   pay for itself — proved, not estimated. This is the same instrument as Pivotal Vote's
   "run the deterministic check alone first", one level more general: it bounds the *entire class*
   of policies at once.
2. **My work is the ceiling-bound regime, not the open one.** Everything here is free-response —
   drafts, analyses, code, decisions. GPQA's β went from ≈0 to 0.127 purely by removing the answer
   options. So the encouraging orchestration numbers from multiple-choice benchmarks do not
   transfer to what I actually do; the models fail together more often than the literature suggests.
3. **"Ask three models once rather than one model three times" survives — but only at matched
   quality, and it is worth +0.027.** With *unmatched* quality it inverts: unweighted majority
   vote over unequal models loses 0.10. That is exactly the shape of the LiteLLM alias pool
   (`smart` = Opus, `simple` = qwen3:8b) — **voting across those would actively hurt**, because
   the weak members outvote the strong one. Correcting the note I wrote from the swarm paper on
   07-30: the rule holds, its precondition does not hold here.
4. **The fallback chain is a cascade with no verifier, so it buys availability and not accuracy.**
   `smart → cloud-oss → simple` escalates on *API failure*, not on answer quality; the paper's
   cascade result says the advantage over random mixing is proportional to verifier AUC and goes
   to zero at 0.5. The README already claims availability ("an answer always returns"), and that
   claim is fine — this is a note not to let it drift into a quality claim.
5. **An LLM shown every model's strengths and asked to pick still routed to the best model 100 %
   of the time.** Before assuming a dispatcher agent can allocate work across models by reading
   the task, note that the strongest version of that idea captured *exactly zero* of the available
   gain. [[rubric weights]] [[one-answer criterion]]

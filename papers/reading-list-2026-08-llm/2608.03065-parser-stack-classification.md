# The schema is the cheapest quality gain a weak model can get — and the same mechanism PhantomFill measured as a lie machine

A compilers paper, not an LLM paper. Grammar-constrained decoding masks the logits so the sampler
can only emit tokens that keep the output parseable. The classical implementation asks the parser
about **every token in the vocabulary, every step** — `O(|V|)`, and modern vocabularies are 128k
(Llama 3), 151k (Qwen), 262k (Gemma 3).

PSC inverts it. For each token, build a finite-state automaton that accepts exactly those **parser
stacks** under which that token is legal; union all of them into one classifier; then at decode
time **check the stack once** and read off a precomputed mask. Online cost becomes independent of
vocabulary size. The theoretical contribution is the proof that "the set of parser stacks
accepting a given token" is a regular language.

Result: **up to 700× faster mask computation on programming-language grammars, up to 30× on
schema-conformant JSON**, and end-to-end throughput that approaches unconstrained decoding.

## What it costs, which is the part worth writing down

The speed is bought with an offline preprocessing pass per (grammar, vocabulary) pair, and the
price varies by four orders of magnitude:

| grammar | preprocessing time | preprocessing RAM | break-even vs LLGuidance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **JSON schema** | 28–53 s | **3–6 GiB** | **20–40 s of decoding** |
| Java | ~8 min | ~40 GiB | 67–147 s |
| Go | ~20 min | ~88 GiB | *(not printed)* |
| SQL | ~1.3 h | **~250 GiB** | 509–2826 s |

Resident cost at decode time follows the same split: SQL needs ~3.4 GiB for the DFA plus ~2.8 GiB
of precomputed masks — about 6 GiB of pure constraint machinery — while a JSON schema needs
**~300 KiB + ~2 MiB**.

So the honest deployment reading: **for JSON schemas this is free and you should just do it**
(preprocess in half a minute, amortised after half a minute of decoding). For programming-language
grammars you rent a 500 GiB cloud instance or you take someone else's preprocessing artifact. The
paper says exactly this and even prices the instance (~2.5 USD/h), which is the right way to
report a cost.

## The downstream numbers say something the paper does not

§4.5 is the quality section, and read column by column it splits cleanly.

**Under greedy decoding, grammar constraints do essentially nothing.** Nine pass@1 cells across
three Go datasets and three models: **six are identical to unconstrained** (5.6/5.6, 8.8/8.8,
0.6/0.6, 6.0/6.0, 12.0/12.0, 4.0/4.0) and three move by 0.1–0.3 points. The paper's "outperforms
unconstrained decoding on all three datasets" is true only once you go up the k axis, where
sampling runs at **temperature 1.0** — which is the mechanism: the constraint prunes syntactically
dead samples out of the pool. Its value is a function of sampling entropy, not of the model.
McEval Go pass@50 is the honest showcase: 22.0 → 26.0.

**The generic grammar buys nothing; the schema buys everything.** JSON-Mode-Eval pass@1:

| model | Standard | GCD (JSON grammar) | **GCD + Schema** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Llama 3.2 1B | 55.6 | 56.6 | **68.7** |
| Qwen2.5 0.5B | 64.7 | 64.7 | **67.7** |
| Gemma 3 270M | 28.3 | 29.3 | **56.6** |

Gemma 3 270M **doubles**. That is the single most useful number in the paper: the weaker the
model, the more a *specific* schema is worth, and "valid JSON" as a constraint is worth almost
nothing. Compare the text-to-SQL column, where Gemma sits at 0.4 % → 1.4 % execution accuracy —
a model that cannot do the task at all, and constraining it changes noise into noise.

(A small internal slip, in the unusual direction of *understating*: the prose reports "up to 10
points" of pass@1 improvement for Llama 3.2 1B, where Table 5 shows +12.1 against GCD and +13.1
against Standard. The Gemma figure — "27 points" — matches the against-GCD reading.)

**The benchmark was adjusted to fit the tool, and only a footnote says so.** Of 100
JSON-Mode-Eval samples: one excluded (oracle answer copies the schema), **six had their schemas
replaced** with "equivalent schemas supported by our grammar generation script", and **five could
not be converted at all**, so on those the headline `GCD + Schema` condition silently falls back to
the generic JSON grammar. That is 12 % of the set touched, 5 % where the advertised method is not
actually running, and every adjustment points the same way. Disclosed in footnote 7; not carried
into Table 5.

## The finding that closes section 4

**PSC and [`2607.20492`](2607.20492-phantomfill.md) run the same mechanism eight days apart and
report opposite signs, and neither benchmark can see the other's result.**

- PSC measures grammar-constrained decoding on JSON-Mode-Eval, Spider, HumanEval Go. **Every
  single item has a correct oracle answer.** There is no unanswerable cell anywhere in the paper.
- PhantomFill measures the same enforced decoding on items that are *unanswerable by
  construction*, and finds the escape token spent **0 times out of 203** on the fields that carry
  the fabrication.

So they are not in conflict — they measure disjoint halves of the same tool, and my setup contains
both halves. The synthesis for section 4, and the rule I take out of the whole "dumb tools"
section:

> **A schema is the cheapest quality gain available to a weak model, and the mechanism that forces
> a lie when the evidence is absent. The discriminator is whether the field has an honest value
> available. Constrain the form; never constrain away "no".**

Both halves have a price tag now: the constraint costs ~2 MiB and 30 seconds for a JSON schema
(PSC), and the escape value costs one line of schema (PhantomFill). There is no budget argument
against doing both.

## What transfers

1. **Force the format of subagent output — the cost is negligible and the weak-model gain is
   large.** Gemma 3 270M 28.3 → 56.6 is the case for it. This is the `simple` alias on the LiteLLM
   proxy: exactly the class of model where a schema is worth doubling, and exactly the class that
   [PhantomFill](2607.20492-phantomfill.md) shows will never take an escape it is offered. Both
   apply at once.
2. **Constrain the *specific* schema, not the generic format.** "Valid JSON" bought 0–1.0 points.
   The schema bought 3.0–28.3. A `ReportFindings`-shaped contract is the useful kind; `response_format:
   json` is not.
3. **Greedy decoding gets nothing from a grammar.** If a step runs at temperature 0 and the model
   already emits well-formed output, adding a constraint is cost without gain. The constraint pays
   where sampling is wide — which is also where [Biased Consensus](2608.02827-biased-consensus.md)
   says the debate stops collapsing. Same knob, two papers, opposite direction: **temperature is
   the parameter that decides whether either machinery does anything at all.**
4. **A method that cannot handle 5 % of its own inputs must fail visibly.** The schema→grammar
   converter silently degraded to the weaker condition on five samples. My equivalent is
   [[unrunnable check]] once more: the cases the tool cannot process were scored as if it had.

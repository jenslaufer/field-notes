# A Data-Driven Reality Check on AI in Business

> **The value of generative AI in companies is mostly unmeasured, and the measurable part sits
> in a few redesigned processes, not in a copilot for everyone.** Davenport calls the broad
> rollout *"spray and pray"*; to get measurable value *"you need to have enterprise level use
> cases"*. Where companies do get value (software development, customer service), review,
> requirements work and change management eat much of the gain — in his estimate from 50
> percent to 20 percent. The bottleneck for agentic AI is not the model: *"We don't even know
> what our workflows are in most organizations and nobody owns them"*. His forecast is slow:
> redesigning a business process end-to-end will take years, and he doubts many companies
> have the patience.

## Old methods, new interface

Davenport's framing to his students: even the largest generative model still draws curves that
minimise error. What changed is that the predictions are *"Linguistic"*, so people without
statistics can work with them, and *"people are dazzled by it"*. He pushes against the idea
that AI began with ChatGPT — *"AI was invented on November 30th 2022"* is the belief he
fights. For executives, *"traditional machine learning or analytical AI"* is still the better
tool for decisions, because it predicts from structured past data. Executives instead ask
which chat vendor to pick, which he does not think is the problem they should worry about.

## Data science after the bar dropped

AutoML has made modelling easier for roughly a decade; now a two- or three-line prompt
returns pages of analysis. The routine part of the job — giving people access to algorithms —
loses value. What remains: deployment (peer reviewers once rejected it as a data-science
capability), stakeholders and change management. His favourite phrase runs across two transcript lines; the second reads *"No employment for data
scientists"*.

## Work slop and process slop

Work slop is a colleague's low-quality AI output that you must review. Process slop is the
same across a whole, often inter-organisational process. His example is recruiting: AI
writes the job definition, applicants answer with AI-written CVs and cover letters, AI screens
them and writes the rejection — and *"the net result is people have lost faith in that
process"*. He sees the same in college admissions and peer review. Expected outcome: *"this
wasn't quite the productivity boom that we thought it was"*, because someone has to clean it
up. No organisation he names has pulled out of that spiral; he notes a backlash starting among
young people.

## Measurement: almost nobody does it

Katie cites numbers she has seen: *"90% of them say that I think this is gonna bring a lot of
value to my company"* (company leaders). Davenport says surveys show executives rate the
potential gains higher than people who use the tools daily, and that executives now report
trouble measuring them. On the MIT study (*"95 percent of generative AI use cases have zero
value"*) he calls it *"really not a very good study"*: the zero means zero *measurable* value,
because most use cases are individual productivity and nobody measures individual
productivity across a large organisation.

What he says would work: *"Semi controlled experiments where this group uses it"* and another
does not, measuring speed and quality of output. That needs a culture of disciplined
experimentation. When he asks large audiences who has one, *"not a single person in the room
in the US anyway"*; in Europe some hands go up. DBS Bank treats individual productivity tools
as *"an employee satisfaction and retention tool, but that's about it"*.

## Where the gains shrink

- **Software development.** Companies generate *"more code than they have humans to review
  it"*. Developers must shift toward requirements and stakeholder work, plus a product mindset:
  *"We're successful when people are using it"*. Net effect in his estimate: from 50 percent
  to 20 percent productivity gain — *"That's still pretty good"*.
- **Customer service, FDA submissions, legal briefs.** Mission-critical output needs careful
  review; law firms and consulting firms *"are getting into big trouble"* by skipping it.
- **Human in the loop.** Katie's summary of his argument: efficiency gain and high-quality
  output *"don't go well together"* as long as a human must watch the AI all day. He sees
  this *"Starting to dawn on"* people, along with more attention to cost.

## Processes before agents

Companies have owners of functions, *"but we don't have owners of processes"*; even the word
process sounds bureaucratic, "workflows" is acceptable. To let agentic AI take over a
workflow, *"We need to understand how much better we need to have owners of them"* and
measure it. His current research: mostly European companies, which are more
process-oriented, preparing for agentic workflows with context knowledge about how to run the
process well. *"I think it's going to take"* years to redesign a process end-to-end, and the
agent technology will probably be ready by the time they finish. Same lesson from knowledge
management: AI revives it, but companies that never curated knowledge still do not.

## His own use

- A robot-football novel: Claude and ChatGPT agreed it needed work and offered to rewrite it;
  he declined.
- A survey of about *"a hundred people"*: Claude analysed the SPSS file and surfaced findings
  he would not have found, but stuck to its own ideas when given the report.
- A family tree from a GEDCOM file with historical context — good, still edited.
- Weak spot: word limits. Asked to cut 2000 words to 1500, *"it'll come back and say okay, I
  did it and it's like 900 words"*.
- Grading: good feedback, but the grade *"Basically gives everybody a B plus"*.
- Teaching: students may use AI for an essay but must vary prompts, verify facts and
  citations; on obscure topics the best-looking citation is *"almost certainly"* invented.

## Insights for me (Jens) — my connections, flagged as mine

- **His diagnosis is my offer.** Companies lack process owners and do not measure. An AI
  agent project that starts with mapping one workflow, naming its owner and setting a
  before/after metric sells the part he says is missing. My freelancermap profile and pitch
  could say that in one line: one process, measured, instead of "AI for everyone".
- **Bring the measurement design along.** His "semi controlled experiments" fit a small
  project: one team or case batch with the agent, one without, same metric (throughput, error
  rate, review time). That gives the client a number his surveys say executives cannot get.
- **Price the review cost in.** His 50-to-20 percent example is the honest framing for an
  estimate: the agent's raw speed-up minus review and change work. Quoting the net figure
  protects me from over-promising.
- **Analytical ML is still a selling point.** For decision problems (fraud, churn,
  forecasting) he says classical ML is the better tool. I can offer both and say which fits,
  instead of defaulting to an LLM.

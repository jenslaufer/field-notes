# Rory Sutherland — trivial improvements vs real strategy

> "if we only use short term measures of return, we're going to over invest in what you
> might call fairly trivial improvements, which don't significantly drive new behaviour.
> And we're going to under invest in the stuff that really matters." [45:31]

Companion note: [`../econtalk/2019-11-11-rory-sutherland-on-alchemy.md`](../econtalk/2019-11-11-rory-sutherland-on-alchemy.md).
That one is the theory (variance, ergodicity, minority rule). This one is the operator's
version: what to do when everyone benchmarks the same numbers, and why the important bet
looks like a failure for its first years.

## 1. Sell how marketers think, not what they do

- Marketers fell into "this whole trap of accountability, which is a kind of financial
  fantasy" [01:30]. Finance will "view marketing as a cost no matter how efficiently you
  do it" [02:00].
- "The real value of marketing and marketers is not what they do. It's how they think." [02:13]
- Without that mindset in the room, a board makes "decisions which seem perfectly rational
  to the people present but are actually extremely dumb" [02:53].
- Closing line of the same thought: marketing "is not a department ... it's a way of
  approaching a problem in business" [86:13].

## 2. Efficiency the customer pays for is not efficiency

- The self-checkout fallacy: a firm tells the City a story of efficiency, "the person
  paying the price for this added efficiency is your customer" [03:27].
- Outsourcing admin to the customer "was kind of cute when two or three people did it" —
  now it "looks like a cost saving. It's actually a cost." [04:21–04:42]
- Counter-move for the AI age: "one or two players in every category saying, we're going
  to go high human high touch" [04:47], because everybody else automates.
- Farmers market as proof: "five transactions in order to buy eight things", a premium
  for buying direct — it satisfies a need "to interact with an organization which isn't
  optimized for efficiency or low cost" [05:57–06:22].

## 3. Benchmarking makes everyone the same — which makes difference cheap

- "when everybody optimizes or benchmarks around the same thing, you automatically create
  an opportunity to be different" [07:01]. The price of distinction "becomes weirdly low" [07:17].
- DoubleTree cookie: a warm cookie at check-in, "memorable precisely because they're
  discretionary". He stayed once, over a decade ago: "All I can remember about it was it
  was in Chicago and I got some cookies." [07:58–08:17]
- Roger L. Martin's chain: metrics become targets; same metrics, same targets; same
  targets, firms converge; hyper-competition. Customers lose choice, investors a smaller
  market, the firm is "forced to compete on price for lack of differentiation" [08:42–09:15].

## 4. Add an axis; rewrite the question

- Brian Eno's idea (he calls it "axis theory"): activity collapses onto one axis; "if you
  add an orthogonal or right angle axis to that, you gain a whole degree of potential to
  innovate" [10:52–11:13]. Steve Jobs added "how does it feel while you're doing it?" to
  "what it does" [10:01].
- School teaches "it's not legitimate to change the question" [12:45]. In business the
  unsolved problem usually exists because "you've been asking the wrong question all
  along" [13:31].
- HS2 brief given to Disney instead of engineers: "how do you make the journey between
  London and Manchester so enjoyable people feel stupid going by car?" [14:03]
- Why bureaucrats hate this: the better question "creates lots of possible solutions",
  and choosing among them needs subjective judgment — "bureaucratic kryptonite". The
  name: "defensive decision making. You optimize your decision not for the quality of the
  outcome, but for the degree of defensibility of the decision." [18:22–19:10]
- Disney's half-joke: go through the Yellow Pages and do each business better — "we only
  got to A for amusement parks" [24:49]. His D for dry cleaning: a carry bag (40p) instead
  of hangers cutting your hand. The bag lets him go to a café afterwards; without it he
  drives straight home — the same trap as frozen food in a mall [25:16–26:56].

## 5. Creativity is detective work

- The creative process "is probably most closely analogous to detective work" [28:04]:
  incomplete information, vague open questions, red-team your own hypothesis.
- Evidence matters for the verdict, but "it's completely wrong to say the only
  information of any value is evidential value" [30:31]. Sometimes you "act in order to
  know rather than acting on what you know" [31:08].
- Yorkshire Ripper arrest: a beat cop noticed the car was parked the wrong way round —
  "no evidential value, it was simply a weird anomaly" — and found the hammer and
  screwdriver where Sutcliffe had relieved himself [33:27–33:43].
- Build in "the part of the process which isn't a process at all" — 48 hours of
  "contented not knowing" [34:35–35:16].

## 6. The S-curve: big ideas look like failures first

- Only "about six or seven percent of people in any field who'll just take a punt on
  stuff" [38:07]. The rest run on social copying and habit.
- Why he bought an electric car — the true answer: "because my brother bought one first" [39:10].
- Measure retention, not sales: for Meta glasses ask "do they go on to keep using them?
  And will they buy the new ones" [41:29]. Same test as automatic gearboxes: "once you
  drive an automatic, you never really go back" [42:07].
- Market research misleads: people "confect reasons as to why they don't want to change
  their behavior" [42:34].
- Road vs rail: a dualled A40 changed behaviour overnight (same trip, faster). High Speed 1
  took "seven or eight years" to fill, because it created trips nobody made before [43:28–45:26].
- Herd effects scale with visibility: weak for haemorrhoid cream, strong for drinks in a
  pub and for Google Glass. "Google Glass gave up too soon." [45:57–48:12]
- Launch geography by geography or in dense networks: New Coke, Warburtons "one city at a
  time", Facebook at Harvard/Cambridge, Bumble on campuses, an SNCF van at a village
  market [54:19–56:02].
- Accounting asymmetry: "we are forced to account for our costs immediately, but we can
  only lay claim to the value in the very short term" [57:12]. Marketing on an S-curve
  "is actually very effective when it looks ineffective in the early stages" [57:46], and
  at the inflection "it's the herd effects" you see, not the ads [58:03].
- IBM: budgets as a share of existing revenue starve the new thing — "do a Wayne Gretzky"
  and weight spend to where revenue will be [58:38–59:14].
- Strategy can be "we're going to make sure we stay alive long enough to have a chance to
  get lucky" [59:54].
- Car salesman: "No selling them their next car" [61:29]. He sold less for three years,
  then everyone came back.

## 7. Downsell, free samples, brand partnerships

- Downselling (credited to Cialdini): tell the customer which option not to buy; then "it
  is automatically taken to be signed that you are now in a relationship" [63:22].
- Behavioural science has what advertising lacks: "there is a free sample in behavioral
  science" [66:39]. Try two small things before the £25,000 project.
- Secret weapons: physical direct mail and brand partnerships; Mark Ritson calls auditory
  branding and brand partnerships the two most underrated forms of marketing [64:40–65:15].

## 8. Winner takes all — be ten times better or start a category

- Two default modes of the brain (do what you did before, do what others do) create a
  Matthew effect. Caruso and the gramophone; the average author earns about the same as
  50 years ago "until you strip out jk rallying [J.K. Rowling] and Dan Brown" [69:35].
- Ehrenberg's double jeopardy: a weaker brand is in fewer repertoires and bought less
  often by those who have it [70:05].
- Ries & Trout ("recent trout" in the ASR): don't be number seven. Either be ten times
  better or build a category you can lead. CitizenM vs the Connaught [70:26–71:36].
- The cheap route to "ten times": "a couple of freaky delight factors" rather than the
  performance axis [72:23].

## 9. Critical non-essentials

- Paddy Lund's dentist practice: a private waiting room, fresh cookies, your favourite
  magazine and music [72:48–73:09]. "critical non-essentials" — the episode's best phrase.
- Jon Steel on whether a gesture is too cheesy: "there's no such thing as too cheesy" [73:36].
- Marketing is itself a critical non-essential. A dirty used car priced slightly low still
  sells worse or not at all; buyers need "a degree of performative marketing bullshit
  before we're happy to buy" [76:47–77:33].
- Framing a discount: "33% off is a lot less exciting than 50% extra free" [79:52]. Nectar
  prices (suggested to Sainsbury's in 1997): a member price feels earned, not given [80:13–80:54].
- His proposed award for critical non-essentials: pianos in stations and the glass roof of
  the new London electric taxi [90:24–91:07].

## 10. Two hurdles and where to start a career

- Hardest lesson: "there's the idea and there's the selling in of the idea" [74:27]. Half
  of the client's brain asks "how the hell do i present this to the board" [75:00].
- Start in direct marketing, a shop, a café or a restaurant — "it's a free mba" [83:31–83:38].
- Four of five IPA Effectiveness gold winners in 2024 were family-owned (McCain,
  Specsavers, a third the ASR renders as "latheways", Yorkshire Tea), the fifth Guinness
  [87:54–88:19]. Unverified.

## For me

- **Distribution without an audience:** the S-curve argument is the answer to "nothing is
  moving after four weeks". Measure retention and repeat use, not adoption speed.
- **Launch in a dense network:** one city, one campus, one niche at a time.
- **Critical non-essentials** cost little and are remembered for ten years. The question
  for every product: what is the cookie?
- **Downsell and free sample** fit selling consulting with no network: offer the small,
  honest first step, not the full project.

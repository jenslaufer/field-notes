# The Right Way To Write With AI

> Nicolas Cole, a ghostwriter turned SaaS founder, gives one model for judging a piece of
> writing before you spend time on it. Content comes in three tiers — **commodity,
> personality, original** — and a second axis, **timely vs. timeless**. His division of
> labour with AI: the human writes and chooses; the machine *repeats* what the human has
> already said ("approved language") and remixes it. The library of what you have written is
> your "personal language model", and the part no model can produce is the **unmade data
> set**: your own stories and your stance on each commodity idea. Greg closes by naming a
> startup idea he will not build himself: **company brains as a service**.

## 1. SaaS has a moat when it has a point of view

Cole's opener answers the claim that *"SaaS no longer has a moat"* with a product claim, not a writing claim:

> *"I think that SaaS has a moat when you have a point of view."*

> *"if you're just building commodity SaaS, meaning like you're just providing features that accomplish tasks to people, that's fundamentally a commodity."*

A method-driven product forces a copier to keep looking back at the person who owns the method:

> *"They're the one doing the thinking. I'm just going to copy them, which means you're always behind."*

> *"there's no moat in anything if you don't have a point of view."*

His own product is the example: Typeshare's writing and scheduling features are, in his words,
a commodity; the method (three content tiers, personal language model) is what the features
serve.

## 2. Three tiers: commodity, personality, original

**Commodity.** Language is open source; most sentences belong to no one. What people call AI
slop is this tier:

> *"what you're really identifying is commodity content."*

The fastest tell is grammatical person — *"you should"* leans commodity, *"I did this"* leans
personality. Commodity still has value, mainly for beginners, and for him the value is in the
**choice**, not the idea:

> *"The question is which types of commodity content do you agree with and which types do you disagree with?"*

> *"It's not the ideas themselves that are unique. It's which ones are tied to your identity."*

His explanation of slop follows from that: the model picks a side you never told it.

> *"AI doesn't know which perspective you agree with."*

**Personality.** Ownership is association, reached first through volume, then through personal
detail:

> *"ownership is really just a different word for association."*

> *"the easiest way to establish association is through volume."*

> *"quote-unquote less credible, more personality content. typically outperforms more credible commodity content."*

His own proof is one sentence reused for years — *"When I was 17 years old, I was one of the
highest ranked World of Warcraft players in North America."* A Quora answer that opened with it
went viral, and he repeated it *"hundreds of times"* afterwards.

**Original.** Earned only by depth on few topics over years:

> *"Original thinking comes as the result of crystallizing the way that you see the world in a specific topic or a small number of topics for a prolonged period of time."*

> *"just because you wrote it doesn't mean you own it."*

## 3. Robots repeat, humans write

The ghostwriter mechanism is the core of the episode. He stitched clients' existing sentences
into new pieces — *"approved language"* — and calls that the manual version of what AI should do:

> *"the larger your library grows, the more ideas and approved language you have, the more net new combinations you can create."*

> *"I don't want the robots writing for me. What I want is the robots repeating me."*

> *"You want AI doing repeating. You don't want AI doing net new writing thinking."*

The shortcut warning, the line most worth keeping:

> *"the shortcut is the longer road in disguise."*

Where the library lives does not matter to him — *"What matters is that AI has access to it."*
The piece nobody else has is the **unmade data set**:

> *"everyone is using the same LLMs, meaning they are all creating from the same data set. Your personality content, your library of content is the unmade data set."*

## 4. Timely vs. timeless, and voice as settings

Every piece sits on a spectrum, and the error is mismatched expectations:

> *"Timely content has very high short-term value and very low long-term value. Timeless content has very low short-term value and very high long-term value."*

His example: an essay that *"Took me two weeks to write"*, which he expects to *"reference and
use that essay for like the next 10 years"*. A date in the first sentence (*"in 2026"*) moves a
piece toward timely.

Voice, in his account, is a bundle of adjustable variables — word choice, sentence length,
sentence structure — not a mystery. The ghostwriting anecdote: a furious client, a piece that
leaned on opinion where he spoke in facts; Cole added two or three *"according to"* citations:

> *"I changed 1% of it."*

And the deflating number: *"I have ghostwritten for north of 300 different founders, executives, investors, the vast majority of the time I used the exact same voice in the writing."*

On format he is flat: his business with Dickie makes *"essentially zero short-form video"* and
*"We run a multi-seven-figure business. It's all driven through writing."* Scripted short video
is writing too.

## 5. Company brain, and the idea Greg hands away

Greg's volume target for his agency: *"how do we ship a minimum of five pieces of content a day?"*
Cole agrees and moves the question upstream — *"The question on top of that is what is the input?"*
His proposal: a company pays one deep thinker only to write long-form on its industry, and the
machinery cuts it up.

> *"I just want you to create the highest quality input."*

> *"None of it matters. What matters is you have the infrastructure, and you have a very valuable content library that you pull from and leverage for a long period of time."*

Greg frames it as the moat and ends on the opportunity:

> *"that's why people are starting to say the company brain is the moat."*

> *"that's a whole other startup idea that someone needs to do, which is just do company brains as a service."*

> *"If I was excited at all about B2B, I would go do it, but I'm not."*

My judgement, not the episode's: neither speaker gives a price, a customer, or a delivery shape
for "company brains as a service". It is named, not specified.

## Insights for me

- **The unmade data set already exists here — it is just not published.** the assistant memory
  (`~/.claude/projects/-home-jens-repos-assistant/memory/`) holds 89 `learning_*` files, each a dated, measured failure with a number attached ("22 of 35
  GitHub links dead", "0 of 60 PRs with proof"). By Cole's tiers that is personality-and-original
  material, not commodity: nobody else has these incidents. The field notes themselves are
  commodity-plus-stance. The cheapest test of his model is to turn existing learnings into
  timeless essays on the jenslaufer sites, not to write new material.
- **But his model assumes the thing I lack: years of volume.** Association "through volume",
  five pieces a day, a Quora answer repeated for years — all of it compounds only where an
  audience accumulates. With no network, the channel that fits is **timeless long-form in
  search**, and the SEO finding here is that authority, not content, is the bottleneck. So:
  write for search on narrow, owned topics (XRechnung mandate 2027 for small firms; data
  extraction from finance portals for FinGrab), expect near-zero short-term value, and set the
  kill number in advance — e.g. no page with measurable impressions in GSC after 90 days →
  stop. Do not copy the five-a-day target.
- **"Point of view" is a product question for my portfolio.** FinGrab, CommentGrab, HostGrab,
  PlaylistGrab are feature tools — commodity SaaS in his sense — and the listing copy is
  commodity too. Fabrik is the one product that could carry a stance ("agents cannot promise;
  every PR carries proof", from the 14.09. note). A method-first landing page is cheaper to test
  than another feature.
- **For the assistant daemon: "robots repeat me" is the right rule for Telegram drafts and
  outbound mail.** The existing tone rules (plain language, no cadence, empathetic mail) are
  approved language already; a stitched library of Jens-approved sentences would be the concrete
  form of his idea. Low cost, but only after the distribution question above has an answer.
- **Numbers to discount.** Greg's intro figure for Cole's writing income is host framing, not
  evidence; "multi-seven-figure" is self-reported. Neither is a benchmark for a solo founder
  without an audience.

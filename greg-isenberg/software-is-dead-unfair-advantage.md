# Software is dead — the honest version

> **The claim being rebutted:** indie hackers are watching Google referrals halve and
> revenue slide, and the loudest reading is that the large labs are eating all
> software. **Greg's counter is not "no" — it's a relocation.** The unfair advantage
> used to be **code**: technical people could build a tool *and* do SEO (SEO is
> technical), so they got traffic no one else could get. **That arbitrage is gone.**
> The new unfair advantages are **distribution, niche depth, proprietary data, network
> effects, and willingness to maintain what others won't.** Who actually loses is
> narrow and specific: *the one-person software engineer selling a $20/month tool to
> other indie hackers.* Everyone else is looking at a bigger addressable market,
> because agents create demand for software rather than removing it. The build
> instruction that follows: **agent-first, not AI-bolted-on** — "adding an engine to a
> horse doesn't make it a car."

## What set it off

Two posts, and 1.8 million views of collective gloom underneath them.

- **The founder of Bannerbear** (~$81K MRR; the auto-transcript garbles his name)
  posted that signups and conversions were weak, and that everything was downstream of
  traffic: *"our Google referrals have basically halved this year… the current strategy
  of I just write content seems to be on the way out."* The chart only goes down.
- **Pieter Levels (levels.io)** quote-tweeted it: *"I'm seeing a trend here of declining
  revenue and traffic with indie hackers, and on my own projects too… To me it seems
  clear AI is cannibalizing everything that used to be apps. Not bad, by the way —
  just times are changing and we have to adapt."*

Greg's note on Levels: the *adapt* clause is the correct part and nobody in the replies
read it. The replies are, in his word, **defeated**.

## The core argument: the advantage moved, it didn't disappear

The mechanism, spelled out:

> Indie hackers' unfair advantage — **it is in the name** — was code. They could write
> it, and **a lot of them won because SEO is itself technical**: build the tool, earn
> links, Google decides the site is high quality, traffic arrives. Two skills, one
> person, when almost nobody had both.

Now the code half is commodity. Pieter Levels' own summary, quoted approvingly in the
stream: *"Before, you needed to be technical to make something, and that plus okay
marketing costs a lot of money. Now you don't need to be technical at all to make
something. So if you're okay at marketing, you're now competing with people who are
great at marketing. TLDR: it's marketing week every week."*

**The new unfair advantages, as Greg lists them:**

1. **Distribution** — media, audience, an owned channel.
2. **Niche depth** — solving one painful problem deeply for one kind of user, rather
   than five problems at the surface for a hundred kinds. "Going deep is the real moat."
3. **Proprietary data** — data no LLM has. His own example is ideabrowser.com, where the
   startup ideas and trends are backed by data they hold, and people pay for that.
4. **Network effects** — anything with a social or community shape.
5. **Willingness to maintain** — *"if you're willing to maintain a piece of software that
   other people don't want to maintain, that's also an opportunity."* Same logic covers
   the categories nobody wants to run themselves: CRM, payment systems, Shopify-shaped
   infrastructure. Those aren't going anywhere.
6. **Paid acquisition skill** — if you can systematically find new ad formats and acquire
   users below their value, that alone is a business.

## Who actually loses

Greg concedes a specific case and refuses the general one. Pat Walls' claim — that 90 %
margins are over, that there will be more one-person businesses but less profitable
ones, with profit redirected from founders to Meta ads — gets this answer:

> If you are **a one-person software developer, making a tool, calling yourself an indie
> hacker, selling it at $20/month to other indie hackers**, with essentially no costs
> because you are the developer: yes, he's right. **Those people have to learn to become
> marketing pros or watch the decline.**

Everything wider than that, he disputes. And he takes a shot at the label itself: *"RIP
to the indie hacker"* — 37signals generates millions in profit and would never describe
themselves that way, because they have a point of view, a story and a mission that
customers identify with. *"Don't call yourself an indie hacker. Don't call yourself
anything. You're building a business."*

The counter-quote he pulls from the replies, which he thinks is the whole thing:
*"My software engineering skills were only a small part of being an indie hacker. The
most important part is still solving valuable problems and looking for customers…
Removing the coding part means removing a lot of friction."*

## Where the opportunity is

- **Agent-first products.** Defined by analogy, and this is the clearest thing in the
  stream: **Instagram was mobile-first Facebook.** Same core value proposition — photo
  sharing — but native to the surface. Facebook was native to desktop and felt like a
  website. So: *"look at categories and ask, what does the agent-first version of this
  look like?"* Concretely: stop assuming a human clicks through the flow. Assume the
  agent completes the task — **and that the agent might be the customer.**
- **Do not bolt AI onto an old product.** From a tweet he endorses: *"In 2025 everyone
  bolted AI into their product. In 2026 the smartest founders are rebuilding. Adding an
  engine to a horse doesn't make it a car."*
- **MCP-ify validated SaaS.** Plenty of already-validated apps become valuable as MCPs.
  His caveat against over-rotating: how many people have actually installed MCPs? Small,
  today. Opportunity as it grows, not revenue now.
- **Hardware + AI.** In a world where you can pull open-weight models off Hugging Face
  and put them into physical products — "a ton of opportunity there." He raises it
  unprompted and nobody in the discourse is talking about it.
- **Services, fulfilled by agents.** Asked whether to sell agents to companies or sell
  the service and fulfil it with agents: *"I would start with the service. That's what we
  do on some of our businesses — start with the service and let the agent fulfil."*
- **Agencies go premium.** The small generic ones go away; agencies move up-market. His
  own (LCA) designs AI-native products for large companies. Lots of demand, very
  premium, because *"a lot of companies are trying to figure out AI, don't know how, and
  need a dream team — design, strategy, engineering — to go figure it out for them."*

## The distribution half, compressed

- **ACP: Audience → Community → Product.** His stated framework for building anything in
  2026.
- **Two jobs, and they're different.** *"How do I earn attention, and how do I craft
  affinity?"* Attention = getting the scroll to stop so someone consumes the thing.
  Affinity = trust, so that later, when you do have something to sell, there is someone
  to sell it to.
- **The worked example:** Alex Hormozi opened the first hundred-plus videos with *"My
  name is Alex Hormozi and I've got nothing to sell you."* He does now. The affinity was
  built first, deliberately, over a long stretch of having nothing to sell.
- **Anti-positioning as a concrete copy exercise:** download the copy of your five
  biggest competitors, feed it in, and ask — *"this is how they speak. If I wanted to be
  the anti-version of them, how would you write copy?"* Robinhood versus TD Ameritrade
  or E\*Trade: same product category, a brand and story that felt completely different.
- **Public sentiment on AI is negative outside tech, and that's a copy instruction.** Ask
  an average person and AI means job loss and a data centre ruining a river. *"Maybe the
  copy shouldn't be AI-powered."* The mistake is putting AI-speak in the marketing. What
  people care about is the **outcome** — how you make their life easier. They can hate
  AI and still buy the thing, if how the sausage is made stays in the kitchen.

## If he were starting over: first $25K

Asked directly. **A service business.** Pick a niche you actually understand — his
throwaway example is centres for older adults — and sell them something you understand:
AI work, web design, mobile. **One-time charge plus a monthly fee.** Go **niche first**,
premium later (his agency advice inverted for a standing start: "I wouldn't go premium
to start, but I would go niche").

On whether to learn to code in 2026: helpful, not an unfair advantage. The gap from idea
to shipped, scalable and secure is smaller if you're technical. But the higher-value
target is **understanding the concepts** — what an API is, what an MCP is, what stacks
exist and how they fit, data structures, basic algorithms — rather than writing the code
yourself.

## The closing story

He went to a Phish show in Saratoga Springs around 2008. Everyone there told him he had
missed it — the golden era was over — and he was crushed, having travelled for it. It
was one of the best shows he's seen. Years later a serious fan told him that period
*was* the golden era.

> *"You don't know you're in the golden era when you're in the golden era. But one good
> sign you're in it is if everyone tells you you missed it."*

And the honest close, which is not the same as the headline: it **is** harder now for
indie hackers to build a moat, because their unfair advantage was code. *"But for the
people whose unfair advantage is being creative, marketing, distribution, connecting
with people, driving value, talking to customers — this is your time."*

## Relevance for a solo builder with no audience

**1. This episode is the same finding as a dead SEO play, arriving from the other side.**
Four months of building calculators that produced a handful of clicks a month, at an
average position in the seventies, is exactly the failure mode described here: **the
unfair advantage was assumed to be production, and it was actually authority.** Greg's
version — "code is not the unfair advantage anymore" — and the retrospective version —
"authority was the bottleneck, not inventory" — are one claim. Useful because it means
the lesson generalises past that one domain: **any strategy whose engine is "I can build
more of these than other people" is now betting on a commodity.**

**2. Take the list, but not the headline recommendation.** Greg's number-one answer is
build media and an audience. For anyone who has no network and no intention of becoming
a poster, that recommendation is not actionable — and pretending otherwise is how a good
episode turns into a shelved to-do. **The subset that does not require an audience:**
niche depth · proprietary data · maintenance nobody else wants · service-first with
agent fulfilment · anti-positioning copy · paid acquisition skill. That subset is worth
more than the parts that require standing in front of a camera.

**3. "Sell the service, let the agent fulfil" is the cheapest test available.** No
product, no store listing, no launch. It is also the one that produces a *paying human*
fastest, which is the only number that has been stubbornly missing.

**4. The copy instruction is immediately usable and free.** Strip "AI-powered" out of
outward-facing copy; sell the outcome. That applies to every landing page and every
proposal, costs nothing, and is testable.

**5. Agent-first ≠ AI feature.** The Instagram/Facebook analogy is a real design test to
run against anything already built: *if the agent is the user, does this flow still make
sense?* Most things built for a human clicking through fail it, and that failure is the
product opportunity rather than a defect.

**One claim not to repeat:** Greg relays a figure that a $200/month Claude plan costs
Anthropic roughly 40× that, and immediately says *"I don't know if that's true or not. I
didn't fact check it."* Treat it as an unverified quote from the stream, not a number.

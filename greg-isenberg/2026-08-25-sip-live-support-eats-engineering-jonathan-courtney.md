# Screensharing top takes in AI/startups (SIP Live)

> Live format: Greg and Jonathan Courtney walk the X timeline and give each post a *sip* or a
> *skip*, with audience questions at both ends. Loose by design, so the value is in three
> takes, not in the whole hour. **The one to keep is "customer support is eating engineering"**
> — build a daily loop that reads support transcripts and prototypes the fix, with a metric
> attached. The second is Jonathan's counter-move: sell **human** support as the premium
> feature. The third is the answer to the chat question about the worst moves for 2026, and it is the
> 100-day lesson said by someone else: build everything, sell nothing.

## 1. Where to start making a company AI-native

Audience question, and Greg's answer is one word:

> *"the number one thing you can do is to make your company legible."*

> *"That means that if you're using an LLM, you need to have the LLM be able to talk to your company."*

Meeting notes, SOPs, everything — in a place a model can read. Then step two, role by role: take
one role, list the jobs to be done (for a social media marketer: research, create, schedule, read
analytics, feed the analytics back into what to create next), and turn the repeatable ones into
loops. Some stay human.

**Jonathan's version is bottom-up and is the more transferable one**, because he was retrofitting
three existing businesses with staff who were not all enthusiastic:

> *"the first phase, honestly, was just giving everybody the pro versions of ChatGPT and Claude."*

> *"letting people mess around with it for a while without any rules."*

Then one department was asked to lead — admin: proposals, tax paperwork, repetitive work — chosen
because the freed time converts into revenue work.

> *"we didn't have an overall top-down mandate that everyone has to use the tools."*

## 2. Take: the $1M/month baby food app

Screenshot from Sensor Tower: *Solid Starts — Baby's First Foods*, doing a million dollars a month.
The tweet's observation is the interesting half:

> *"The more expensive the app, the more parents seem to trust it."*

Jonathan explains the mechanism from the buyer's side — first child, high stakes, one shot, and
price is the only legible quality signal:

> *"the price is helping me figure out, okay, does this company actually take this seriously or is this just an AI-generated thing?"*

Two reusable rules come out of it. Greg's transfer rule:

> *"If the product works well for babies, chances are it works well for pets."*

And the niche rule, which is the one that matters for a solo operator:

> *"if something is making a million dollars a month, there's always an opportunity to niche this down into, you know, a niche."*

Geography, allergy, diet. Nobody needs the million: a niched version at 10K–30K a month is a good
business, *"especially if you don't half-ass it"* — in a category where buyers genuinely check
quality, effort is the moat.

## 3. Take: an app store for iMessage agents

Someone built a directory of AI agents you can text on iOS (Poke, Caddy, Tomo). Greg's read on why
these feel different from a general chatbot:

> *"These assistants that you're seeing on this iMessage agent store are pretty verticalized."*

> *"They do a few things and they do a few things well."*

He points at **poke.com** specifically as an onboarding study — *"anyone who cares about UX"* should
see what agent-first onboarding looks like. He is an investor in Lindy.ai and quotes its founder's
positioning against the general tools: *"what we're trying to build is like the Apple version."*

Jonathan's framing is the useful one for anyone selling to non-builders: it is a polished front end
for the complicated things these tools can do, for people who would never open a terminal. *"most
people I know are still just using ChatGPT, still just using it as a search engine."*

## 4. Take: customer support is eating engineering — the keeper

Jonathan guesses the meaning before Greg explains it, and gets it right:

> *"the people who are directly connected with the customers are actually able to make a lot of the changes that engineers used to have to make."*

Greg's extension is the concrete build, and it is the whole reason to listen to this episode. Loop
talk so far has been about engineering — *slash goal*, build the startup, build the startup. He
thinks the sharper loop crosses the boundary:

> *"I mean that if you're getting 50 customer support pieces of data every single day, they're telling you what they like and what they don't like about your product."*

> *"If you can create a loop that says every day look at those transcripts and every day prototype a feature that solves their problem."*

> *"And give them a goal."*

> *"The goal could be, you know, DAU over MAU increases, retention increases, virality increases, revenue increases."*

That is the shape: **support transcripts in → daily prototype out → one objective metric as the
stop condition.** It is Elie Steinbock's loop engineering (13.07.) pointed at the one input most
solo founders already have and never read.

## 5. Counter-take: human support as the premium feature

Jonathan's addition, and he is more certain about this than about anything else in the hour:

> *"One of the things you can really do to stand out is by clarifying that your customer support is not AI and that you're going to be able to access a human 24 seven."*

His two examples: **Trade Republic** ran a billboard campaign across Berlin on exactly this claim —
he adds *"Not sure how true that is."* — and **Basecamp**, which names and shows the person running
support on the website.

> *"I want you to be using these AI tools to improve the product, but when I have a problem, I want to talk to a goddamn human and not to some bot person."*

He extends it into a positioning idea worth more than the take it is attached to:

> *"Someone needs to do an advertising campaign around how their product is getting reversed inshittificated."*

Greg lands on the split verdict: skip 100% AI support, *"gulping heavily this idea of human support"*.
Note that this and take 4 are not in conflict — the loop reads support to build the product, the
human answers the customer.

## 6. Take: RIP the two-pizza team

David (CTO of Cursor) on Bezos's two-pizza rule: right about small teams, but *"in the AI era, two
pizzas is too much pizza."* Both agree; the transferable part is Greg's replacement habit for
hiring:

> *"before hiring someone, I'm like, could I create a loop that can actually get this done?"*

> *"Or I ask myself, what are the jobs to be done for the person that I think is going to save me?"*

Then build agents against those tasks first. Jonathan's caveat: a builder still needs pairing with
someone who can pitch and sell — *"one American pizza is probably enough"*.

## 7. Take: Paul Graham on reading

PG to his 14-year-old: *"People are going to stop reading books."* … *"if you're one of the few
people who still read, you'll have a huge advantage over everyone else."* The rebuttal from Kieran
Ritchie is the sharper post: *"books are a monumental waste of time."* … *"600 pages for every one
page of actually valuable content."* PG's reply is the line to keep:

> *"most books are bad. Part of reading well is finding the good ones."*

Greg refuses the frame:

> *"I think it's a barbell approach."*

Both, not either. Jonathan adds the specific: *"make sure fiction is a big part of this and not just
you're reading the stoic books on repeat or something."* And a parenting trick that is a small
masterpiece of incentive design — his seven-year-old has no bedtime while she is reading:

> *"as long as you're reading, there is no, you have to go to sleep time."*

## 8. Take: how to screw yourself over in 2026

Chat question. Jonathan's answer:

> *"spend all of your time building apps and all of your time building software and all of your time building loads of complicated systems and none of your time actually selling things, getting users and building an audience."*

> *"I still think the core of running a business is building an audience and getting people interested in what you actually are creating."*

Greg's is a spectrum with two failure modes. Ignore AI: finished. Chase every new tool: also
finished — *"over stimulated"*. His allocation is roughly nine tenths of the work on the main
players and a deliberate look at something new once or twice a month, when it genuinely interests
you. His timing claim, for what it is worth: *"I believe that 2026 to 2029, there's going to be that
same wave"* as 2020–2022.

## 9. Closing question: I can build, I have never sold

> *"This is the age of the generalist, period."*

Greg's route: read the standard sales books for the frameworks, then make cold calls, get rejected,
do the reps. Jonathan, 16 years running a design agency, gives the harder version:

> *"A large proportion of your job, if not"* … *"50% is to be the promoter of your business, to be both the marketer and salesperson for your business."*

Your job is to promote and to find people to deliver — not to deliver forever. And the escape hatch
comes with a price tag:

> *"if you truly can't stick to that, you actually have to find a co-founder and they have to own 50% of your business."*

> *"Because if you find someone who can find you clients, they will either take your clients or own your business very soon."*

## Insights for me

- **Take 4 is the `/customers` gap again, and this is now the second independent source.** The
  17.08. episode said the missing piece is a `customers/` folder; here the same hole is described
  as the highest-value *loop*. We have the input and never read it: 16 written FinGrab store
  reviews, a PlaylistGrab cancellation after 6 min 43 s with reason `unused`, one InvoiceGrab buyer.
  It is not 50 items a day — it is maybe 20 items total, which makes it *easier*, not less
  valuable. The daily-loop version is overkill; a one-off read of everything a customer ever wrote,
  into one file per product, is a session's work and has never been done.
- **Human support is free positioning for a solo founder and it is literally true here.** "You will
  talk to the person who wrote it" costs nothing to claim on the InvoiceGrab and FinGrab store
  listings, and it is the one thing the big competitors cannot say. That is a listing-text change,
  i.e. a distribution move, not a build.
- **Take 8 is the 100-day lesson from the outside.** Worth noting because it is uncomfortable: our
  binding constraint has been named by two strangers in one week without knowing anything about us.
- **The niche rule and the trust-price rule point at InvoiceGrab.** Amazon invoices for German small
  businesses is already the niche cut of a bigger market; the untested half is whether the price is
  *too low* to read as serious. Not a decision I make — but it is a question worth putting to Jens
  with the store numbers next to it.
- **Skip:** the baby/pet app idea, the iMessage agent store, and the reading debate. Interesting,
  not on the critical path.

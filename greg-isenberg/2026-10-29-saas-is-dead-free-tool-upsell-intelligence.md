# Give the tool away, sell the intelligence on top

> Per-seat SaaS ("dumb SaaS") is dead because the tool is now cheap to build. The new model
> ("smart SaaS"): the tool is free, the user pays with data and network effects, and the money
> comes from an upsell — agents that do the work, or humans who provide a service.

**Source note:** an 11-minute solo whiteboard. No numbers about his own businesses, no case
with revenue. Everything here is Greg's thesis, not a measurement.

## Insights

1. **Why per-seat dies.** For 15 years the hard part was paying enough engineers to build a
   good tool, sold at $9.99 or $29.99 per seat. Now *"building the SaaS is not hard"*.
2. **The free tool is the acquisition channel.** *"give away the CRM, give away the dashboard,
   give away the app"* — find a niche where people still pay for an old-model tool and give it
   away. The user pays with **data** (makes the tool better, can be sold) and **network
   effects** (the moat).
3. **Two upsells.** Intelligence by agents (*"if you want the CRM to go and do something for
   me with AI agents, that's going to cost you money"*) or a service by humans. His consumer
   example: a free calorie tracker; paid = personalized meal plans, precise tracking, or a
   weekly weight coach.
4. **How to charge for the upsell:** per token, per outcome (when the agent actually did
   something), or a weekly/monthly fee for the human part.
5. **Bigger market, he argues:** *"the market for people accomplishing things is just a lot
   bigger than people actually using a tool"*.
6. **Enterprise is the exception.** Fortune 500/2000 still pay per seat (lower over time),
   for security and because *"it just feels good to pay because it feels like you're getting
   taken care of"*. SMB, prosumer and individuals trend to free.
7. **Format:** a *"product studio around free tools"* — many niches, same pattern.

## What it means for Jens

- **His extensions already run the first half.** FinGrab, InvoiceGrab and PlaylistGrab are
  free tools with a paywall on more of the same tool (longer ranges, more exports). Greg's
  model says the paid part should be a **different kind of value** — work done for the user —
  not a bigger quota of the free tool. Not tested by anyone with numbers in this episode.
- **Counter-evidence from his own data:** the binding constraint is reaching the register
  (18 checkout sessions across all products from 24.04. to 20.09.), and 4 of 6 cancelled
  subscriptions were cancelled within 7 minutes of purchase (Stripe, 29.10.). A free tool only works as
  a channel if people arrive. Greg's thesis does not address distribution at all.
- **The data half does not fit him.** Extensions that process in the browser collect no data
  by design, and the listings promise exactly that. Paying with data would break the privacy
  claim.

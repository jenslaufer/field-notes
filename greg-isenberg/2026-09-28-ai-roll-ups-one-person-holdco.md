# $5T opportunity: AI Roll Ups

> Greg alone, no guest. The thesis: owners of small service firms (accounting, insurance,
> property management) are retiring, AI agents can now do the back-office work those firms
> run on, and the firms are still priced as if their margins can never move. Thrive and
> General Catalyst already buy such firms and run agents inside them; they need big deals,
> so the small ones are left for solo founders. His one-person version: a holdco with a
> GM per business, one shared layer of agents and rules underneath, a hard rule that no
> agent ever sends anything to a client, and a **corrections log** that is turned into rules
> every week and becomes the real asset. The entry path is not buying — it is selling one
> annoying job as a service to firms in one industry until an owner wants out.

## 1. Why now — three things at once

> *"I think three things happen at the same time. And when that happens, you usually get a new kind of business."*

Owners retiring, sourced to McKinsey: *"they expect about a million of those businesses to
actually sell by 2035."* Second, AI can do the work — and he describes the work concretely:

> *"Someone types numbers from a PDF into a spreadsheet and someone chases a client from the same missing document for the third time."*

> *"Now, you know, if you set it up correctly, you get a draft that somebody checks."*

Third, the pricing:

> *"So a normal service firm runs at 5% to 10% profit, something like that."*

> *"you get a huge wave of businesses about to change owners full of the exact kind of work agents are good at and priced as if nothing about them can change."*

## 2. What the funds do — and his own caveat

Thrive Holdings: *"they've bought close to 50 of these accounting firms over the last 24
months and they just committed another billion dollars to buy more."* The one case with
detail, Larson Gross:

> *"This tax season, it processed 7,000 returns. An accountant saved 31% of their time on average."*

> *"One accountant had a job that used to take her 180 hours a year and she got it down to 15."*

> *"The people who used to do the work are becoming the people who check it."*

General Catalyst: *"They set aside $1.5 billion for this strategy."* Long Lake (property
management) claims 18 businesses and $100 million EBITDA in under two years; Crescendo (support
centres) claims *"AI now handles about 90% of frontline tickets."* The deal structure he likes:
*"they usually pay about 60 to 70% in cash. And the founder rolls about 30% equity into the new
company."*

The caveat, in his words — this is the frame for every number in this section:

> *"A lot of these numbers, when I was doing my research, are self-reported by young companies that are raising money and none of them have been through a recession yet."*

The playbook, condensed: build the platform first, buy a trusted firm, run agents in the
background where *"nobody outside sees anything change"*, move the back office over, plug in the
next firm. The margin claim is conditional and he says so: *"profit goes from anywhere around 5%
to 10% to, if the thesis holds, to 30% or 40%."*

The gap:

> *"If you're a billion-dollar fund, you can't spend your time on a bookkeeping firm doing $2 million a year."*

## 3. The one-person holdco — structure and files

Founder on top, a GM per business, *"usually that's someone who's already there"*, with *"a real
piece of the upside."* Underneath, one shared layer:

> *"Every business uses the same agents, the same rule system, the same back office, and the same dashboards."*

The solo founder's edge, as he argues it: same models as the funds, and *"You also get to be the
integration."* Plus the seller's side: *"some of them are just not going to want to sell to a
large venture capital firm, but they might want to sell to you."*

Folders: `thesis` (keeps you *"from buying something just because you got excited about it"*),
`shared` (agents, global rules, *"a folder of real accepted work that you use to test the agents
every time you change something"*, runbooks), and one folder per business with clients, people,
rules and a corrections log. His minimum:

> *"If you set up only three files, I'd say set up the global rules, each business rules, and the corrections log."*

## 4. The pipeline rule: agents may block, never send

Intake agent → preparer agent → reviewer agent → a person → client.

> *"I think the most important rule of this whole system is the reviewer can block, but it can never send anything."*

> *"A person always approves before anything reaches the client."*

Each agent file is *"basically like a plain English job description"* — job, what it may do, what
it may not, when to stop and ask. His diagnosis of agent failures:

> *"Most of the agent problems I've seen come from nobody telling the agent where its job ends and writing down what it can never do fixes a lot of that."*

## 5. The week, the corrections log, and the first deal

Monday: five metrics per business — margin, human minutes per job, how often agent drafts need
fixing, client retention, whether key people stay. Tuesday: one call per GM. Wednesday is the
corrections log, *"the most important hour of the week"*. The prompt he reads out:

> *"compare each agent draft with the version of person approved this week sort every change into factual error client preference missing information or style for any correction that happened more than once propose a rule add each approved rule as a test using the original input and the accepted output"*

> *"anyone can use the same AI models, but nobody else has your list of every way they go wrong in your kind of business."*

Thursday and Friday: finding the next business. On marketplaces: *"once they hit the
marketplaces, unless it's super curated, you're not going to get a good deal."* On the first
one — this is the most actionable passage of the episode:

> *"pick one industry, say bookkeeping firms, and I start by selling them a service. Take one annoying job and do it with agents, like cleaning up month end for their messiest clients or chasing missing documents."*

> *"After six months, nine months, 12 months of doing good work for a bunch of firms, one of those owners is going to be ready to step back and you'll be the obvious person to take it over."*

**Objections** he answers (lightning round): roll-ups fail — *"they overpay or they buy faster
than they can integrate or the culture falls apart"*, so buy slowly and *"price the business on
what it makes today"*; margins get competed away — *"eventually, probably yes"*, but the window
is *"actually years long"*; regulated work needs humans — *"checking a draft takes a lot less time
than making it from scratch"*; layoffs — *"I'd be lying if I said no jobs are going to change."*
The one he takes most seriously is staff and clients leaving: *"I take this the most seriously."*
His answer is that the first 30 days change nothing clients can see.

My judgement on the episode as a whole: the fund numbers are marketing by the funds' own
portfolio companies, relayed without checking; the X-impressions and "keep this quiet" framing is
Greg's standard hook. The structural content — shared layer, block-never-send, corrections log,
service-first entry — is sound and independent of those numbers.

## Insights for me

- **The corrections log is the one idea to take, and it is already half-built here.**
  `state/memory/` with its `learning_*` / `feedback_*` files *is* a corrections log turned into
  rules. What Greg adds is the step I do not have: **every approved rule becomes a test with the
  original input and the accepted output.** Most of my learnings stay prose; the tools that did
  become tests (klartext, check-links, zusage-check) are the ones that actually stopped the
  repeat. Weekly pass "which learning is still prose and has recurred?" is the direct transfer.
- **"Reviewer can block, never send" is the rule I already live under for mail** (drafts only,
  Jens sends). Worth stating it as the general rule for any agent product I sell — Fabrik
  included: agents open PRs, never merge. It is also the sentence a cautious German Kanzlei
  owner needs to hear before letting an agent near client data.
- **The entry path fits my situation better than the roll-up does.** Buying a firm needs capital
  concentration (against a Taleb small-bets posture with ~1M assets), a GM, and years — and
  delivers no cash in the next months. "Sell one annoying job as a service to one industry" is a
  cashflow product. For me the obvious industry is German Steuerberater / bookkeeping offices,
  and the obvious job is the one the 2027 XRechnung mandate forces on their clients: receiving,
  validating and booking e-invoices, plus chasing missing Belege. That is my e-invoice API with
  an agent in front of it.
- **Distribution is still the bottleneck, and the episode does not solve it.** Greg's deal flow is
  "coffee with owners" and outreach — a network plan. For me it has to be cold: a narrow offer
  ("we chase your clients' missing documents / convert incoming e-invoices") sent to a
  scraped list of small Kanzleien, measured by reply rate, with a kill threshold set before
  sending.
- **Unverified, my judgement, check before acting:** German tax-advisory firms have ownership
  restrictions (StBerG) that likely block a non-Steuerberater from simply buying one. That would
  make the German roll-up itself closed to me and leave only the service wedge — which is the
  part I would pursue anyway.

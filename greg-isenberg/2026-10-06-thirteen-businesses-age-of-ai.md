# Thirteen businesses for the age of agents

> A list episode, one speaker. Greg names thirteen business types he would want to **own**
> once agents do most knowledge work. The list is broad — sauna clubs sit next to compute and
> energy — and most items get one example and no numbers. Three items carry an argument
> rather than an example: **distribution gets more expensive every year**, **proprietary data
> is the thing a personal agent cannot fetch for you**, and **harnesses are the new GPT
> wrapper**. The rest is a map, not a playbook.

**Source note:** written from the publisher's VTT transcript of the full episode. Greg frames
the list with *"I think that there's only 13 businesses to be building in the world of
agents"* and distances himself from the "permanent underclass" framing. Company names come from
ASR and are given as heard (e.g. "Mercore, Micro One").

## The thirteen

1. **AI-native service firms.** Agents do most of the delivery, a human reviews exceptions,
   the client pays per completed job. Greg's line on the definition: *"I think you can still
   have the agent doing 90% of the work and still be an AI native firm."* Example: bookkeeping
   for one type of business. Argument: high margin, few clients needed.
2. **Offline businesses.** *"people actually want to pay to go and do something with other
   people"*. Example: Othership, a membership sauna club. The useful bit is the start: ask
   *"what is the food truck version of your business?"* — the first Othership was in the
   founder's backyard.
3. **Distribution.** Building is cheap, reaching people is not: *"my belief is that the price
   of reaching people is going to get higher and higher."* He recalls Facebook clicks at five
   cents around 2008 and "a dollar, two dollars" or more today (from memory, he says he has no
   data in front of him). His model is the **ACP funnel — audience, community, product**:
   advertisers pay you to build the audience, then you build what it asks for.
4. **Proprietary data sets.** Big version: pay people for data, sell to the labs. Solo
   version: *"how do you just get very useful information and that people cannot get by using
   their personal agents"*. He calls it the first inning of a data gold rush.
5. **Domain-specific harnesses.** Model = engine; harness = *"the tools it can access, the
   workflow rules, the memory, the checks"*. Claim: *"I believe that harnesses are the new GPT
   wrapper."* Most wrappers died, the surviving tenth added industry tools, rules and memory.
   Examples named: Harvey, Spellbook (legal); his own: an operating system for a freight
   brokerage agent.
6. **Robotics and physical AI.** Example: window cleaning as a service (heard as "Osmo").
   Entry for non-engineers: buy units and rent them out, or sell them.
7. **Physical products with a fan following.** Fellow coffee kettles; a phone for people who
   don't want to scroll. New angle: AI models make *"too niche"* hardware designable, a desktop
   printer makes it prototypable.
8. **Compute and energy** — *"These are the scarce inputs of our time."*
9. **Health, longevity and care** — older adults as an ICP *"a lot of people, especially
   younger people, are not creating for"*.
10. **Marketplaces and social networks** — a real moat if liquidity works, which he calls more
    art than science. Personal agents will want to access them.
11. **Real assets** — workshops rented to trades, warehouses, property.
12. **Vertical agents** — one agent owns one recurring job in one industry. Example:
    purchasing for independent restaurants (check stock, compare offers, ask approval, order).
13. **Security** — agent access control for sensitive workflows (example: accounting firms,
    read invoices and draft entries only), and physical security. Forecast, labelled as his
    guess: a big breach at a personal-agent startup.

## What it means for Jens

- **Item 1 and item 12 are what Jens already runs for himself.** The autonomous assistant books
  his accounts, checks bank balances against the ledger and sweeps three mailboxes. The
  bookkeeping example in item 1 is almost exactly the `buchhaltung` workflow — agent prepares,
  human reviews exceptions. The open question is not whether it can be built, but which single type
  of business would pay per closed month.
- **Item 13 is a niche Jens can show evidence for.** The setup already separates read paths from
  write paths (read-only PSD2 banking, gates before every send). "Agent access control for
  accounting firms" is a concrete, sellable form of what he built for his own safety.
- **Item 3 repeats the finding that already stands in these notes:** building is not the
  bottleneck, reaching people is. FinGrab has 557 users and 6 payers; the lever is the channel,
  not another feature.
- **Discount the rest.** No numbers per business, no failure rates, no costs. The list is a
  prompt for ideas, not evidence that any item works.

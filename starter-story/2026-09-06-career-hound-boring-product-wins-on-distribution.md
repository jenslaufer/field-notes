**The gap between $28,000 and $12K MRR is the number to carry away, and he explains it
himself:** *"the reason there's a gap between our MRR and our total revenue for the month is
because we have a mix of people who pay a yearly fee versus a weekly fee versus like a

He names the churn cause without being asked: *"The churn is high because we're selling a job

not a defect to fix — and it is precisely why the machine has to keep running.

## Where the product came from (and how little of it is product)

> *"I'm non-technical and when I started I thought that was a disadvantage, but in the last
> 2 years I realized it's actually the secret sauce."*

Background: he studied human psychology, then **sold alarm systems door-to-door for five
summers** and ended up managing sales teams. He ties that directly to the thing that scaled
the business: *"this gave me a huge advantage when it comes to managing content creators,
which is how I scaled Career Hound."*

The idea was borrowed, not found. A friend had built himself a scraper that pulled jobs
**posted directly on company websites** rather than from the aggregators. *"So we turned his
process of how he found his job into a product just to get our skin in the game. And initially,
our intention was to make just a thousand dollars each."*

Asked what makes it different, he refuses the product frame entirely:

> *"I think what makes me stand out has nothing to do with a product. It all has to do with my
> ability to spike people's emotions to make people think things in their heads through my
> content. This ability can be developed. You just need to do mass research on your niche and
> then produce content using this information, using the insights that come from this research."*

## 1. Timely vs timeless — the whole playbook in one distinction

> *"There's timeless content, there's timely content. A timely piece of content is something
> that's in trend right now, but a week from now nobody cares about it. And if you build your
> distribution system on timely content, it's a constant grind chase after trends."*

His worked example of each, described on screen:

- **Timely:** whatever format everyone is running this month. *"People are going to see enough
  of it, and they're going to start scrolling past it because they already know this is an ad."*
- **Timeless:** *"everyone is looking for jobs that are hiring remotely and worldwide. Like
  that's the dream. So, this format gives it to them. It calls them out with this hook. It
  can't be made any more simple, and then it goes through a list of companies that are offering
  this dream outcome that people want. And at the very end, he says, “Hey, to find more jobs
  like this, go on Roman's website.”"*

The format is: **hook naming the dream outcome → list of companies that deliver it → one line
of attribution at the end.** He has run it for two years.

> *"So the key to escape the hamster wheel in UGC and content marketing is to discover formats
> that are converting and repeatable. For example, in my niche, people will always want to find
> companies hiring remotely and worldwide. So, I can keep running this list over and over and
> over again."*

## 2. "Content thrifting" — how he finds a timeless format

This is the only genuinely non-obvious method in the episode, and it is a research procedure,
not a creative one:

> *"What you want to do is you want to spend like hours scrolling and watching content in your
> niche on different platforms like Instagram, Reddit, TikTok. It's got to be video and text.
> Like you you don't want to limit yourself to just one format. And I call it content thrifting.
> Basically, as you scroll and go through all of this content, you're just accumulating a bunch
> of stuff that grabs your attention. Because if it's grabbing your attention, there's something
> about it."*

And the justification, which is an argument about edge rather than taste:

> *"In order to have a breakthrough in content, you need to spot something that's not obvious to
> other people. And to spot that, you need to do more research than others. Because if you scroll
> enough, you'll inevitably become aware of things that others aren't aware of in your niche. And
> those consistent patterns reveal timeless content pieces, timeless topics within your niche."*

Note what the unit of research is: **not keywords and not competitors — individual pieces of
content that already stopped a human being mid-scroll.** The signal is his own attention, used
as a cheap proxy for the audience's.

## 3. Creators at $20 a video, and why they are old

The distribution engine is bought, not owned. He does not build a personal audience.

> *"The way I find my creators, the easiest way is to use a marketplace. There are a bunch of
> creator marketplaces, but most of my creators I find just by DMing people. I scroll, scroll,
> and scroll, and I find people who are already making content just for fun, and I'll often send
> a message saying, “Hey, do you want to do some paid content work?”"*

> *"In like 90% of situations, you don't want to pay more than $20 a video."*

**Why $20 works is a supply-side design decision, not a negotiation:**

> *"One of the reasons why I'm not spending a crazy amount of money on creators is because I make
> it so easy for them to make content for me. It's zero research. All they have to do is open up
> their laptop, and there's a sheet of formats perfectly instructed. And when I'm designing the
> instructions for their content, I'm asking myself, “Have I left any room for confusion, or is
> it stupid simple?” That's why I'm able to give content to people in their 50s who've never done
> content in their life."*

The format sheet is the asset. The creator is interchangeable — which is exactly why the price
stays at $20 and why a two-year-old format keeps paying.

**Older creators, deliberately, and only because of the niche:**

> *"I think every niche is different. Like for certain niches, young people make sense. But, if
> you're looking for job search advice, why would you be listening to somebody who's 19 years
> old? But, when you see somebody who's older, maybe has some gray hairs, some wrinkles, that is
> on a subconscious level associated with wisdom and credibility."*

The interviewer correctly generalises it away from age: the point is matching the messenger to
what the niche treats as authority, and he had measured what his niche treats as authority.

## 4. Sourcing an idea from who is already paying for distribution

> *"One creative way to source product ideas is to sign up on SideShift, which is a creator
> marketplace, and look at what companies are hiring creators because these companies have
> money, pay them, which means they're making money. Or you can also use Sensor Tower to find
> apps that are getting downloads. Then choose one that you like because it's important that you
> enjoy it as well, and create a similar product, and then use their content as inspiration for
> marketing your product."*

And the reason this particular filter is worth something:

> *"This way, if you struggle to get paying users, you know it's not the product, but your
> execution skills."*

**That is the load-bearing sentence of the episode.** Deliberately entering a proven category
removes demand as a variable, so that a failure can only be read as a distribution failure.
It buys a clean experiment, not an easier market.

Followed by the door-to-door frame on failure:

> *"You want to strive for failed results because this way you can eliminate what doesn't work
> and get closer to the thing that's going to work. When I did door-to-door, we used to tell
> ourselves, I get closer and closer to my sale with every door I knock. So, you get closer and
> closer to your first dollar with every marketing experiment you do."*

## 5. Stack

Named in full, and it is deliberately lean: **Short URL** (tracking social views) · **PostHog**
(marketing experiments) · **WhatsApp** (creator comms) · **Microsoft Clarity** (visitor sessions)
· **Hetzner** (server) · **Postmark** (email campaigns) · **Google Workspace** · **Cloudflare**.

No analytics-heavy setup, no growth platform. Two of the eight tools exist solely to measure
marketing (Short URL, PostHog) and a third to watch users (Clarity) — on a stack of eight, the
measurement share is the tell.

## What it says against our own situation

1. **The "proven category, blame the distribution" filter is a cleaner version of a decision we
   already make badly.** Our repeated pattern is to build artefact N+1 and then discover nobody
   finds it. His filter inverts the diagnosis in advance: enter only where demand is proven, so
   that silence can only mean the distribution is wrong.
2. **His "timeless format" and our measured store law are the same law.** We measured on
   PlaylistGrab (03.08.) and again on PDFuse (13.09.) that Chrome Web Store rank follows the
   listing text, not popularity — PDFuse ranks on `splitting merging pdf` and nowhere on
   `split pdf`. Roman's version: find the words the audience already uses, then repeat them
   forever. The store listing is a timeless format that we write once and it earns for years.
3. **The $20-a-video creator channel is a distribution test we have never run, and it is the
   cheapest one in this note.** We have no audience and no list. Buying 10 videos against a
   written format sheet is roughly $200 and produces a real read on whether anyone wants the
   thing — far below any ad budget.
4. **The format sheet, not the creator, is the asset.** If we ever test it, the deliverable to
   build first is the instruction sheet that leaves no room for confusion, and only then the
   outreach.
5. **Counterweight — do not read $28K as $28K.** MRR $12K, churn 31 %, LTV $50. His business
   requires 11.7 million views per month to stand still. That is the price of the model, and it
   is not passive income. Against Jens's stated goal (cashflow, as passive as possible), this
   engine is the opposite end of the axis: high throughput, high maintenance, no compounding
   asset except the format sheet.
